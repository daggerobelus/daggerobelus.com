HAND-SPECIFIC ALPHABET CHART
Manuscript: Henslow MS 688, Page 12
================================================================

This is a clear, practiced hand with strong italic/secretary hybrid characteristics.
Large decorative capitals begin each recipe section. The hand is relatively
consistent and well-spaced, making it one of the more legible pages.

================================================================
INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS (a–z)
================================================================

a:
  Letterform: Single-compartment 'a' formed with a curved stroke on the left
  and a minim on the right. Sometimes nearly round.
  Example words: "handfull" (line 3), "and" (line 4), "take" (line 7)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'o' when loosely formed
  Variants: Generally consistent

b:
  Letterform: Tall ascender with a rounded bowl at baseline. The ascender
  is a straight upstroke.
  Example words: "Brothe" (line 2), "before" (line 3), "Borrrage" (line 4)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'h' or 'l' if bowl is small
  Variants: Larger, more decorative form in capitals ("Brothe", "Borrrage")

c:
  Letterform: Small curved stroke open to the right, like a modern 'c'.
  Example words: "Caunteberries" (line 1), "Consumptione" (line 2), "Coleworte" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'e' in some instances
  Variants: Large decorative capital C in "Caunteberries" and "Consumptione"

d:
  Letterform: Round bowl with a looped ascender that curves back to the left
  (secretary-style 'd').
  Example words: "handfull" (line 3), "and" (line 4), "morter" section
  Confusion risks: The looped ascender can look like 'cl' to modern eyes
  Variants: Consistent secretary 'd' throughout

e:
  Letterform: A small rounded form, sometimes with a visible horizontal stroke.
  Often resembles a reversed loop typical of secretary hand.
  Example words: "the" (line 6), "leaues" (line 3), "one" (line 7)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'o' — this is the highest confusion risk
  Variants: Terminal 'e' is sometimes a simple curved stroke

f:
  Letterform: A tall letter with ascender and descender. Has a crossbar through
  the middle, distinguishing it from long-s.
  Example words: "for" (line 2, 6), "ffull" in "handfull" (line 3), "flixe" (line 12)
  Confusion risks: Confused with long-s (ſ), but f has a clearer crossbar
  Variants: Double-ff used as capital F in "ffor" in headings

g:
  Letterform: Round bowl at baseline with a descending loop below the line
  that curves left.
  Example words: "Langdebeefe" (line 4), "Borrrage" (line 4), "grinde" (line 9)
  Confusion risks: The descending loop could be confused with 'y' if poorly formed
  Variants: Consistent

h:
  Letterform: Tall ascender on the left with a curved stroke descending to the
  right from the top. The descender sometimes loops below the baseline.
  Example words: "the" (line 6), "them" (line 8), "handfull" (line 3)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'b' if the descending right stroke is short
  Variants: Generally consistent

i:
  Letterform: A single minim stroke, sometimes with a dot or tittle above.
  Example words: "in" (line 8), "itt" (line 9), "violett" (line 3)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with first stroke of 'm', 'n', 'u'
  Variants: Consistent

k:
  Letterform: Tall ascender with two diagonal strokes meeting at the midpoint.
  Example words: "Take" (line 3, 7, 12, last section)
  Confusion risks: Distinctive, unlikely to be confused
  Variants: The 'k' in "Take" is consistent and clear throughout

l:
  Letterform: Tall ascender, a single upward stroke, slightly curved.
  Example words: "leaues" (line 3, 4), "laye" (line 10), "like" (line 3)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'b' or 'h' — look for absence of bowl or curve
  Variants: Consistent

m:
  Letterform: Three minim strokes connected at the top.
  Example words: "My" (line 1), "morter" (line 9), "them" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: 'm' vs 'in' vs 'ni' — count the minims carefully
  Variants: Capital M in "My" is large and decorative

n:
  Letterform: Two minim strokes connected at the top.
  Example words: "one" (line 7), "and" (line 4), "in" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: 'n' vs 'u' — in this hand, n has a connecting stroke at top,
  u has connecting stroke at bottom
  Variants: Consistent

o:
  Letterform: A round, closed loop.
  Example words: "one" (line 7), "of" (line 7), "Coleworte" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: Confused with 'e' and 'a' when hastily written
  Variants: Generally round and clear

p:
  Letterform: Descender below the baseline with a round bowl at the top right.
  Example words: "pyles" (line 6), "putt" (line 3), "pyll" in context
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'q' but p has bowl on right
  Variants: Sometimes has special abbreviation form for "pre-", "per-"

q:
  Letterform: Round bowl with descender curving to the right.
  Example words: "quantitye" (line 5)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'p'
  Variants: Only appears rarely

r:
  Letterform: Two forms visible — (1) a short downstroke with a rightward
  shoulder at top, (2) a 2-shaped form after 'o'.
  Example words: "Brothe" (line 2), "wrappe" (line 7), "rested" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: The 2-shaped r can look like 'z'; the first form can be confused with 'v'
  Variants: Both standard and 2-shaped forms appear

s:
  Letterform: Two forms — (1) long-s (ſ) in initial and medial positions,
  tall like an 'f' but without a full crossbar; (2) round-s at end of words.
  Example words: long-s in "savinge" (line 3); round-s in "leaues" (line 3)
  Confusion risks: Long-s easily confused with 'f' — look for crossbar presence
  Variants: Long-s (ſ) and round-s clearly distinguished by position

t:
  Letterform: A short upstroke with a horizontal crossbar. Sits at x-height.
  Example words: "the" (line 6), "take" (line 7), "them" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'c' if crossbar is faint
  Variants: Consistent

u:
  Letterform: A curved stroke connecting two minims at the bottom.
  Example words: "putt" (line 3), "untill" (line 8), "upon" (context)
  Confusion risks: Confused with 'n' — u connects at bottom, n at top
  Variants: Used medially; 'v' used at word-initial position

v:
  Letterform: Two strokes meeting at a point at the bottom, like modern 'v'.
  Example words: "violett" (line 3), "vpon" (last section)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'u' — v is at word-initial position
  Variants: Used word-initially where modern English uses 'u'

w:
  Letterform: Broad letter with two 'v' shapes joined, or a wavy series of strokes.
  Example words: "wrappe" (line 7), "well" (line 8), "woole" (last section)
  Confusion risks: Distinctive, unlikely to be confused
  Variants: Consistent

x:
  Letterform: Two crossing diagonal strokes.
  Example words: "flixe" (line 12)
  Confusion risks: Distinctive
  Variants: Rare on this page

y:
  Letterform: Two strokes meeting above the baseline with a descender curving
  left below the line.
  Example words: "My" (line 1), "pyles" (line 6), "yee" (line 8)
  Confusion risks: Could be confused with thorn (þ) — thorn represents "th"
  Variants: Consistent; also appears in "ye" meaning "the" (thorn usage)

z:
  Letterform: Not clearly visible on this page.
  Example words: None identified
  Confusion risks: N/A
  Variants: N/A

================================================================
COMMON COMBINATIONS AND LIGATURES
================================================================

th: Formed as separate letters, sometimes with thorn (þ) appearing as 'y'.
    "the" appears frequently; sometimes written as "ye" (thorn form).

sh: Formed with long-s + h. Visible in "sheepe" (last section).

ff: Double-f used as capital F. Seen in heading "ffor" style.

-es: Terminal '-es' sometimes written with a looped graph. Visible in
     "leaues", "pyles".

-er: An '-er' graph (hook-shaped upstroke) may be present in some words.

-ll: Double-l is common: "handfull", "untill", "well".

-tt: Double-t is common: "putt", "itt".

================================================================
DISTINCTIVE FEATURES OF THIS HAND
================================================================

1. Large, elaborate decorative capitals at the start of each recipe section,
   especially "M" in "My", "T" in "Take", "F" in "For".
2. Consistent spacing between words — this is a neat, practiced hand.
3. Decorative dots/points used as line fillers (e.g., ".........." between
   recipe titles).
4. The hand is a hybrid of italic and secretary features — generally more
   legible than pure secretary hand.
5. Strong, dark ink throughout — no significant fading on this page.
6. Each recipe section is clearly demarcated with centered headings.
7. "Take" begins most recipes with a large decorative capital T.

================================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (highest to lowest)
================================================================

1. e / o — HIGHEST RISK
   Why: Both are small rounded forms at x-height. In this hand, 'e' sometimes
   has a slightly more angular or reversed form, but when hastily formed they
   look nearly identical. Distinguish by looking for the small horizontal
   bar in 'e' vs. the fully closed loop of 'o'.

2. long-s (ſ) / f
   Why: Both are tall letters with ascenders and descenders. The key difference
   is the crossbar — 'f' has a full crossbar through the stem, while long-s
   either has no crossbar or only a partial one on the left side. In this
   hand the distinction is relatively clear but still requires attention.

3. n / u
   Why: Both are formed from two minim strokes. In this hand, 'n' has the
   connecting stroke at the top (arch) while 'u' has the connecting curve
   at the bottom. When the pen strokes are ambiguous, count minims and look
   for the direction of the connecting stroke.

4. m / ni or in
   Why: Three minim strokes could be read as 'm' or as 'ni'/'in'. Count
   carefully and look for the connecting patterns at top vs. bottom.

5. c / t
   Why: Both are short letters. 'c' is an open curve; 't' has a crossbar.
   If the crossbar of 't' is faint, it can look like 'c'. In this hand,
   the distinction is usually clear.

6. h / b
   Why: Both have tall ascenders. The key difference is the right-side
   stroke: 'h' curves down and sometimes below the baseline, while 'b'
   has a rounder bowl that stays at or above the baseline.

7. y / thorn (þ)
   Why: Both have descenders. Thorn represents "th" and looks like 'y'
   to modern eyes. In this hand, thorn usage appears in common words
   like "the" written as "ye". Context helps: if "ye" appears where
   "the" makes sense, it's likely thorn.
