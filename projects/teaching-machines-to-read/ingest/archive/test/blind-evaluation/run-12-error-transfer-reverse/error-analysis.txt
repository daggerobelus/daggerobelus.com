A1. "1 pound of white Lead" misread universally
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
REFERENCE: "Take one pound of oyle olive of the best 1 pound of white
           Lead very well calcinated into dust, 12 ounces of"

Agent 1: "Take one pd of oyle oliue of the best & of lead very well centred wth dust 12 ounces of"
Agent 2: "Take one pt of oyle oliue of the best & a color lead very well [seirced?] wth dust 12 ounces of"
Agent 3: "Take one pd of oyle oliue of the best & a quarter of lead very well centured wth dust & 12 ounces of"
Agent 4: "Take one pd of oyle oliue of the best & a lead very well [sifted?] wth dust 12 ounces of"
Agent 5: "Take one pd of oyle oliue of the best & of red lead very well searced with dust 12 ounces of"

ERRORS (multiple compounding):
  (a) "pound" → "pd" / "pt" (5/5 agents). The abbreviation was not
      expanded. The reference reads the full word "pound."
  (b) "olive" → "oliue" (5/5 agents). All agents normalized the long-s
      or u/v convention differently from the reference, which modernizes
      to "olive."
  (c) "1 pound of white" → "& of" / "& a color" / "& a quarter of" /
      "& a" / "& of red" (5/5 agents). The entire phrase "1 pound of
      white" is lost by every agent. The numeral "1" is apparently read
      as an ampersand "&", and "pound of white" is either dropped or
      wildly misread. This is the single most catastrophic systematic
      error on the page.
  (d) "calcinated into" → "centred wth" / "[seirced?] wth" / "centured
      wth" / "[sifted?] wth" / "searced with" (5/5 agents). No agent
      recovered "calcinated into." The word "calcinated" (a period
      chemistry term meaning heated to powder) is unknown to all agents.
      ROOT CAUSE: Vocabulary gap (period-specific chemical terminology)
      combined with an unfamiliar letterform sequence.

ROOT CAUSE: This is a compound failure. The numeral "1" is misread as
"&" (letterform confusion), the word "white" is dropped or garbled
(likely because agents cannot reconcile it once the preceding text is
wrong), and "calcinated" is a specialized term outside agent vocabulary.
A human paleographer with knowledge of early modern chemistry would
recognize "calcinated" as standard alchemical/pharmaceutical language.

A4. "then make your fire" → "yt/yr make yt/yr fire"
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
REFERENCE: "then make your fire some what bigger"

Agent 1: "yt make yt fire som=what bigger"
Agent 2: "yr make yr fire som=what [nigher?]"
Agent 3: "yt make yt fire some what eager,"
Agent 4: "yt make yr fire som what bigger,"
Agent 5: "yt make it fire some what eager,"

ERRORS:
  (a) "then" → "yt" (5/5). All agents read "then" as an abbreviated
      form. The reference has "then" as a full word.
  (b) "your" → "yt" / "yr" (5/5). Same issue — reading abbreviation
      marks where the reference has the full word.
  (c) "bigger" → "bigger" (2), "nigher" (1), "eager" (2). Only 2 of 5
      get the correct word.

ROOT CAUSE: The agents are over-reading abbreviation marks. In early
modern manuscripts, thorn (y-with-superscript-t) represents "the" or
"that," and y-with-superscript-r represents "your." The agents are
seeing abbreviation marks where the reference reads full words. This
suggests the agents default to reading ambiguous marks as abbreviations
rather than attempting the full word.

SECTION E: PATTERN ANALYSIS
=========================================================================

1. DOMINANT ERROR CATEGORIES (ranked by frequency):

   (a) VOCABULARY GAPS (most damaging): The most catastrophic errors
       involve period-specific terminology that agents do not know:
       - "calcinated" (chemical process)
       - "Searclothes" (cerecloth medical bandages)
       - "Alcemus" (alkermes cordial)
       - "fundaments" (posterior/anus)
       - "vnnilla" (uncertain term)
       - "Runinge" (discharge)
       When agents encounter unknown vocabulary, they substitute
       familiar words rather than attempting letter-by-letter
       decipherment. This produces confident-sounding but wrong
       readings.

   (b) NUMERAL CONFUSION: The numeral "2" is systematically read as
       "a" across multiple recipes and multiple agents. The numeral "1"
       is read as "&" in one critical passage. Agents appear to have
       difficulty distinguishing manuscript numerals from letters.

   (c) SEMANTIC HALLUCINATION: When letterforms are ambiguous, agents
       generate readings that are semantically plausible in context but
       textually unsupported. Key examples:
       - "glory" for "older the better" (5/5 agents)
       - "Mr Nowill/Nowell" for "A woman" (5/5 agents)
       - "Gall" for "head" (4/5 agents)
       - "forehead" for "fundaments" (3/5 agents)
       - "memorie" for "vnnilla" (3/5 agents)
       These consensus hallucinations are particularly dangerous because
       agreement among agents might be mistaken for accuracy.

   (d) ABBREVIATION OVER-READING: Agents default to reading ambiguous
       marks as abbreviation marks (yt for "then," yr for "your," yt
       for "them"), even when the reference has full words.

   (e) EYE-SKIP / LINE CONFUSION: In dense passages with repeated
       anatomical terms, agents jump between lines (e.g., reading
       "reines of the back" from the wrong clause). This mimics a
       classic scribal error pattern.

   (f) MINOR SPELLING NORMALIZATION: Consistent but low-severity
       modernization (pott→pot, trenchar→trencher, oliue→olive). These
       suggest the agents slightly favor modern spellings.

2. ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS:

   The fundamental problem is that agents READ FOR MEANING rather than
   READ FOR FORM. A human paleographer deciphers letter by letter,
   building words from individual letterforms, and then checks the
   result against context. These agents appear to do the reverse:
   they grasp an approximate visual impression and then generate the
   most contextually plausible word that roughly fits.

   This explains why:
   - Familiar words are substituted for unfamiliar ones (nutmegs for
     alcemus, forehead for fundaments)
   - Multiple agents converge on the SAME wrong reading (consensus
     hallucination)
   - Agents are better at common recipe terms (Spanish sope, Mortar,
     Lemon) than specialized medical/chemical terms
   - Ambiguous passages produce creative but unsupported readings
     rather than honest "[...]" markers

3. WHAT A HUMAN PALEOGRAPHER WOULD DO DIFFERENTLY:

   (a) LETTER-BY-LETTER DECIPHERMENT: A paleographer would build an
       alphabet from confirmed letterforms elsewhere in the manuscript,
       then apply it to difficult passages. When "fundaments" looks like
       "forehead," a paleographer would check: does the 'u' match other
       u's? Does the 'n' match other n's? The agents skip this step.

   (b) VOCABULARY KNOWLEDGE: A paleographer specializing in early
       modern recipe books would know "calcinated," "Searclothes,"
       "Alcemus," "fundaments," and "Runinge of the reines" as standard
       terminology. This domain knowledge prevents the substitution of
       familiar words for unfamiliar ones.

   (c) CONTEXTUAL REASONING: "A woman in Childbed" is the only
       sensible reading because men do not lie in childbed. A
       paleographer would use this logic to override a visual impression
       of "Mr Nowill."

   (d) HONEST UNCERTAINTY: A paleographer marks genuinely illegible
       passages as illegible rather than generating a plausible guess.
       The agents too often produce confident wrong readings instead of
       acknowledging uncertainty.

   (e) CROSS-REFERENCING: A paleographer would compare letterforms
       across the manuscript. If "r" in a confirmed word looks a certain
       way, that same form should be "r" everywhere. The agents treat
       each word in isolation.

4. RECOMMENDATIONS FOR AN ERROR PROTOCOL:

   Based on this analysis, a future error protocol should address:

   (a) Provide a GLOSSARY of period medical/chemical terms likely to
       appear: calcinated, Searclothes/cerecloth, Alcemus/alkermes,
       fundaments, Runinge (as discharge), vnnilla, Collicke, reines.

   (b) Provide NUMERAL EXAMPLES from the manuscript. The consistent
       misreading of "2" as "a" and "1" as "&" suggests agents need
       explicit guidance on how this scribe writes numerals.

   (c) Warn against SEMANTIC SUBSTITUTION: instruct agents to
       transcribe what they see, not what makes sense. If a reading
       seems medically implausible, flag it rather than substitute.

   (d) Require LETTER-BY-LETTER verification for uncertain passages
       rather than gestalt reading.

   (e) Flag the CONSENSUS HALLUCINATION problem: agreement among
       transcribers does not guarantee accuracy when all transcribers
       share the same biases.
