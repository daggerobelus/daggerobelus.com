TRANSCRIPTION PROTOCOL FOR EARLY MODERN MANUSCRIPTS
Derived from systematic error analysis of five independent transcriptions
=====================================================================

This protocol addresses documented, recurring error patterns observed
when agents transcribe early modern English manuscripts (c. 1550-1700),
particularly recipe books, medical texts, and household manuals. Every
rule below is motivated by a specific, observed failure.

You will be transcribing a manuscript from this period. The scribe's
hand will be unfamiliar. Read this protocol in full before you begin.


=====================================================================
PART 1: CRITICAL MINDSET RULES
=====================================================================

These are the most important behavioral rules. Violating any one of
them will produce systematic errors.

RULE 1: TRANSCRIBE WHAT YOU SEE, NOT WHAT MAKES SENSE.

  Your job is to decipher letterforms, not to write plausible English.
  When five agents transcribed a passage reading "the older the better,"
  all five wrote "glory" instead — because the letterforms loosely
  resembled "glory" and they filled in from context rather than reading
  letter by letter. Similarly, all five agents read "A woman in
  Childbed" as "Mr Nowill in Childbed" because the letterforms of
  "A woman" visually suggested a proper name. Only contextual reasoning
  (men do not lie in childbed) reveals the correct reading.

  If your reading produces a word that seems wrong in context, DO NOT
  silently replace it with something that sounds right. Flag it with
  [?] and move on. A flagged uncertainty is infinitely more useful than
  a confident wrong answer.

RULE 2: THE MANUSCRIPT IS THE AUTHORITY, NOT YOUR EXPECTATIONS.

  Do not normalize. Do not modernize. The manuscript may spell words
  in ways that look wrong to you. Preserve exactly what is written:
    - "pott" not "pot"
    - "trenchar" not "trencher"
    - "oliue" not "olive"
    - "stomacke" not "stomach"
    - "espetially" not "especially"
    - "enoughe" not "enough"

  In the observed error analysis, every single agent normalized "pott"
  to "pot" and "trenchar" to "trencher." These are minor errors
  individually, but they reveal a dangerous habit: you are silently
  editing the text to match your expectations. That same habit, applied
  to harder words, produces catastrophic errors.

RULE 3: WHEN IN DOUBT, GO LETTER BY LETTER.

  Do not read words as whole shapes. When a word is unclear, isolate
  each letterform and compare it to confirmed examples of that letter
  elsewhere in the manuscript. Five agents read "fundaments" (a
  standard medical term for the posterior) as "forehead" because they
  read the word as a visual gestalt rather than checking each letter.
  The f-u-n-d sequence does not match f-o-r-e-h — but you will only
  catch this if you check letter by letter.

RULE 4: MARK WHAT YOU CANNOT READ. DO NOT GUESS.

  Use [...] for illegible passages. Use [word?] for uncertain readings.
  An honest gap is better than a fabrication. Agents consistently
  generated confident-sounding but completely wrong readings rather
  than admitting uncertainty. "Searclothes" (waxed medical bandages)
  was read as "harder you," "character of," "charcoale," and "thicker
  you" — none of which were flagged as uncertain.

RULE 5: AGREEMENT DOES NOT EQUAL ACCURACY.

  Multiple agents can converge on the same wrong reading. This happened
  repeatedly in the observed analysis:
    - 5/5 agents read "glory" for "older the better"
    - 5/5 agents read "Mr Nowill/Nowell" for "A woman"
    - 4/5 agents read "Gall" for "head"
    - 3/5 agents read "forehead" for "fundaments"
    - 3/5 agents read "memorie" for "vnnilla"
    - 3/5 agents read "Nutmegs" for "Alcemus"
  If you find yourself producing a reading that "feels obvious," be
  extra cautious. The obvious reading may be a shared bias.


=====================================================================
PART 2: SPECIFIC LETTERFORM WARNINGS
=====================================================================

The following letterform confusions were documented across multiple
agents. These are not Sedley-specific — they reflect general
difficulties with early modern English secretary hand and italic hand.

2A. NUMERAL vs. LETTER CONFUSION

  This is the single most consistent mechanical error observed.

  "2" misread as "a":
    - Reference: "2 Spoonefulls" → All 5 agents: "a Spoonefull"
    - Reference: "2 pennyworth" → All 5 agents: "a pennyworth"
    The early modern "2" can resemble a scribal "a." ALWAYS consider
    whether a lone "a" before a quantity might actually be a numeral.

  "1" misread as "&":
    - Reference: "1 pound of white Lead" → All 5 agents read "&" for "1"
    and then lost the entire phrase "pound of white."
    When you see "&" followed by a measurement word (pound, ounce,
    pennyworth, handfull), re-examine: it may be a numeral.

  Numeral-to-numeral errors:
    - "3 or 4 dayes" → "2 or 3 dayes" (2 agents shifted both down by 1)
    - "20 yeares" → "10 yeares" (1 agent)
    Double-check all numerals. Compare the forms of confirmed numerals
    elsewhere in the manuscript.

2B. ABBREVIATION OVER-READING

  Agents systematically read full words as abbreviations:
    - "then" → "yt" (5/5 agents). Thorn abbreviation (y-superscript-t)
      was read where the reference has the full word "then."
    - "your" → "yr" (5/5 agents).
    - "them" → "yt" or "yr" (4/5 agents).

  Do not assume every "y" begins an abbreviation. Check: is there
  actually a superscript mark? If the word could be read as a full
  word ("then," "them," "your"), prefer the full word unless the
  abbreviation mark is unambiguous.

2C. r/v CONFUSION

  "reines" → "veines" (3/5 agents). In secretary hand, the minuscule
  r and v can be nearly identical. "Reines" (kidneys/lower back area)
  is a common medical term in this period. If you read "veines" in a
  medical context, consider whether the first letter might be an r,
  giving "reines."

2D. w/sm CONFUSION

  "wash" → "smale" or "smalt" (3/5 agents). The letterforms for "w"
  can resemble "sm" in some hands. If you read "smale" or "smalt" and
  it does not make sense in context, consider whether the word is
  "wash" or another w-initial word.

2E. c/t CONFUSION

  "especially" → "espetially" (2/5 agents). The secretary-hand c and
  t can be similar. When you see a t that produces an unfamiliar
  spelling, consider whether it might be a c, and vice versa.

2F. -in/-on CONFUSION

  "therein" → "thereon" (4/5 agents). The final minim strokes of -in
  and -on are easily confused. Pay attention to context: "therein
  dipped" (dipped in it) vs. "thereon dipped" (dipped on it).

2G. SUPERSCRIPT INSERTIONS

  "h^eales" (where e is inserted above the line) was missed by all 5
  agents, who produced garbled readings like "sores," "styles," and
  "byles." Watch for letters written above the line — they are
  interlinear corrections or insertions, not decoration. Read them as
  part of the word.

2H. CAPITAL LETTER CONFUSIONS

  Capital R misread as "Op":
    - "Runinge" → "openings/Openinge" (5/5 agents)
    The ornate capital R in some hands can resemble the letter
    combination "Op." If a word begins with "Op" and does not make
    clear sense, consider whether it might begin with R.

  Capital I misread as b or long-s:
    - "Iron" → "boxe/browne" (5/5 agents)
    Capital I in secretary hand can be elaborate. In recipe contexts,
    "Iron" is a common material (stirring rods, pots, mortars). If you
    read an unfamiliar word where a metal or material is expected,
    consider whether the initial letter is a capital I.

PART 3: PERIOD VOCABULARY
=====================================================================

Agents consistently failed on specialized vocabulary, substituting
familiar words for unfamiliar ones. The following terms are standard
in early modern recipe books, medical texts, and household manuals.
Encountering them should not surprise you.

3A. MEDICAL / ANATOMICAL TERMS

  belly — the abdomen (agents substituted "reines of the back,"
    "nauel," "sides" — all nearby body parts from the same passage)
  bloudy flux — dysentery (agents handled this correctly)
  Collicke / colic — abdominal pain, intestinal disorder
  fundaments — the anus / posterior region. Standard medical term.
    (All 5 agents misread this as "forehead" or "junctures.")
  Impostumes — abscesses (agents handled this correctly)
  pushes — pustules, skin eruptions
  reines — the kidneys or lower back region. NOT "veines."
    (3/5 agents misread as "veines" due to r/v confusion.)
  Runinge of the reines — a urinary discharge. "Runinge" is NOT
    "openings." (5/5 agents misread "Runinge" as "openings.")
  Searclothes / cerecloth — waxed cloth bandages used for medical
    dressings. (No agent recovered this word.)
  Tetter — a skin disease (eczema, ringworm). Agents handled this.
  ffellons — whitlows, infected sores on fingers (agents handled this)
  vnnilla — possibly vanilla or a variant medical term

3B. CHEMICAL / ALCHEMICAL TERMS

  calcinated — heated to powder, reduced by fire. A standard
    alchemical/pharmaceutical process. (No agent recovered this word;
    agents wrote "centred," "seirced," "centured," "sifted," "searced.")
  Alcemus / alkermes — a medicinal cordial made from kermes insects,
    red in color. (No agent recovered this; 3/5 wrote "Nutmegs.")

3C. RECIPE / HOUSEHOLD TERMS

  Clovegillyflowers — clove-scented pinks or carnations, used in
    cordials and syrups. This is ONE compound word, not two.
    (1 agent split it into "Cleare Gilliflowers.")
  Cowslip water — a distilled floral water used in cordials
  dramme / dram — a unit of weight (1/8 ounce)
  Manus Christi — an actual early modern confection (sugar preparation).
    If you see this, it may be correct — but verify letterforms.
  pennyworth — a quantity purchasable for a penny
  Sallandine / celandine — a medicinal plant (agents handled this)
  Syrrup — syrup (note double r, which is period-standard)
  Spanish sope — Castile soap, made from olive oil
  trenchar — a wooden plate or cutting board. NOT "trencher."

3D. CONTRACTION FORMS

  t'is — "it is." This contraction can split across line breaks and
    was universally lost by agents. (5/5 agents failed to recover it.)
    If you see what looks like "tis" or "t'is" at a line boundary,
    read it as "it is."

PART 4: SCRIBE HABIT DETECTION
=====================================================================

Before you begin transcribing, spend time studying the manuscript to
identify the scribe's habits. Do this BEFORE attempting a full
transcription.

4A. BUILD A LETTERFORM ALPHABET

  Find words you can identify with confidence (recipe titles, common
  words like "Take," "the," "and," "of"). Use these to establish what
  each letter looks like in THIS scribe's hand. Pay special attention
  to:
    - How the scribe writes numerals (1, 2, 3, 4, 10, 12, 20)
    - How the scribe writes r vs. v
    - How the scribe writes w vs. sm
    - How the scribe writes n vs. u (minim confusion)
    - How the scribe writes c vs. t
    - Capital letters: R, I, A, S, C, T, F (these cause the most
      confusion)

4B. IDENTIFY THE SCRIBE'S ABBREVIATION HABITS

  Does this scribe use abbreviations frequently or rarely? Check:
    - Does "the" appear as a full word or as thorn (y-superscript-e)?
    - Does "your" appear as a full word or as y-superscript-r?
    - Does "that" appear as a full word or as y-superscript-t?
    - Are there superscript letters (like the observed h^eales)?
    - How does the scribe mark abbreviations — with a tilde, a
      superscript letter, or a horizontal stroke?

  Once you know the scribe's abbreviation frequency, you can avoid
  over-reading or under-reading abbreviation marks.

4C. IDENTIFY THE SCRIBE'S SPELLING CONVENTIONS

  Does the scribe use u/v interchangeably? (Common: "vpon" for "upon,"
  "oliue" for "olive," "vse" for "use.") If so, preserve the scribe's
  convention — do not modernize.

  Does the scribe use double letters? ("pott," "ffellons," "Syrrup")
  If so, preserve them.

  Does the scribe use the double-f capital convention? ("ffellons,"
  "ffor" = capital F). This is standard in many early modern hands.

4D. IDENTIFY LINE-BREAK CONVENTIONS

  Does the scribe hyphenate across line breaks? Use "=" or "-"? Or
  does the word simply break with no mark? Note how the scribe handles
  "incor=porated," "souer=aigne," and similar long words. Reproduce
  the scribe's convention.


=====================================================================
PART 5: POST-TRANSCRIPTION VERIFICATION CHECKLIST
=====================================================================

After completing your first-draft transcription, go through EACH of
these checks before submitting.

CHECK 1: NUMERALS

  Re-examine every instance of "a" before a quantity word (ounce,
  pound, pennyworth, spoonfull, handfull, dramme). Is it truly "a"
  or might it be the numeral "2" or "1"?

  Re-examine every "&" (ampersand). Could it be the numeral "1"?

  Compare all numerals to confirmed numeral forms elsewhere in the
  manuscript.

CHECK 2: ABBREVIATIONS

  For every "yt," "yr," "ye" in your transcription, go back and verify:
  is there truly a superscript abbreviation mark, or could the word be
  "then," "them," "your," "the" written in full?

CHECK 3: SPECIALIZED VOCABULARY

  Scan your transcription for any words you flagged as uncertain.
  For each one, consult the vocabulary list in Part 3 above. Could the
  uncertain word be a period-specific term you did not recognize?

  Specifically check: did you write "openings" anywhere? (Might be
  "Runinge.") Did you write "veines"? (Might be "reines.") Did you
  write "forehead" in a context about applying plaster? (Might be
  "fundaments.") Did you write any modern ingredient name where a
  rarer period term might belong?

CHECK 4: SPELLING NORMALIZATION

  Scan for common modernizations you may have introduced:
    - -er endings that might be -ar (trenchar, not trencher)
    - Single consonants that might be doubled (pott, not pot)
    - "v" where the scribe wrote "u" or vice versa
    - Modern "olive" for period "oliue"

CHECK 5: EYE-SKIP DETECTION

  In passages with repeated similar terms (body parts, ingredients,
  measurements), verify that you have not skipped a line or merged
  two clauses. Read the passage against the manuscript line by line.

  Key warning sign: if your transcription mentions the same body part
  or ingredient in a different order than the manuscript, you may have
  jumped between lines.

  In the observed analysis, agents reading a passage about belly /
  reines / back / stomach skipped between clauses, assigning body parts
  to the wrong remedies.

CHECK 6: LINE BREAKS AND CONTRACTIONS

  Check every line break. Did a word split across lines? Is there a
  contraction (like "t'is") that spans a line boundary?

CHECK 7: SUPERSCRIPT AND INTERLINEAR ADDITIONS

  Scan the manuscript for any text written above the main line. These
  are insertions. Make sure they are included in your transcription.


=====================================================================
PART 6: DECISION FRAMEWORK FOR AMBIGUOUS LETTERFORMS
=====================================================================

When you encounter a word you cannot read with confidence, follow this
hierarchy in order:

STEP 1: LETTERFORM COMPARISON

  Isolate each letter of the ambiguous word. Find that same letterform
  in a word you have already read with confidence. Does the letter
  match? If you can build the word letter by letter from confirmed
  forms, that reading is likely correct even if the resulting word is
  unfamiliar.

STEP 2: PERIOD VOCABULARY CHECK

  Does the letter-by-letter reading produce a word from the vocabulary
  list in Part 3? If so, that reading is strongly favored, even if you
  have never seen the word before. "Calcinated" looks unusual, but it
  is a real and common early modern term. "Searclothes" looks unusual,
  but it is a real medical term. Do not reject a reading just because
  the word is unfamiliar to you.

STEP 3: CONTEXTUAL PLAUSIBILITY

  Does the reading make sense in context? This is a TIE-BREAKER, not
  a primary criterion. Context can confirm a reading, but it must never
  override letterform evidence. The danger of relying on context first
  is that you will generate a reading that "sounds right" but is not
  what the manuscript says.

  Example of context used correctly: "A woman in Childbed" is confirmed
  by the fact that childbed is a female experience. The letterforms
  looked like "Mr Nowill," but context overrules because "Mr Nowill in
  Childbed" is nonsensical.

  Example of context used incorrectly: Reading "Nutmegs" for "Alcemus"
  because nutmeg is a familiar recipe ingredient. The context (a
  cordial recipe) accommodates both readings, so context cannot
  distinguish them — you must rely on letterforms.

STEP 4: FLAG AND MOVE ON

  If Steps 1-3 do not resolve the ambiguity, mark the word as
  uncertain: [word?] for your best guess, [...] for truly illegible.
  Do not fabricate a confident reading. An honest uncertainty marker
  preserves information; a wrong confident reading destroys it.

HIERARCHY SUMMARY:
  Letterform evidence > Period vocabulary match > Contextual plausibility > Flag as uncertain

  NEVER let contextual plausibility override clear letterform evidence.
  NEVER substitute a familiar word for an unfamiliar one without
  letterform justification.
