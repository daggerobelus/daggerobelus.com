SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO HENSLOW MS688 SECRETARY HAND
==================================================
Generated from studying pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20

GENERAL OBSERVATIONS
---------------------
This is an English secretary hand from the late 16th/early 17th century,
recording medical and household recipes. The hand is relatively consistent
and practiced, with clear spacing between words. Spelling is early modern
English with systematic doubling patterns.

ALPHABET — DISTINCTIVE LETTERFORMS
------------------------------------

a — Two forms: (1) a simple single-stroke "a" resembling modern lowercase;
    (2) a secretary "a" with a looping top, sometimes resembling an "o" with
    a tail. Context usually disambiguates.

b — Tall ascender with a rounded bowl at the baseline. The ascender often
    curves rightward at the top. Distinct from "l" by the bowl.

c — Small rounded letter open to the right. Can look like a small "e" but
    lacks the crossbar. Sometimes hard to distinguish from "t" in combinations.

d — Secretary "d" with the ascender looping backward (to the left) in a
    distinctive reverse curve. This is one of the most recognizable secretary
    letterforms. The loop often extends well above the line.

e — Small letter, sometimes resembling a reversed "3" or a simple curve with
    a horizontal midstroke. In final position, "e" is often a simple upward
    flick or is very reduced. The scribe consistently writes terminal "-e" on
    words like "the," "bee," "putte."

f — Long descending letter that goes below the baseline, with a crossbar.
    Double "ff" is used as a capital F (as in "ffasten"). The descender and
    crossbar distinguish it from long "s."

g — Has a distinctive descending loop below the baseline with a closed or
    partially closed bowl above. The descender curves back to the left.

h — Tall ascender with a rightward stroke at the baseline that sometimes
    curves below the line. The scribe writes "th" as a connected ligature
    frequently.

i — A simple minim (short vertical stroke) with a dot or flick above.
    Difficult to distinguish from individual strokes of "m," "n," "u" in
    clusters of minims.

k — Tall ascender with two angled strokes extending to the right from the
    midpoint, forming the characteristic "k" shape. Appears in "take,"
    "make," "keepe."

l — Tall ascender without a bowl (distinguishing it from "b"). Simple
    vertical stroke, sometimes with a slight rightward curve at top.

m — Three minims joined at the baseline. Context is essential to distinguish
    from "in," "ni," "nn," etc. The scribe is consistent about spacing.

n — Two minims joined at the baseline. See note on minims under "m."

o — Rounded letter, usually a clear oval. Sometimes confused with secretary
    "a" but "o" is typically more closed and symmetrical.

p — Descender below the baseline with a bowl to the right at the x-height.
    The descender is usually straight or slightly curved.

q — Rare in this hand. Appears in "quantite," "Quinsie," "quarte." Has a
    descender and resembles "p" mirrored, with bowl on the left.

r — Two forms: (1) a short stroke with a rightward shoulder (like a truncated
    "n"); (2) a "2"-shaped "r" that appears after rounded letters like "o."
    The scribe uses both forms freely.

s — Two forms: (1) long "s" resembling "f" but WITHOUT a full crossbar,
    used in initial and medial positions; (2) short "s" (resembling modern
    lowercase) used in final position. This distinction is consistent.

t — Short letter at x-height with a crossbar. The vertical stroke barely
    rises above the other letters. Can be confused with "c" in some contexts.

u/v — "v" is used at the beginning of words ("verie," "vntill," "uppon"
    sometimes written with "v" form). "u" appears medially with minim form.
    The scribe uses "w" for the /w/ sound rather than "uu."

w — A distinctive letter formed by two connected "v" shapes, often with a
    final rightward stroke. Clearly wider than surrounding letters.

x — Rare. Appears in "waxe." Two crossing diagonal strokes.

y — Has a descender below the baseline that curves left. The top resembles
    a "v" or two short strokes. Common in "ytt," "yow," "youre." The
    descender distinguishes it from "v."

z — Very rare. Not observed in training pages.

CAPITALS
---------
Capitals are enlarged and often elaborated versions of the lowercase forms:
- T: Large sweeping initial stroke, often with a long crossbar
- F: Written as "ff" (double f) rather than a distinct capital
- A: Large rounded form with a more angular top
- B: Enlarged "b" with decorative loops
- Q: Very large, ornate form in "Quinsie" and "Queene"
- Y: Enlarged "y" at the start of "Yow"
- S: Large form, sometimes resembling a modern capital
- R: Enlarged "r" sometimes with extra flourish
- M: Enlarged version of lowercase, wider spread

COMMON ABBREVIATIONS & CONTRACTIONS
-------------------------------------
- "ytt" = it (the scribe writes "ytt" consistently)
- "yow" = you
- "withe" = with (consistently spelled with final -e)
- "lett" = let (doubled terminal consonant)
- "putt" = put (doubled terminal consonant)
- "att" = at (doubled terminal consonant)
- "probatum" = proven/tested (Latin, appears at end of recipes)

SPELLING PATTERNS
------------------
1. Double consonants at word endings: "putt," "lett," "att," "sett," "nott"
2. Terminal "-e" on many words: "withe," "muche," "colde," "warme," "sore"
3. "ee" for long vowels: "teethe," "keepe," "sweete," "bee," "three"
4. "ow" diphthong: "yow," "owt," "howre," "bowle"
5. "-inge" endings: "runninge," "moreninge," "swellinge," "stoppinge"
6. "ie" in some words: "Quinsie," "verie"
7. "-tion" appears as "-cion" or "-tion" in practice
8. "ey" in "they," "beynge"

COMMON VOCABULARY (recipe context)
------------------------------------
Take, boyle/boile, straine, putt, seethe, drinke, washe, stampe,
handefull, spoonefull, quarte, pinte, ounce, quantitye,
runninge water, white wine, butter, waxe, sugare, hunnye,
moreninge, eveninge, probatum, plaister/plaisteer

LIGATURES & LETTER COMBINATIONS
---------------------------------
- "th" — very frequent, written as connected strokes
- "sh" — the long "s" connects to "h"
- "st" — connected in many instances
- "tt" — doubled "t" is very common at word endings
- "ll" — doubled "l" in "all," "untill," "shall"
- "ss" — in "blossomes"

WORD BOUNDARIES
----------------
The scribe generally leaves clear spaces between words. Prepositions and
articles ("in," "of," "the," "and") are usually clearly separated. However,
some compound constructions may run together slightly.

PUNCTUATION
------------
- Colons used to separate clauses
- Periods (dots) used occasionally
- Commas after "Take" at recipe openings
- Tildes or wavy lines sometimes used as decorative dividers between recipes

POTENTIAL CONFUSION POINTS
---------------------------
1. Minim confusion: "m" vs. "in" vs. "ni" vs. "nn" vs. "un" — count strokes
2. Long "s" vs. "f": check for crossbar (f has one, long s does not or barely)
3. Secretary "d" vs. "e" + "l": the reversed loop of "d" is distinctive
4. "c" vs. "t": look for the crossbar on "t"
5. "u" vs. "n" vs. "v": position in word helps (v initial, u medial)
6. "y" vs. "v": check for descender below baseline
7. "a" vs. "o": secretary "a" has a tail or is more angular
