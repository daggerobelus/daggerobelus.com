LETTERFORM ALPHABET CHART
Brumwich MS 160, Page 10
=========================================================

HAND DESCRIPTION:
This scribe writes in a mixed hand — predominantly an informal italic
with some secretary-hand influence. The writing is relatively rapid
and somewhat cramped, with moderate consistency. Letters tend to lean
slightly to the right. The hand is small, with tight spacing between
words and lines. There is visible variation between more carefully
written recipe titles/openings and the hastier body text.

=========================================================
LOWERCASE LETTERS (a-z)
=========================================================

a:
  Form: Single-compartment 'a' — a rounded stroke curving left then
        closing with a short vertical on the right side. Resembles a
        modern lowercase 'a' but often left slightly open at the top.
        Occasionally appears more like a pointed 'u' shape when written
        hastily.
  Examples: "water," "and," "Take," "a" (article)
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' or 'o' when hastily formed; the
        closing stroke is sometimes minimal.

b:
  Form: Tall ascender rising well above the minim line, with a rounded
        bowl at the baseline. The ascender has a slight leftward lean
        at the top. The bowl is formed in one stroke curving right and
        back.
  Examples: "be," "but," "before"
  Confusion risk: Low — the tall ascender is distinctive. Could
        occasionally be confused with 'l' if the bowl is very small.

c:
  Form: Simple curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern 'c.'
        Consistent in size with other minims. Sometimes has a slight
        hook at the top.
  Examples: "concoction," "cleare," "cast"
  Confusion risk: Can look like an incomplete 'e' — the scribe's 'e'
        is essentially a 'c' with an added tongue/crossbar.

d:
  Form: Secretary-influenced 'd' — the ascender loops back to the LEFT
        (secretary form) rather than rising straight up (italic form).
        The bowl sits at the baseline. This is one of the most
        distinctive letters in the hand.
  Examples: "and," "good," "dried"
  Confusion risk: The looped ascender could resemble 'cl' combination
        if read carelessly. Secretary 'd' is a known confusion risk
        for modern readers.

e:
  Form: Small rounded form — a 'c' shape with a horizontal tongue or
        crossbar through the middle. Often very small and compressed.
        When at the end of a word, the tongue may extend into a
        connecting stroke.
  Examples: "the," "Take," "water," "before"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — frequently looks like 'c' when the tongue is
        faint; also resembles 'o' when fully closed. This is one of
        the most problematic letters in this hand.

f:
  Form: Descends below the baseline with a long descender, has a
        crossbar at or just above the baseline. The top curves to the
        right. The descender distinguishes it from a tall 's.'
  Examples: "for," "of," "from"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with long 's' (see 's' entry).
        The crossbar on 'f' is the key distinguishing feature.
        Also potentially confused with 'p' if descender is misread.

g:
  Form: A rounded bowl at the baseline with a descender curving back
        to the left, sometimes forming a closed loop below the line.
        The descender is relatively short compared to 'p' or 'y.'
  Examples: "good," "great," "Sugar"
  Confusion risk: Moderate — the bowl resembles 'q' but the descender
        direction differs (g curves left, q curves right or straight).

h:
  Form: Tall ascender with a curved stroke at the baseline extending
        to the right, resembling a modern 'h.' The second stroke
        (the arch) sometimes descends slightly below the baseline.
  Examples: "the," "hath," "have," "herbs"
  Confusion risk: Low when carefully written. In hasty passages, the
        arch can be minimal, making it resemble 'l' plus 'i.'

i:
  Form: Single minim (short vertical stroke) with a dot above, though
        the dot is frequently omitted or displaced. Without its dot,
        indistinguishable from a single stroke of 'm,' 'n,' or 'u.'
  Examples: "in," "it," "is," "with"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — without dot, identical to one minim of
        m/n/u. This is the single greatest source of confusion in
        minim-based scripts. Context is essential.

j:
  Form: Rarely appears as a distinct letter in this period. When
        present, written as an 'i' with descender, typically as the
        final letter in Roman numerals (e.g., "iij" for 3).
  Examples: Roman numerals only
  Confusion risk: Usually distinguished by context (numerals).

k:
  Form: Tall ascender with two short diagonal strokes meeting at the
        minim line — one angling down-left, one angling down-right.
        Relatively rare in this text.
  Examples: "Take" (when not capitalized), "make"
  Confusion risk: Low — distinctive shape. Occasionally the upper
        diagonal could make it resemble 'h' if hastily formed.

l:
  Form: Simple tall ascender stroke, sometimes with a very slight
        rightward curve at the bottom. No additional features.
  Examples: "let," "last," "long," "cleare"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' without a clear bowl, or be
        absorbed into adjacent letters. In combinations like 'll'
        or 'li,' distinguishing individual letters is difficult.

m:
  Form: Three minims (short vertical strokes) connected at the top
        by arches. The three humps are roughly equal in height.
  Examples: "more," "make," "them"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — three minims are easily misread as
        'ni,' 'in,' 'iu,' 'ui,' etc. The minim problem is acute
        with this letter. Must use context to distinguish.

n:
  Form: Two minims connected by an arch at the top. Identical in
        stroke structure to parts of 'm.'
  Examples: "and," "in," "no," "not"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — two minims identical to 'u' (which
        also has two minims connected at bottom). Also confused
        with parts of 'm' or 'ii.' The arch direction (top vs.
        bottom) is the only distinguishing feature.

o:
  Form: Rounded closed form, slightly oval. Generally consistent
        in size. Sometimes not fully closed at the top.
  Examples: "of," "or," "one," "good"
  Confusion risk: Moderate — when not fully closed, can resemble
        'a' or 'e.' Generally one of the more legible letters.

p:
  Form: Descender going straight down below the baseline, with a
        rounded bowl at the minim line on the right side. The
        descender is straight, unlike 'g.'
  Examples: "part," "put," "pieces," "pepper"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'q' (see below), though 'q' is rare
        except before 'u.' Also the bowl can look like 'b' if the
        descender is not noticed.

q:
  Form: Rounded bowl with a descender, but the descender angles to
        the RIGHT (unlike 'p' which goes straight down or 'g' which
        curves left). Almost always followed by 'u.'
  Examples: "quantity"
  Confusion risk: Mirror-image of 'p' — but the nearly universal
        'qu' combination provides context.

r:
  Form: Two forms present — (1) a short vertical stroke with a small
        rightward shoulder at the top (modern 'r' form), and (2) a
        '2'-shaped form used after rounded letters like 'o' (the
        round 'r' or '2-r,' a secretary convention). Both appear.
  Examples: "receipt," "rose," "great," "or" (2-shaped r)
  Confusion risk: HIGH — the round '2-r' can be mistaken for 'z,'
        a numeral, or simply missed. The standard form's shoulder
        can be minimal, making it look like 'i' or part of 'n.'

s:
  Form: TWO FORMS — (1) Long 's' resembling 'f' but WITHOUT a
        crossbar, used at the beginning and middle of words. Extends
        above and sometimes below the minim line. (2) Round/terminal
        's' (modern form) used only at the END of words.
  Examples: long s: "said," "such," "sugar" / round s: "is," "this"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — long 's' is frequently confused with 'f.'
        Look for the crossbar: 'f' has one, long 's' does not.
        This is a systematic confusion risk throughout the MS.

t:
  Form: Short vertical stroke (about 1.5x minim height) with a
        crossbar. The crossbar is often a quick horizontal flick
        that may extend to the right and connect to the next letter.
  Examples: "the," "to," "that," "Take"
  Confusion risk: Moderate — when the crossbar is faint, can look
        like 'l' (but 't' is shorter). In combination 'th,' the
        crossbar of 't' sometimes merges with the arch of 'h.'

u:
  Form: Two minims connected by a curve at the BOTTOM (as opposed
        to 'n' which connects at the top). In this period, 'u' and
        'v' are often interchangeable; 'u' typically appears mid-word
        and 'v' at the start.
  Examples: "put," "but," "upon," "use"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — identical minim structure to 'n.'
        Also interchangeable with 'v' by position. The bottom-
        connecting curve is the only structural difference from 'n,'
        and it is often barely visible.

v:
  Form: Used primarily at the beginning of words where modern English
        would use either 'u' or 'v.' Formed with two diagonal strokes
        meeting at a point at the baseline, slightly more angular
        than 'u.'
  Examples: "very," "vinegar" (may appear as "uinegar")
  Confusion risk: Moderate — distinguished from 'u' mainly by word
        position. The angular form can resemble 'b' in some hands.

w:
  Form: Formed as two 'v' shapes joined, or sometimes as a connected
        series of curves. Wider than other letters. Generally
        legible due to its distinctive width.
  Examples: "water," "with," "well," "was"
  Confusion risk: Low — its width makes it identifiable. Occasionally
        the middle section can be ambiguous.

x:
  Form: Two crossing diagonal strokes. Relatively rare in English
        text of this period.
  Examples: Rare — possibly in recipe quantities or Latin terms
  Confusion risk: Low — distinctive shape when it appears.

y:
  Form: Similar to modern 'y' — two strokes meeting above the
        baseline with a descender curving to the left below. The
        descender sometimes forms a loop. Can resemble a thorn (þ)
        usage in abbreviations like "ye" for "the."
  Examples: "you," "your," "yet," "days"
  Confusion risk: Moderate — the descender can resemble 'g' or 'p'
        if the upper strokes are unclear. Historical "y" for thorn
        (y^e = the) can cause misreadings.

z:
  Form: Rare. When present, formed with a horizontal top stroke,
        diagonal, and horizontal bottom stroke, sometimes with a
        descender.
  Examples: Very rare in this text
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with round 'r' (2-shaped) or
        the numeral '3' in some contexts.

=========================================================
CAPITAL LETTERS
=========================================================

A:
  Form: Two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak, with a crossbar.
        Sometimes the left stroke begins with a serif-like initial
        curve. Generally taller than surrounding text.
  Examples: "A water," "And," "Also"
  Confusion risk: Low — distinctive as a capital.

B:
  Form: Tall vertical stroke with two rounded bowls on the right.
        Often decorated with a slight flourish at the top.
  Examples: Rare in this sample
  Confusion risk: Low.

C:
  Form: Large curved stroke, essentially an enlarged lowercase 'c.'
  Examples: Possible recipe titles
  Confusion risk: Low — size distinguishes it.

D:
  Form: Can appear in either secretary form (looped ascender) at
        a larger scale, or a more formal upright form with a large
        bowl.
  Examples: "Dried"
  Confusion risk: Low at capital size.

F:
  Form: Enlarged version of lowercase, with a pronounced crossbar
        and sometimes a decorative initial stroke.
  Examples: "For"
  Confusion risk: Low — the capital size helps distinguish from
        long 's.'

I:
  Form: Simple tall vertical stroke, sometimes with serifs at top
        and bottom. Can be confused with 'J' in this period.
  Examples: Used at sentence starts
  Confusion risk: Moderate — 'I' and 'J' not yet fully distinct.

P:
  Form: Tall vertical stroke with a bowl at the top right, sometimes
        open. The descender may extend below the baseline.
  Examples: Recipe ingredients
  Confusion risk: Low at capital size.

R:
  Form: Vertical stroke with a bowl at the top and a diagonal leg.
        Sometimes the leg is replaced by a curved tail.
  Examples: "Receipt," "Rose"
  Confusion risk: Low.

S:
  Form: The capital 'S' uses the modern round form (not long 's'),
        often with decorative swashes. Distinctly larger than the
        lowercase round 's.'
  Examples: "Sugar," "Said"
  Confusion risk: Low — clearly a capital.

T:
  Form: A broad horizontal crossbar with a vertical stroke descending
        from the center. The crossbar often has decorative extensions.
        Very prominent as a recipe opener.
  Examples: "Take" — appears frequently as recipe openings
  Confusion risk: Low — highly distinctive and common.

W:
  Form: Enlarged 'w' — two joined 'v' forms with potential decorative
        initial stroke.
  Examples: "Water"
  Confusion risk: Low.

=========================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL FEATURES
=========================================================

1. THORN ABBREVIATION: "y^e" (y with superscript e) = "the"
   The letter 'y' stands in for the thorn character (þ). May also
   appear as "y^t" = "that." Look for superscript letters after 'y.'

2. SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS: Various words are abbreviated with letters
   raised above the line — e.g., "w^th" = "with," "w^ch" = "which,"
   "y^r" = "your." The superscript letters sit clearly above the
   baseline text.

3. MACRON/TILDE OVER VOWELS: A horizontal line or tilde over a vowel
   typically indicates a missing 'm' or 'n' — e.g., "comon" might
   be written "como" with a line over the 'o.'

4. AMPERSAND / TIRONIAN ET: The word "and" may appear as an ampersand
   (&) or the Tironian et sign (resembling a '7' or reversed 'c').

5. TERMINAL FLOURISHES: Final letters in words sometimes have
   decorative trailing strokes, especially 'e,' 's,' and 'd.' These
   are not additional letters.

6. LONG 's' vs. ROUND 's': Long 's' (looks like 'f' without crossbar)
   used at word beginnings and internally. Round 's' (modern form)
   used only at word endings. The combination 'ss' may appear as
   long-s + round-s.

7. SPELLING VARIANTS: Expect early modern spelling — 'u'/'v'
   interchangeable, 'i'/'j' interchangeable, doubled vowels, terminal
   '-e' on words that have since lost it (e.g., "cleare," "herbe").

=========================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (Highest to Lowest)
=========================================================

CRITICAL RISK (expect frequent misreadings):
  1. i / m / n / u — THE MINIM PROBLEM. These letters are all
     composed of short vertical strokes (minims) and are routinely
     indistinguishable from one another. A word like "minim" itself
     is simply a row of identical strokes. This is the #1 source
     of transcription error in secretary and mixed hands.

  2. long s / f — Both have tall vertical strokes extending above
     the minim line. The ONLY difference is the crossbar on 'f.'
     When the crossbar is faint, rushed, or blotted, these are
     effectively identical.

  3. e / c — The scribe's 'e' is a 'c' with a small tongue. When
     the tongue is minimal or the ink is faded, 'e' and 'c' merge.

HIGH RISK (expect regular misreadings):
  4. c / t — In hasty writing, a short 't' with faint crossbar can
     resemble 'c.' The crossbar is the key differentiator.

  5. u / v — Interchangeable by early modern convention, but also
     structurally similar. Position in the word is the main guide.

  6. r (round form) / z / numerals — The '2-shaped' round r after
     vowels can be mistaken for 'z' or the number '2.'

  7. a / o — When 'a' is not fully closed, it resembles 'o' and
     vice versa. Both are small rounded forms.

  8. secretary d / cl — The leftward-looping ascender of secretary
     'd' can look like a 'c' followed by 'l' to modern eyes.

MODERATE RISK (occasional misreadings):
  9. e / o — When 'e' is fully closed and the tongue is invisible,
     it becomes 'o.'

  10. h / b — If 'h' arch is very rounded or 'b' bowl is angular,
      these can converge. Generally distinguishable.

  11. y / g / p — All have descenders; confusion occurs when the
      above-baseline portions are unclear.

  12. t / l — Both are vertical strokes; 't' is shorter with a
      crossbar, but if the crossbar is faint, they merge.

LOW RISK (rare misreadings):
  13. w / m — Width difference usually distinguishes them, but
      a narrow 'w' or a wide 'm' could cause confusion.

  14. k / h — Similar ascender structure, but 'k' has angular
      strokes vs. 'h' has an arch.

  15. b / l — Distinguished by the bowl on 'b,' which is usually
      visible.

=========================================================
NOTES FOR TRANSCRIPTION AGENTS
=========================================================

- When encountering a string of minims, COUNT them and consider ALL
  possible letter combinations (in/ni/m/iu/ui etc.), then choose
  based on what makes a real English word.

- For long s vs. f: always check for the crossbar. If uncertain,
  consider whether 'f' or 's' makes sense in context.

- This scribe's hand becomes less careful in the body of recipes
  compared to recipe titles/openings. Expect higher confusion rates
  in densely written passages.

- Recipe titles and opening words ("Take...") are generally the most
  legible parts of the text and the best anchors for establishing
  letter forms.

- Watch for ink blots, bleed-through from the other side of the page,
  and fading — all of which increase confusion risk beyond the
  inherent letterform similarities.
