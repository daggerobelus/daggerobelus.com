SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO HENSLOW MS688 SECRETARY HAND
===================================================
Compiled from study of pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20

SECTION 1: OVERALL CHARACTERISTICS
-----------------------------------
This is a neat, practiced secretary hand from 1601. The scribe writes
with consistent letterforms and clear word spacing. Titles are written
in a larger, more decorative script. The body text is smaller but
generally legible with careful attention to individual letter shapes.

The scribe consistently doubles final consonants: putt, ytt, itt, sett,
lett, pott, hott, nott. This is a strong scribal habit that must be
preserved in every instance.

The scribe uses "ytt" and "itt" interchangeably for "it."
The scribe writes "yow" for "you."
The scribe writes "withe" for "with" (frequently, though not always).
The scribe writes "togeather" for "together."
The scribe writes "euerie" for "every."
The scribe writes "moreninge" for "morning."
The scribe writes "muche" for "much."

SECTION 2: CAPITAL LETTERS
---------------------------
Capital T: Very large, ornate, with sweeping curves. The "Take" that
begins most recipes has a dramatically large T with decorative flourishes.
It extends well above the line and often has a long horizontal stroke.

Capital F: Written as "ff" (double-f convention). Seen in "ffor" and
at the start of words like "ffasten." This is the standard secretary
hand convention where two lowercase f's represent a capital F.

Capital A: Seen in titles and recipe headings. Has a broad, rounded top
with the crossbar visible. Example: "A watter" on page 08.

Capital B: Has a tall ascender with two rounded bowls. Seen in "Boyle,"
"Butt," "Butter." Distinguished from 't' by its fuller bowl shape.

Capital Y: Seen in "Yow" — has a long descending stroke.

Capital R: Seen in "Redd" (page 20) — relatively recognizable with a
vertical stroke and a rightward curve.

Capital Q: Seen in "Quinsie" and "Quinsee" — large and ornate with
a long descending tail.

Capital H: Seen in "Halle" (page 20) — has a tall vertical stroke
with a curved approach to the second stroke.

SECTION 3: LOWERCASE LETTERS
------------------------------
a: Small, round, sometimes open at the top. Can resemble 'u' when
hastily written.

b: Has an ascender that rises above the line, with a round bowl at
the baseline. Distinguished from 't' by having a fuller, rounder bowl.

c: Small, curved, open to the right. Can be confused with 't' in
some positions.

d: Has a looped ascender that curves to the left. The loop is
distinctive of secretary hand 'd'. Distinguished from 'g' by the
direction of the descender — d goes up-left, g curves back right.

e: Often has a characteristic secretary form — written as a small
backward loop, sometimes resembling a modern 'e' with extra curvature.

f: Has an ascender AND a descender — it crosses the baseline and goes
below. Has a crossbar. Distinguished from long-s by the crossbar.

g: Has a descender that curves back to the right (unlike d which
loops left). Seen clearly in "-inge" endings.

h: Has a tall ascender with a curved stroke descending to the right.

i: A single minim stroke. Sometimes has a dot above it, but not always.

k: Has an ascender with angular strokes joining at mid-height.

l: Tall vertical stroke, similar height to long-s and f but without
crossbar or descender below the baseline.

long-s: Tall stroke similar to f and l. KEY DISTINCTION: no crossbar
(unlike f) and descends below the baseline slightly. Used in initial
and medial positions; round-s used at word ends.

m: Three minim strokes connected at the top.

n: Two minim strokes connected at the top. Can be confused with 'u'
which is two minims connected at the bottom.

o: Small round letter at mid-height. Can look like 'a' when open.

p: Has a descender below the baseline with a round bowl above.

r: In secretary hand, can look quite different from modern r. Often
appears as a short angled stroke. Can resemble 'v' in some contexts.

round-s: Used at word endings. Looks like a modern lowercase s.

t: Has an ascender (shorter than b, d, l) with a crossbar. The crossbar
may be faint or joined to the next letter. Can be confused with 'b'
when the crossbar is unclear.

u: Two minim strokes connected at the bottom. Distinguished from 'n'
(connected at top) by the curve direction.

v: Used sometimes at word beginnings where modern English uses 'u,'
but this scribe often uses 'u' initially too (e.g., "use," "untill,"
"uppon"). Do NOT over-correct u to v.

w: Broad letter formed by connected v-shapes. Distinctive and usually
clear.

x: Rare. Crossed strokes.

y: Has a descender. Seen frequently in "ytt," "yow," "ytt."

SECTION 4: COMMON WORD PATTERNS
---------------------------------
"Take" — begins almost every recipe. Large decorative T followed
by "ake" — use this as a reference for T, a, k, e.

"the" — extremely common. Use to calibrate t, h, e.

"and" — common conjunction. Use to calibrate a, n, d.

"of" — very common preposition. Use for o, f.

"in" — use for i, n (minim practice).

"withe" — the scribe's form of "with." Note the final e.

"then" — use for t, h, e, n.

"putt" — doubled final t. Use as reference for doubled consonants.

"thereto" / "thereof" / "therein" — compound prepositions the scribe
uses frequently.

"handefull" / "handfull" — note doubled final l and the variation
in spelling (with or without 'e').

"probatum" — Latin word meaning "proven/tested." Appears at the end
of some recipes. Written in a slightly different register.

SECTION 5: PUNCTUATION AND LAYOUT
-----------------------------------
The scribe uses colons (:) as the primary punctuation mark within
recipes, functioning somewhat like modern commas or semicolons.

Periods appear occasionally but are less common than colons.

Recipe titles are written in a larger hand, sometimes centered or
set apart from the body text.

The word "probatum" sometimes appears at the end of recipes, often
preceded by decorative marks or dashes.

SECTION 6: CRITICAL WARNINGS
------------------------------
1. MINIMS: The letters u, n, m, and i are built from identical short
   vertical strokes. Count them carefully. "nn" vs "un" vs "nu" all
   look similar.

2. LONG-S vs F vs L: All three are tall letters. Only f has a crossbar.
   Long-s may dip slightly below the baseline. L is typically the
   straightest vertical stroke.

3. DOUBLED CONSONANTS: This scribe doubles final t, l, and sometimes
   other consonants consistently. Always look for the second letter.

4. DO NOT MODERNIZE: Keep "ytt" not "yt" or "it." Keep "putt" not
   "put." Keep "moreninge" not "morning." Keep "withe" not "with."

5. The scribe's u/v usage: This scribe predominantly uses 'u' even
   at word beginnings: "use," "untill," "uppon." Do NOT change these
   to "vse," "vntill," "vppon" unless you clearly see a v-form.
   Exception: page 14 has "vntill" — the scribe is not perfectly
   consistent, so read what you see.

6. "ff" = capital F: At word beginnings, "ff" represents a capital F.
   But "ff" can also appear mid-word (e.g., "Deffnes" = deafness).
