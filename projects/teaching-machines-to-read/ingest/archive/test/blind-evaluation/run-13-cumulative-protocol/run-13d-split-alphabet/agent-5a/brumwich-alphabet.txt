BRUMWICH MS 160 — PAGE 10 LETTERFORM ALPHABET CHART
=====================================================
Source image: brumwich-ms160-page10.jpg
Hand: Mixed secretary/italic hand, 17th-century English
General character: Relatively fluent, moderately cursive hand with
  secretary-form letters mixed with italic tendencies. Writing is
  small and tightly spaced, with visible ink variation. Two-page
  spread visible (verso and recto).

ANCHOR WORDS USED FOR IDENTIFICATION
======================================
- "water" / "A water" (recipe title, appears near top of left page)
- "Take" (recipe opening formula, appears multiple times)
- "the" (frequent throughout)
- "and" (frequent throughout)
- "of" (frequent throughout)
- "in" (frequent throughout)
- "with" (appears in ingredient lists)
- "for" / "For" (recipe title marker)
- "good" (appears in descriptions)
- "green" (ingredient reference)
- "sugar" (ingredient reference)
- "drink" / "Drinke" (recipe context)
- "pint" / "pinte" (measurement)
- "boile" (cooking instruction)
- "put" (instruction verb)

=====================================================
LOWERCASE LETTERFORMS (a–z)
=====================================================

a
  Form: Secretary 'a' — formed with a rounded bowl, often open at
    the top. Sometimes resembles a small 'u' with a curve. The bowl
    sits on the baseline and the stroke does not rise above minim
    height.
  Examples: "water", "Take", "and", "a"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' or 'o' when hastily written.
    The open top of secretary 'a' is the key distinguishing feature.

b
  Form: Tall ascender with a loop, descending into a rounded bowl
    on the right. The ascender often has a leftward approach stroke.
    The bowl sits on the baseline.
  Examples: "boile", "be", "before"
  Confusion risk: Ascender loop can resemble 'l' if the bowl is
    small or faint. Rarely confused with 'h' because 'h' has a
    different descending stroke on the right.

c
  Form: Simple curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern
    'c'. Sometimes has a small entry stroke at the top.
  Examples: "reciept", "course"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'e' if the interior horizontal
    stroke of 'e' is faint. Also sometimes hard to distinguish
    from 't' when 't' lacks a clear crossbar.

d
  Form: Secretary 'd' — the distinctive looped form. The bowl is
    formed first, then the ascender loops backward to the left
    (resembling a modern cursive 'd' but with an exaggerated
    leftward loop). This is one of the most recognizable secretary
    letterforms.
  Examples: "and", "good", "powder"
  Confusion risk: The leftward-looping ascender can be misread as
    'cl' or 'el' by those unfamiliar with secretary hand. Critical
    letter to master.

e
  Form: Secretary 'e' — resembles a reversed '3' or a small backward
    loop. The letter is formed with a single stroke that curves up
    and back. Sometimes simplified to a more italic form (a simple
    curved stroke with a horizontal bar).
  Examples: "the", "Take", "water", "before"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — secretary 'e' is easily confused with 'c',
    'o', or even 'r' depending on execution. When the loop is tight,
    it can look like a dot or comma.

f
  Form: Descends below the baseline. Has a long vertical stroke
    with a crossbar or loop in the middle. The descender curves
    to the left. Often has a leftward approach stroke at the top.
  Examples: "for", "of", "efore"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble long 's' (see below). Both descend
    below the baseline and have a vertical stroke. The crossbar on
    'f' is the distinguishing mark — long 's' lacks it.

g
  Form: Rounded bowl above the baseline with a descender that
    curves to the left or forms a closed loop below the line. The
    descender is distinctive — often a sharp leftward hook.
  Examples: "good", "sugar", "green"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'y' if the descender shape is
    ambiguous. The bowl of 'g' is rounder; 'y' typically has two
    strokes meeting at a point.

h
  Form: Tall ascender (sometimes looped) with a shoulder stroke
    that descends to the baseline. The right stroke sometimes
    curves below the baseline slightly. In secretary hand, the
    right leg can loop under the baseline.
  Examples: "the", "his", "have"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' if written quickly. The key
    difference is that 'h' has an open right side descending to
    the baseline, while 'b' has a closed bowl.

i
  Form: A single minim stroke, sometimes dotted but often not in
    this hand. When dotted, the dot may be displaced or faint.
  Examples: "in", "with", "pinte"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — indistinguishable from individual
    strokes of 'm', 'n', 'u' without context. This is the
    fundamental minim problem in secretary hand.

j
  Form: Rare in this period. When it appears, it looks like 'i'
    with a descender curving left. More often, 'i' is used in
    its place (e.g., "iuice" for "juice").
  Examples: Rare/absent on this page
  Confusion risk: Confused with 'i' — essentially the same letter
    in this period.

k
  Form: Tall ascender with two angular strokes meeting at the
    midpoint on the right. The upper right stroke often has a
    distinctive loop or kick.
  Examples: "Take", "make"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' or 'h' if the angular strokes
    are unclear. Look for the sharp angular junction at the
    midpoint.

l
  Form: Tall ascender, often with a loop at the top. Simple
    vertical stroke descending to the baseline. May have a small
    foot serif.
  Examples: "boile", "let"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' (if a minim follows), 'h',
    or a tall 'e'. In sequences like 'll', can be misread as
    other combinations.

m
  Form: Three minim strokes connected at the top. Sits on the
    baseline. The first stroke often has an approach curve.
  Examples: "make", "some", "them"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — the three minims can be misread as
    'in', 'ni', 'ui', 'iu', 'mi', 'im', etc. Context is the
    only reliable guide.

n
  Form: Two minim strokes connected at the top. Nearly identical
    to 'u' in appearance (the strokes connect at the top for 'n'
    and at the bottom for 'u', but in practice this is often
    indistinguishable).
  Examples: "and", "in", "green"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — virtually identical to 'u'. Also
    part of the minim confusion cluster with 'i', 'm'.

o
  Form: Rounded oval, usually closed. Similar to modern 'o' but
    sometimes slightly angular or compressed.
  Examples: "of", "good", "boile"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'a' (especially open-top secretary
    'a'). When written small, may look like 'e'.

p
  Form: Descender going below the baseline with a bowl or shoulder
    on the right at minim height. The descender is usually straight
    or curves slightly left.
  Examples: "pinte", "put", "powder"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'q' (which curves its descender
    the opposite way), but 'q' is rare. More commonly confused
    with long-tailed 'y' in hasty writing.

q
  Form: Rare. When present, a bowl with a rightward-curving
    descender (opposite of 'p').
  Examples: "quarter" (if present)
  Confusion risk: Low — rare letter, and the rightward descender
    is distinctive when it appears.

r
  Form: Two main forms in this hand:
    (1) Short 'r' — a minim with a rightward shoulder or flick at
        the top. Used after most letters.
    (2) Round 'r' (2-shaped r) — resembles a '2', used after 'o'
        and other round letters. This is a classic secretary form.
  Examples: "water", "green", "for", "receipe"
  Confusion risk: SHORT 'r' can resemble 'v', 'n', or a minim.
    ROUND 'r' can resemble 'z' or a numeral '2'. Both forms
    require context.

s
  Form: THREE forms in this hand:
    (1) Long 's' — tall vertical stroke, resembles 'f' but WITHOUT
        a crossbar. Used at the beginning and middle of words.
    (2) Short/terminal 's' — resembles modern 's' or a figure-8.
        Used at the end of words.
    (3) Sigma 's' — a rounded, kidney-shaped form, sometimes used
        at the end of words. Resembles the numeral '6'.
  Examples: "sugar" (long s + short s), "some", "his"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — long 's' vs. 'f' is a classic confusion
    point. The ONLY reliable difference is the crossbar (present
    on 'f', absent on long 's'). Terminal 's' is usually clear.

t
  Form: A minim-height stroke with a crossbar. The crossbar may be
    light, displaced, or merged with the following letter. Sometimes
    the crossbar is a separate pen-lift.
  Examples: "the", "Take", "put", "water"
  Confusion risk: When the crossbar is faint or absent, 't' looks
    like 'l', 'i', or another minim. Also can resemble 'c' in
    hasty execution.

u
  Form: Two minim strokes connected at the bottom (a trough shape).
    Virtually identical to 'n' in many hands.
  Examples: "sugar", "put", "but"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — indistinguishable from 'n' without
    context. Part of the minim confusion cluster.

v
  Form: Used at the beginning of words where modern English uses
    'u' (e.g., "vse" for "use", "vpon" for "upon"). Formed as a
    pointed trough, similar to modern 'v' but sometimes with a
    rightward flick.
  Examples: "very" (if present), "vpon"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' or 'r'. At word-initial
    position, assume 'v' for the /v/ or /u/ sound.

w
  Form: Formed as a double-v or double-u. Two connected trough
    shapes. Often wide and takes up more horizontal space than
    other letters.
  Examples: "water", "with"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'vv' or 'uu'. Usually clear from
    its width and context.

x
  Form: Two crossing diagonal strokes. Rare in English text.
  Examples: Rare on this page
  Confusion risk: Low — distinctive when present.

y
  Form: Two strokes meeting at a point, with a descender that
    curves left below the baseline. The descender is often long
    and sweeping.
  Examples: "very", "they"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'g' (both have descenders). Also
    resembles thorn (see abbreviations). The descender shape is
    key — 'y' typically has a more angular junction above the
    baseline.

z
  Form: Rare. When present, resembles a '3' or a zigzag.
  Examples: Rare/absent on this page
  Confusion risk: Can resemble round 'r' (2-shaped r) or the
    numeral '3'.

=====================================================
CAPITAL LETTERFORMS
=====================================================

A
  Form: Two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak with a horizontal
    crossbar. Sometimes has a looped or flourished top. The left
    stroke often begins with an approach curve.
  Examples: "A water", "And"
  Confusion risk: Generally clear. Flourished forms can sometimes
    obscure the basic shape.

B
  Form: Vertical stroke with two bowls on the right. Often
    flourished at the top.
  Examples: Rare on this page
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'D' if the upper bowl is small.

D
  Form: Large looped form, sometimes with an exaggerated leftward
    sweep of the ascender (an enlarged version of secretary 'd').
  Examples: "Drinke"
  Confusion risk: The looped form can be misread as 'O' or 'G'
    by the unfamiliar reader.

F
  Form: Tall stroke with one or two crossbars. Often flourished
    with a sweeping initial curve.
  Examples: "For"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'T' if the lower crossbar is faint.
    Also resembles 'ff' ligature.

T
  Form: Vertical stroke with a prominent crossbar or cap stroke.
    Often the crossbar sweeps to the right with a flourish.
  Examples: "Take", "The"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'F' or 'I' depending on the
    crossbar placement. The crossbar on 'T' is typically higher
    than on 'F'.

I
  Form: A tall vertical stroke, sometimes with serifs at top and
    bottom. May have a small approach stroke.
  Examples: "In"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'J', 'T' (without clear crossbar),
    or 'l'.

S
  Form: A large sinuous curve, sometimes with a flourished head.
    More ornate than the lowercase forms.
  Examples: Rare on this page
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'G' or 'L' in very flourished
    examples.

W
  Form: Large double-v formation. Prominent and usually clear.
  Examples: If present
  Confusion risk: Generally distinctive due to size.

=====================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL FEATURES
=====================================================

1. THORN (resembles 'y')
   The letter thorn (representing 'th') looks like a 'y' and is
   commonly used in abbreviations like 'ye' (= "the") and 'yt'
   (= "that"). Look for superscript letters as a clue.

2. SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS
   Common in this hand for abbreviations:
   - 'wth' with superscript 'th' = "with"
   - 'wch' with superscript 'ch' = "which"
   - 'ye' with superscript 'e' = "the"
   - 'yt' with superscript 't' = "that"

3. MACRON / TILDE (line above a letter)
   A horizontal line or tilde above a vowel indicates a missing
   nasal consonant ('m' or 'n'):
   - 'comon' with tilde over 'o' = "common"
   - 'the' with tilde = "them" or "then"

4. AMPERSAND / 'and' ABBREVIATION
   The word "and" may appear as an ampersand-like symbol or a
   special ligature.

5. LONG S / F DISTINCTION
   As noted above, long 's' and 'f' are distinguished ONLY by
   the crossbar. This is the single most important distinction
   to master.

6. FINAL FLOURISHES
   Final letters (especially 'e', 's', 'd') sometimes have
   trailing flourishes that extend to the right. These are
   decorative and should not be read as additional letters.

7. 'ff' FOR CAPITAL F
   Common in this period — a double lowercase 'f' is used where
   we would use a capital 'F'. E.g., "ffor" = "For".

=====================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (highest to lowest)
=====================================================

TIER 1 — CRITICAL (expect frequent misreadings)
  1. MINIM CLUSTER: i / m / n / u
     The single greatest challenge. These letters are formed from
     identical vertical strokes (minims). A sequence like 'minim'
     is literally just a row of identical strokes. ONLY context
     and word knowledge can disambiguate.

  2. LONG s vs. f
     Distinguished only by the presence/absence of a crossbar,
     which can be very faint. Misreading one as the other changes
     words entirely (e.g., "suffer" vs. other readings).

  3. SECRETARY e vs. c vs. o
     The secretary 'e' (reversed-3 shape) can closely resemble
     'c' and 'o', especially when written quickly or with
     variable ink density.

TIER 2 — HIGH RISK (expect occasional misreadings)
  4. SECRETARY d vs. 'cl' or 'el'
     The distinctive looped secretary 'd' is unfamiliar to modern
     readers and is commonly misread as two letters.

  5. r (short form) vs. v vs. n
     The short 'r' with its rightward shoulder is easily mistaken
     for these other minim-based letters.

  6. t vs. l vs. c
     When the crossbar of 't' is faint, it becomes a minim ('l'
     or 'i'). When the vertical of 't' is short, it resembles 'c'.

  7. a vs. o vs. u
     The open-top secretary 'a' can resemble both 'o' (when
     closed) and 'u' (when open at the top).

TIER 3 — MODERATE RISK
  8. y vs. thorn (both look like 'y')
     Requires knowledge of abbreviation conventions to distinguish.

  9. round r (2-shaped) vs. z vs. numeral 2
     Context usually resolves this, but the visual similarity is
     real.

  10. g vs. y (both have descenders)
      Distinguished by the shape above the baseline (round bowl
      for 'g', angular junction for 'y').

  11. b vs. l (both have tall ascenders)
      Distinguished by the presence of a bowl on 'b'.

  12. Capital F vs. T vs. I
      Crossbar position and number of crossbars are the key
      distinguishing features.

TIER 4 — LOW RISK
  13. p vs. q (different descender direction)
  14. w vs. vv (width and context resolve this)
  15. k vs. h vs. b (angular strokes on 'k' are distinctive)
  16. x, j, z (rare letters, distinctive when present)

=====================================================
GENERAL ADVICE FOR TRANSCRIPTION
=====================================================

1. ALWAYS start by identifying anchor words (recipe titles,
   common words) and work outward from known to unknown.

2. For minim sequences, count the total number of minims and
   try ALL plausible letter combinations against known words.

3. For long s vs. f, look for the crossbar under magnification.
   When in doubt, consider which reading makes a real English word.

4. Secretary 'd' is your friend once you learn it — its
   distinctive loop makes it one of the most recognizable letters.

5. Watch for superscript letters as abbreviation markers.

6. This hand shows moderate consistency — the same letter may
   vary somewhat between instances, but the basic forms remain
   stable enough to identify with practice.
