LETTERFORM ALPHABET CHART
Brumwich MS 160, Page 10
=========================================================

SOURCE: Two-page spread (verso/recto) from an early modern
English recipe book. Mixed secretary-italic hand, likely
mid-to-late 17th century. The scribe writes in a relatively
fluid, moderately cursive hand with some secretary letter
survivals mixed into a predominantly italic style.

METHODOLOGY: Anchor words used to establish letterforms:
- "Take" (recipe openings), "water," "of," "the," "and,"
  "in," "with," "for," "good," "put," "boile," "sugar,"
  "make," "a," "to," "it," "then," "your"

=========================================================
LOWERCASE ALPHABET
=========================================================

a:
  Form: Round, single-compartment italic 'a'. The scribe
  uses an open bowl that sometimes fails to close fully at
  the top right. Written in one or two strokes. The letter
  sits on the baseline with no descender.
  Examples: "a" (article, frequent), "Take," "water," "and"
  Confusion risk: Can look like 'u' when hastily formed and
  the bowl opens. May also resemble 'o' when tightly closed.

b:
  Form: Tall ascender with a round bowl at baseline. The
  ascender rises to roughly the same height as 'l' and 'h'.
  The bowl is formed in a clockwise motion and closes at
  the baseline. Sometimes the ascender has a slight leftward
  hook at top.
  Examples: "boile," "be," "herb"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'l' if the bowl is
  small or poorly formed. The ascender can look like 'h' if
  the bowl opens.

c:
  Form: Simple open curve, italic style. Sits on the baseline
  with no ascender or descender. Formed as a leftward arc.
  Sometimes has a slight hairline entry stroke at top.
  Examples: "receipts," "sauce"
  Confusion risk: May be confused with 'e' when the scribe
  adds a crossbar-like entry stroke. In tight spacing, can
  blur with adjacent letters.

d:
  Form: The scribe uses BOTH secretary 'd' and italic 'd'.
  The secretary form has a looped ascender that curves back
  to the left (resembling a reversed 'e' atop a bowl). The
  italic form has a straight or slightly curved ascender
  leaning right. The secretary 'd' appears more frequently.
  Examples: "and," "good," "dried"
  Confusion risk: HIGH. Secretary 'd' can look like 'e' + 'l'
  or 'cl' to modern readers. The looped ascender is a major
  confusion point. Italic 'd' is less problematic.

e:
  Form: Small, round, sits on baseline. Formed with a short
  horizontal crossbar and curved body. Sometimes appears as
  little more than a short backward loop. The scribe
  frequently uses 'e' as a final letter with a slight
  rightward flourish.
  Examples: "the," "Take," "make," "water"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'c' or 'o' in hasty writing.
  Final 'e' flourishes can be mistaken for 's' or ignored
  entirely.

f:
  Form: Descends below the baseline (like secretary tradition).
  Has a crossbar at or near the baseline. The descender curves
  leftward. The ascender rises above the line. This is one of
  the more distinctive letters in this hand.
  Examples: "for," "of"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with LONG 's' (see 's' entry).
  Both descend below baseline and have similar vertical strokes.
  The crossbar on 'f' is the key distinguishing feature, but
  it is not always clearly visible.

g:
  Form: Round upper bowl sitting on the baseline with a
  descending tail that curves leftward and sometimes loops
  back. The tail descends about as far as 'p' or 'y'.
  Examples: "good," "sugar," "strong"
  Confusion risk: The upper bowl can look like 'q' or 'y'
  depending on tail formation. When the tail is short, may
  be mistaken for 'a' or 'o'.

h:
  Form: Tall ascender (like 'b' and 'l') with a shoulder
  stroke that curves down to the baseline. The shoulder
  sometimes has a slight rightward kick at the bottom.
  Written in two strokes generally.
  Examples: "the," "then," "herb," "with"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' if the shoulder stroke
  looks like a bowl. The descending stroke can blend with
  adjacent letters in tight writing.

i:
  Form: Simple minim stroke (single downstroke) with a dot
  above. The dot is not always present or may be displaced.
  Examples: "in," "it," "with," "boile"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH. Without the dot, 'i' is
  indistinguishable from a single minim of 'n', 'm', 'u',
  or 'w'. Minim confusion is the single biggest challenge
  in this hand.

j:
  Form: Rarely used. When present, appears as an 'i' with a
  descender curving leftward. May appear in archaic spellings.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.
  Confusion risk: Could be mistaken for 'i' or 'y'.

k:
  Form: Tall ascender with two diagonal strokes meeting at
  the midline. The upper diagonal sometimes has a loop.
  Examples: "Take," "make"
  Confusion risk: Moderate. Can look like 'h' if the
  diagonals are not clearly separated, or like 'b' in
  very hasty writing.

l:
  Form: Simple tall ascender with no bowl, shoulder, or
  other attachment. Just a vertical or slightly rightward-
  leaning stroke descending to the baseline. Sometimes has
  a small foot serif.
  Examples: "boile," "let," "small"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'b' (if adjacent
  letter creates illusion of a bowl), 'h' (if a following
  letter's entry stroke mimics a shoulder), or tall 's'.

m:
  Form: Three minim strokes connected at the top, sitting on
  the baseline. The humps are relatively even. Sometimes the
  final stroke has a slight rightward curve.
  Examples: "make," "small," "them"
  Confusion risk: HIGH. Three minims can be misread as 'in',
  'ni', 'iu', 'ui', etc. Classic minim confusion problem.

n:
  Form: Two minim strokes connected at top. Sits on baseline.
  Similar to 'm' but with one fewer hump.
  Examples: "and," "in," "then," "one"
  Confusion risk: HIGH. Two minims can be misread as 'u', 'ii',
  or part of 'm'. See minim confusion note under 'i'.

o:
  Form: Round, closed letter sitting on the baseline. Formed
  in a single counterclockwise stroke. Generally well-
  differentiated from other letters.
  Examples: "of," "or," "good," "to," "boile"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'a' when 'a' is tightly
  closed. In very small writing, may blur with 'e'.

p:
  Form: Descender drops below baseline, with a round bowl at
  the right sitting on or just above the baseline. The
  descender is usually straight.
  Examples: "put," "pepper," "upon"
  Confusion risk: Moderate. Could be confused with 'b' if the
  descender is not noticed (e.g., on a faded baseline), or
  with 'q' which has a similar structure.

q:
  Form: Similar to 'p' but mirrored — the bowl is on the left
  and the descender/tail on the right. Always followed by 'u'.
  Examples: "quantity"
  Confusion risk: Could be confused with 'p' or 'g'. The
  automatic 'qu' pairing helps identify it.

r:
  Form: The scribe uses primarily an italic 'r' — a short
  downstroke with a small rightward shoulder or tick at the
  top. Occasionally a secretary '2-shaped r' appears after
  round letters (o, b, p).
  Examples: "for," "your," "water," "or"
  Confusion risk: Moderate. The short italic 'r' can look like
  'v' or 'n' in hasty writing. The secretary '2-shaped r' can
  be mistaken for 'z' or a number.

s:
  Form: TWO FORMS IN USE.
  (1) Long 's' — used initially and medially. Looks like a
      tall stroke descending below baseline, very similar to
      'f' but WITHOUT a crossbar. This is the more common form.
  (2) Round/terminal 's' — used at word endings. Looks like
      the modern lowercase 's'.
  Examples: "sugar," "small," "sauce," "receipts"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH. Long 's' vs 'f' is a constant
  source of error. The only reliable distinction is the
  crossbar on 'f'. Long 's' can also be mistaken for 'l'
  when the descender is minimal.

t:
  Form: Short vertical stroke with a crossbar. The crossbar
  may extend in both directions or only to the right. The
  ascender is shorter than 'l', 'b', 'h' — roughly half
  their height. Sometimes the crossbar is very faint.
  Examples: "the," "Take," "to," "it," "put," "then"
  Confusion risk: Moderate. Can be confused with 'l' if the
  crossbar is missing or faint. Can resemble 'c' or 'r' if
  the vertical stroke is short.

u:
  Form: A curved trough shape — down, curve, up. Sits on the
  baseline. Sometimes has an exit stroke going right.
  Examples: "put," "your," "sugar," "upon"
  Confusion risk: HIGH. Two minims read as a trough can be
  mistaken for 'n', 'ii', or parts of 'm'/'w'. Classic minim
  confusion. Also can resemble 'a' when the exit stroke loops.

v:
  Form: Angular or slightly curved V-shape. Used at the
  beginning of words where modern English uses 'u' (e.g.,
  "vpon" for "upon"). Also used as the consonant 'v'.
  Examples: "very," "vinegar"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' or 'r' in hasty writing.
  The angular nature usually distinguishes it, but when
  rounded it merges with 'u'.

w:
  Form: Essentially a double-v or double-u. Four minim
  strokes or two connected v-shapes. Often one of the wider
  letters in the scribe's hand.
  Examples: "water," "with," "was"
  Confusion risk: HIGH. Four minims can be misread as various
  combinations of i, n, u, m. Context is essential.

x:
  Form: Two diagonal strokes crossing. Rare in this text.
  Examples: Rare; possibly in abbreviated forms.
  Confusion risk: Low, due to distinctive shape.

y:
  Form: Two strokes meeting at a point, with the right stroke
  descending below the baseline as a tail (curving left). The
  tail distinguishes it from 'v'.
  Examples: "your," "very" (if spelled with y)
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'v' if the descender is
  short. May also resemble 'g' or 'p' in hasty writing.

z:
  Form: Rare. When present, has a horizontal top stroke,
  diagonal, and horizontal bottom stroke, sometimes with a
  descending tail.
  Examples: Very rare in this sample.
  Confusion risk: Could be confused with secretary '2-shaped r'
  or the numeral 3.

=========================================================
CAPITAL LETTERS
=========================================================

A:
  Form: Two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak with a
  crossbar. Sometimes the left stroke has a serif or
  foot. Moderately large compared to lowercase.
  Examples: "A water," "A receipt"

B:
  Form: Tall vertical stroke with two bumps/bowls on
  the right side. The upper bowl is usually smaller.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.

C:
  Form: Large open curve, similar to lowercase but
  scaled up. Sometimes has a decorative initial stroke.
  Examples: Possibly in recipe names.

D:
  Form: Large curved letter. May use either a looped
  secretary form (scaled up) or a more formal italic D.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.

F:
  Form: Tall vertical with one or two horizontal crossbars.
  Distinguished from 'f' by size and context (sentence-
  initial position). Crossbars more prominent than lowercase.
  Examples: "For"

G:
  Form: Large curved letter with a horizontal bar or
  inward-turning tail.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.

I:
  Form: Simple tall stroke, sometimes with serifs at
  top and bottom. May have a dot or not.
  Examples: Initial "I" or "It"

M:
  Form: Scaled-up version of lowercase, with clear
  three-hump structure or two vertical strokes with
  connecting diagonals.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.

P:
  Form: Tall vertical with a closed or open bowl at top
  right. Bowl is proportionally larger than lowercase.
  Examples: Possibly in recipe names.

R:
  Form: Vertical stroke with a bowl at top right and a
  diagonal leg. More ornate than lowercase.
  Examples: Possibly in recipe names.

S:
  Form: Large round 'S' shape (NOT long s). May have
  decorative entry/exit strokes. Always the modern form,
  never the long-s form, in capitals.
  Examples: Rare; possibly in names.

T:
  Form: Prominent crossbar atop a vertical stroke. The
  crossbar often extends well beyond the vertical in both
  directions. Clearly differentiated from lowercase 't'
  by size.
  Examples: "Take," "Then," "The"
  Note: "Take" is a key anchor — it opens most recipes
  and the capital T is very recognizable.

W:
  Form: Large double-V or double-U shape. Wider than most
  capitals. May have decorative initial stroke.
  Examples: Rare in this sample.

=========================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL FEATURES
=========================================================

1. THORN / 'ye' form:
   The scribe may use a character resembling 'y' for the
   thorn (representing "th"). "ye" = "the"; "yt" = "that".
   Watch for this carefully — do not read as literal 'y'.

2. SUPERSCRIPT ABBREVIATIONS:
   - Superscript letters indicate omitted letters beneath.
     Common: "wth" with superscript 't' = "with"
     "yor" with superscript 'r' = "your"
     "wch" with superscript 'ch' = "which"
   - The superscript is often very small and easily missed.

3. AMPERSAND / ABBREVIATION FOR "AND":
   The scribe may use an ampersand-like symbol or a
   shorthand mark for "and."

4. TERMINAL FLOURISHES:
   Final letters (especially 'e', 's', 'd') may have
   decorative exit strokes that can be mistaken for
   additional letters.

5. LONG 's' USAGE RULE:
   Long 's' appears at the start and middle of words.
   Round 's' appears at the end of words. Double 's' may
   appear as long-s + round-s, or two long-s forms.

6. 'ff' FOR CAPITAL F:
   The scribe may use a double-f ("ff") at the start of
   words where a capital F is intended. This is a common
   early modern convention.

7. MACRON / TILDE OVER VOWELS:
   A horizontal line over a vowel (especially 'o' or 'a')
   may indicate a following nasal consonant has been omitted
   (e.g., line over 'o' = "on" or "om").

=========================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (Highest to Lowest)
=========================================================

CRITICAL RISK (most likely to cause transcription errors):
1.  i / n / m / u / w — MINIM CONFUSION
    The single biggest source of error. These letters are
    all composed of identical minim strokes. Without dots
    on 'i' (often missing) and without context, sequences
    like "iiii" could be m+i, n+i, i+n, u+i, etc.
    Strategy: Use word context and spelling knowledge to
    decode minim sequences.

2.  long-s / f
    Nearly identical vertical strokes descending below
    baseline. The ONLY reliable distinction is f's crossbar,
    which is sometimes faint or absent.
    Strategy: Check for crossbar. Use word context (does
    the word make sense with 's' or 'f'?).

3.  secretary-d / cl or el
    The looped secretary 'd' looks nothing like a modern 'd'
    to untrained readers. The leftward loop mimics 'c' or 'e'
    followed by 'l'.
    Strategy: Learn to recognize the secretary 'd' pattern.
    If you see what looks like 'cl' in an unexpected position,
    consider whether it is actually 'd'.

HIGH RISK:
4.  c / e
    Small round letters that differ only by 'e's crossbar,
    which may not always be visible.
    Strategy: Look for the horizontal crossbar in 'e'.

5.  a / o
    Both round baseline letters. When 'a' closes fully, it
    looks like 'o'; when 'o' has a slight opening, it
    resembles 'a'.
    Strategy: Look for the entry stroke on 'a'.

6.  u / v
    Used somewhat interchangeably in early modern spelling.
    'v' tends to be more angular, 'u' more curved, but in
    this hand the distinction blurs.
    Strategy: Positional clue — 'v' tends to appear word-
    initially, 'u' medially.

MODERATE RISK:
7.  t / l
    Both are vertical strokes; 't' is shorter with a crossbar,
    'l' is taller without. But when 't's crossbar is faint or
    'l' is short, they converge.
    Strategy: Compare height to established ascender letters.

8.  r / n
    Short italic 'r' (shoulder + downstroke) can look like
    the second half of 'n'.
    Strategy: Look for the distinctive shoulder tick on 'r'.

9.  b / l / h
    All have tall ascenders. Distinguished by bowl (b),
    shoulder (h), or nothing (l) at the baseline, but these
    features can be subtle.
    Strategy: Examine carefully what happens at the baseline.

10. long-s / l
    Both tall vertical strokes. Long 's' descends below
    baseline, 'l' does not, but this can be hard to judge.
    Strategy: Check for descender below baseline.

LOW RISK:
11. p / q
    Mirror images; 'q' always followed by 'u'.
    Strategy: Look at what follows.

12. y / v
    Distinguished by y's descending tail.
    Strategy: Check for descender.

13. g / q / y
    All have descenders but different bowl/body shapes.
    Strategy: Examine the body above the baseline.

14. k / h
    Both have ascenders with strokes at baseline. K has
    diagonals, h has a smooth shoulder.
    Strategy: Look for angular vs curved junction.

MINIMAL RISK:
15. x, z, j — Rare letters, distinctive shapes.
16. Capital letters — Generally large enough to read clearly.

=========================================================
TRANSCRIPTION STRATEGY NOTES
=========================================================

When confronting this hand, a transcriber should:

1. ANCHOR FIRST: Find "Take," "the," "of," "and," "water,"
   "for," "good," "your," "put" — these are near-certain
   in recipe texts and establish most common letterforms.

2. DECODE MINIMS BY CONTEXT: Never try to count individual
   minims in isolation. Read the whole word shape and use
   vocabulary knowledge to resolve ambiguity.

3. WATCH FOR LONG-s vs f: Every time you see a tall
   descending stroke, actively check for the crossbar
   before deciding.

4. LEARN THE SECRETARY-d: This is the single most
   disorienting letterform for modern readers. Once
   recognized, it is actually quite consistent.

5. CHECK ABBREVIATIONS: Superscript letters and macrons
   are easy to miss but change meaning significantly.
