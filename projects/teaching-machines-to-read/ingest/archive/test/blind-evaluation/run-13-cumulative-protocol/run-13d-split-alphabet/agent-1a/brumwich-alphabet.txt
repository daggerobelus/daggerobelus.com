BRUMWICH MS160 — PAGE 10 LETTERFORM ALPHABET CHART
====================================================
Source: brumwich-ms160-page10.jpg (two-page spread, pages 6-7)
Hand: Secretary/mixed hand, likely late 16th or early 17th century
Ink: Brown/iron gall ink, consistent darkness throughout
General character: Moderately cursive, fairly consistent hand,
  moderate slant to the right. The scribe writes quickly but legibly.
  Letter connections are frequent. Ascenders and descenders are
  moderately long.

ANCHOR WORDS IDENTIFIED
========================
- "Take" — appears multiple times as recipe openings (left and right pages)
- "the" — extremely frequent throughout
- "and" — frequent, sometimes abbreviated as "&"
- "of" — frequent throughout
- "water" / "A water" — appears in recipe titles (top of left page)
- "for" — appears in recipe headings
- "good" — appears several times
- "put" — appears in instructions
- "it" — frequent
- "in" — frequent
- "with" — appears in instructions
- "green" — appears on left page
- "sugar" — appears on left page
- "boile" / "boyle" — appears in cooking instructions
- "powder" — appears in instructions
- "receipt" / "recipe" — appears on right page
- "a" (article) — frequent


LOWERCASE ALPHABET
==================

a
  Formation: Small rounded letter, formed with a single curved stroke
    closing at the top, sometimes with a slight tail to the right for
    connecting to the next letter. Generally a single-compartment 'a'
    (not the two-compartment printed form).
  Example words: "Take" (second letter), "and", "water", "a" (article)
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'o' when hastily written and not fully
    closed; can resemble 'u' when the top is left open.

b
  Formation: Tall ascender stroke going up, then a loop coming down and
    curving right to form the bowl. The ascender has moderate height.
    The bowl is rounded.
  Example words: "boile"/"boyle", "be", "but"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'l' if the bowl is small or
    unclear. The ascender loop can resemble a 'k' in some formations.

c
  Formation: Simple curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern 'c'.
    Fairly consistent and usually clear.
  Example words: "receipt", "clean"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'e' when the scribe adds a connecting
    stroke from the middle. Sometimes hard to distinguish from 't'
    in certain ligatures.

d
  Formation: Secretary 'd' — formed with a looped ascender that curves
    back to the LEFT (characteristic secretary form). The bowl sits
    at the baseline and the ascender loops backward over it. This is
    NOT the modern 'd' shape.
  Example words: "and", "good", "powder"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — the looped-back ascender can make 'd' look
    like 'e' or even 'cl' to untrained readers. This is one of the
    most distinctive secretary-hand features.

e
  Formation: Small letter formed with a curved stroke, often resembling
    a reversed '3' or a simple loop. In this hand, 'e' is typically
    a small rounded mark that sits on the baseline. Sometimes has a
    slight upward flick for the connecting stroke.
  Example words: "the", "Take", "green", "be"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'o' in quick writing. The secretary 'e'
    can also look like a small 'c' or even an 'i' without a dot.

f
  Formation: Descends below the baseline with a long stroke downward.
    Has a crossbar at the midline. In this hand, 'f' often has a
    looped descender and the crossbar may merge with connecting
    strokes to adjacent letters.
  Example words: "for", "of"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — can be confused with long 's' (see 's'
    entry below). In secretary hand, 'f' and long 's' are extremely
    similar; 'f' typically has a crossbar on BOTH sides while long 's'
    has a crossbar only on the LEFT or no crossbar at all.

g
  Formation: Formed with a rounded bowl at the baseline and a descender
    that loops back up, sometimes curving to the left. The descender
    is moderate in length.
  Example words: "good", "green", "sugar"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'q' or 'y' depending on how the
    descender is formed. The bowl can look like 'a' or 'o'.

h
  Formation: Tall ascender stroke, then a curved stroke coming down
    from the top to form the shoulder. The right leg comes down to
    the baseline and may curve slightly. In this hand, 'h' is
    generally clear and well-formed.
  Example words: "the" (when written as full word), "this", "have"
  Confusion risk: Can occasionally resemble 'b' if the shoulder is
    very rounded, or 'k' if the stroke is angular.

i
  Formation: A short minim (vertical stroke) with a dot above. In this
    hand, the dot is sometimes omitted or displaced, making 'i'
    indistinguishable from the strokes of 'm', 'n', 'u'.
  Example words: "it", "in", "with"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — without the dot, 'i' is identical to
    a single minim of 'n', 'm', or 'u'. This is the classic minim
    confusion problem of secretary hand. (See confusion ranking below.)

k
  Formation: Tall ascender with two diagonal strokes meeting at the
    midline — one coming down from the ascender and one kicking up.
    Sometimes the upper diagonal has a loop.
  Example words: "Take" (initial letter uses capital, but lowercase
    appears in other contexts)
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'b' or 'h' in hasty writing.

l
  Formation: Simple tall ascender stroke, usually straight or with a
    very slight curve. May have a small foot serif at the baseline.
    Sometimes has a slight loop at the top.
  Example words: "boile", "clean"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'b' (if a loop at top creates
    an apparent bowl), or with tall 'e' strokes. In combinations
    like 'll', can be mistaken for other letter pairs.

m
  Formation: Three minims (vertical strokes) connected at the top.
    The connecting arches are rounded. Sits entirely on the baseline.
  Example words: "make", "them"
  Confusion risk: EXTREMELY HIGH — minim confusion. Three minims could
    be read as 'in', 'ni', 'mi', 'im', 'iu', 'ui', etc. This is
    the single greatest source of error in secretary hand.

n
  Formation: Two minims connected at the top with a rounded arch.
    Identical in stroke to individual minims of 'm', 'i', 'u'.
  Example words: "and", "in", "green"
  Confusion risk: EXTREMELY HIGH — same minim problem as 'm' and 'i'.
    Two minims could be 'n', 'u', 'ii', etc.

o
  Formation: Rounded letter, formed as a simple closed oval or circle.
    Usually sits on the baseline. May have a connecting stroke
    trailing to the right.
  Example words: "of", "good", "boile"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'a' when the 'a' is very rounded and
    closed. Occasionally confused with 'e' in very cursive passages.

p
  Formation: Descender goes below the baseline. The bowl is at the
    midline, formed by a curved stroke to the right. The descender
    is straight or slightly curved.
  Example words: "powder", "put", "upon"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'q' if the bowl curves the wrong way
    in reading. Generally fairly clear in this hand.

q
  Formation: Small bowl at the baseline with a descender, followed by
    a tail curving to the right (usually followed by 'u'). The bowl
    resembles 'a' or 'o'.
  Example words: "quantity"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'g' or 'p'. Usually disambiguated by
    context (almost always followed by 'u').

r
  Formation: This scribe uses TWO forms of 'r':
    (1) Long 'r': a short stroke with a shoulder, similar to modern 'r'
    (2) Round 'r' (2-shaped r): appears after round letters like 'o',
        resembling a '2' or 'z' shape
  Example words: "green", "water", "for", "receipt"
  Confusion risk: Round 'r' can be confused with 'v', 'z', or 'x'.
    Long 'r' can resemble 'n' or part of an 'm' (minim confusion).

s
  Formation: TWO forms used:
    (1) Long 's': tall stroke similar to 'f', descending slightly or
        sitting at x-height with an ascender. Crossbar on LEFT side
        only (distinguishing it from 'f'). Used at the beginning and
        middle of words.
    (2) Short/round 's': resembles modern 's' or figure '8'. Used at
        the END of words.
  Example words: "sugar" (initial long s), "this" (final round s)
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — long 's' vs 'f' is one of the most
    dangerous confusions in secretary hand. Must look carefully at
    the crossbar.

t
  Formation: Short vertical stroke (shorter than ascenders like 'l', 'b',
    'h') with a crossbar. The crossbar often extends and connects to
    the next letter. In this hand, 't' is generally shorter than 'l'.
  Example words: "Take", "the", "put", "it"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'c' or 'e' when the crossbar merges
    with connecting strokes. Height distinction from 'l' can be
    ambiguous.

u
  Formation: Two minims connected at the BOTTOM with a curve, the
    opposite of 'n'. Sits on the baseline. In this period, 'u' and
    'v' are sometimes interchangeable.
  Example words: "sugar", "put", "upon"
  Confusion risk: EXTREMELY HIGH — minim confusion with 'n', 'i', 'm'.
    Two minims connected at bottom = 'u'; connected at top = 'n';
    but in practice the connections are ambiguous.

v
  Formation: Often used interchangeably with 'u' at the start of words
    (e.g., "vpon" for "upon"). Formed with a pointed bottom and two
    strokes diverging upward. Sometimes resembles round 'r'.
  Example words: "very", possibly "vpon"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u', round 'r', or 'b' in certain
    formations.

w
  Formation: Formed as a double-'v' shape or with connected curved
    strokes. Generally wider than other letters. Usually clear
    because of its distinctive width.
  Example words: "water", "with"
  Confusion risk: Relatively low. Sometimes the first stroke can
    look like a separate 'v', making 'w' appear as 'vu' or 'uv'.

x
  Formation: Two crossing diagonal strokes. Relatively rare in English
    text. When it appears, usually clear.
  Example words: rare on this page
  Confusion risk: Can resemble round 'r' or 'z' in hasty writing.

y
  Formation: Formed with two strokes meeting above the baseline and a
    descender going down and usually curving to the left. The
    descender distinguishes it from 'v'.
  Example words: "boyle", "they"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'g' (both have descenders), or 'v'
    if the descender is very short. The top can look like 'i' + 'i'.

z
  Formation: Rare. When present, formed with a zigzag stroke similar
    to modern 'z'. May resemble round 'r'.
  Example words: rare on this page
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with round 'r' (2-shaped r).


CAPITAL LETTERS
===============

A
  Formation: Two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak, sometimes with a
    crossbar. In this hand, capital 'A' is often elaborate with an
    initial swash or loop on the left stroke. Clearly larger than
    surrounding letters.
  Example words: "A water" (recipe title)
  Confusion risk: Generally clear due to size and position.

F
  Formation: Large version of lowercase 'f' with more elaborate
    flourishing. The crossbar is pronounced.
  Example words: "For" (recipe headings)
  Confusion risk: Can resemble capital 'T' if the crossbar dominates.

T
  Formation: Large sweeping crossbar at the top with a vertical stroke
    descending. Often the crossbar has a decorative curve or swash.
    Very prominent at recipe openings.
  Example words: "Take" (recipe openings — very frequent)
  Confusion risk: The crossbar can sometimes make it resemble 'F' or
    'J'. Generally clear from context (always starts "Take").

I/J
  Formation: In this period, 'I' and 'J' are often the same letter.
    Formed as a tall stroke, sometimes with a swash at top or bottom.
  Example words: contextual
  Confusion risk: 'I' and 'J' interchangeable in this period.

S
  Formation: Capital 'S' is typically the round form, larger and more
    elaborate than the lowercase round 's'. May have decorative
    strokes.
  Example words: contextual
  Confusion risk: Generally clear due to size.

Other capitals appear but are less frequent on this page. The scribe
generally signals recipe openings with larger initial letters.


ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL FEATURES
====================================

1. THORN (þ) for "th": The scribe may use a character resembling 'y'
   with a superscript 'e' or 't' to represent "the", "that", "them".
   This looks like "ye" but is actually "the". Watch for superscript
   letters as abbreviation markers.

2. SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS: Letters written above the line often signal
   abbreviation. Common examples:
   - 'wth' with superscript 'th' = "with"
   - 'yor' with superscript 'r' = "your"
   - 'wch' with superscript 'ch' = "which"

3. AMPERSAND / TAILED ABBREVIATION: "&" or a character resembling it
   used for "and"

4. LONG S (ſ): Used in initial and medial positions, round 's' only
   at the end of words. This is standard for the period.

5. MACRONS / TILDES: A horizontal line above a vowel may indicate a
   missing 'm' or 'n' (e.g., a tilde over 'o' = 'om' or 'on').

6. SPELLING VARIATION: Expect early modern spelling (e.g., "boile"
   for "boil", "receit" for "receipt", "pouder" for "powder",
   "vpon" for "upon"). These are NOT errors — they are period-
   appropriate spellings.

7. RECIPE STRUCTURE: Recipes typically begin with "Take" and use
   imperative verbs. Titles may be offset or written in slightly
   larger hand.


CONFUSION RISK RANKING
=======================
(Ranked from MOST dangerous to LEAST dangerous)

CRITICAL RISK — Will cause frequent errors
-------------------------------------------

1. MINIM CONFUSION: i / n / m / u
   THE single biggest source of transcription error. These letters
   are formed from identical vertical strokes (minims). The only
   distinction is how they connect at top or bottom, and whether
   'i' has a dot. In sequences like "minim" itself, it is nearly
   impossible to distinguish individual letters without context.
   Words like "him", "inn", "nun", "minimum" become strings of
   nearly identical strokes.
   STRATEGY: Use word context and spelling knowledge to resolve.
   Count minims carefully and try all possible readings.

2. LONG S (ſ) vs. F (f)
   Both have tall vertical strokes and crossbars. The theoretical
   distinction (f has crossbar on both sides, long s only on left)
   is often unclear in practice. This WILL cause errors.
   STRATEGY: Consider whether 'f' or 's' makes sense in context.
   Check for double-s formations.

HIGH RISK — Will cause regular errors
--------------------------------------

3. SECRETARY D vs. other letters (e, cl)
   The backward-looping ascender of secretary 'd' is unfamiliar to
   modern readers and can be misread as 'e', 'cl', or 'a' + ascender.
   STRATEGY: Learn to recognize the distinctive backward loop. If
   you see a loop going LEFT over a small bowl, it is almost
   certainly 'd'.

4. C vs. E vs. T (in cursive connections)
   When these letters flow into connecting strokes, their forms
   can merge and become ambiguous.
   STRATEGY: Look at the overall word shape and context.

5. ROUND R (2-shaped) vs. V vs. Z
   The round 'r' after curved letters can be mistaken for 'v' or 'z'.
   STRATEGY: Round 'r' almost always follows 'o', 'p', 'b', 'd' —
   if you see a '2'-like shape after a round letter, it is likely 'r'.

MODERATE RISK — Occasional errors
-----------------------------------

6. A vs. O (when 'a' is very rounded)
   In quick writing, the distinguishing feature (the slight opening
   or tail of 'a') may be absent.
   STRATEGY: Context usually resolves this.

7. Y vs. G (both have descenders)
   The bowls are different but descenders can look similar.
   STRATEGY: 'g' has a rounder, more closed bowl; 'y' has a more
   open, v-shaped top.

8. H vs. B vs. K (ascender letters)
   The distinctive features (bowl for b, shoulder for h, diagonals
   for k) can be unclear in hasty writing.
   STRATEGY: Look at the stroke coming off the ascender — curved
   bowl = b, shoulder arch = h, angular kick = k.

9. R (long form) vs. N (minim confusion variant)
   Long 'r' has a shoulder that can resemble the arch of 'n'.
   STRATEGY: 'r' shoulder is usually shorter and more angular than
   'n' arch.

LOWER RISK — Infrequent errors
--------------------------------

10. W being read as VV or UU
    Generally 'w' is wide enough to be recognized, but can split
    visually.

11. P vs. Q (different bowl directions)
    Usually clear from context and from which side the bowl is on.

12. L vs. B (if bowl is faint)
    Usually distinguishable by looking carefully for the bowl.


TRANSCRIPTION STRATEGY NOTES
==============================

For the transcription agent who will use this chart:

1. MINIMS FIRST: In any ambiguous word, count the minims and consider
   ALL possible letter combinations before committing to a reading.

2. CONTEXT IS KING: When letterforms are ambiguous, rely on:
   - Common recipe vocabulary (take, boil, put, strain, etc.)
   - English word patterns (consonant clusters, common endings)
   - The sense of the recipe instruction

3. LONG S vs F: Default to 's' unless 'f' clearly makes a real word.
   Long 's' is far more common than 'f' in running text.

4. SECRETARY D: If you see a backward loop over a bowl at the baseline,
   read it as 'd'. Do not read it as 'e' or 'cl'.

5. ABBREVIATIONS: Watch for superscript letters — they signal
   contracted words (wth = with, yor = your, wch = which, ye = the).

6. ROUND R: After any round letter (o, b, p, d), a '2'-shaped mark
   is almost certainly 'r', not 'v' or 'z'.
