SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO EARLY MODERN SECRETARY HAND
Based on study of Henslow MS688 (pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)

================================================================
LETTERFORM IDENTIFICATION
================================================================

LOWERCASE LETTERS:

a — Two forms observed:
  1. Secretary "a": a rounded form with a small curved stroke on top, resembling a backwards "e" with a closed bowl. Seen consistently in "and", "Take", "water".
  2. Sometimes appears more angular, with a pointed top.

b — Tall ascender with a closed bowl at the baseline. The ascender loops slightly to the left. Seen in "bloude", "boyle", "bee".

c — A simple curved stroke, open on the right. Very similar to modern "c" but sometimes has a slight hook at the top. Seen in "colde", "come", "cann".

d — Secretary "d": the ascender curves backwards over the letter to the left, creating a looping shape. This is very distinctive — the ascender goes up and curves left/back. Seen in "and", "dothe", "dried". NOT like modern "d".

e — Secretary "e": looks like a reversed "3" or a backwards epsilon. A small rounded form, often with two small curves. Seen throughout in "the", "thee", "eache". This is the most common letterform.

f — Has a descender below the baseline AND an ascender above. Often crossed with a horizontal stroke. The double-f (ff) is very common at word beginnings: "ffasten", "fier", "fine", "figges". The ff ligature looks like two tall strokes side by side with a cross-bar.

g — Has a distinctive descender that loops below and back up. The bowl is open or closed at top. Seen in "gargole", "goode", "greace".

h — Tall ascender, similar to modern "h" but the second stroke sometimes curves more. Seen in "his", "her", "halfe", "handefull".

i — A simple minim stroke (short vertical line) with a dot or tick above. Easy to confuse with strokes in m, n, u. Seen in "in", "itt", "is".

k — Tall ascender with a distinctive looped lower portion. Seen in "keepe", "killed", "kinges".

l — Tall ascender, simple vertical stroke, sometimes with a small foot serif. Seen in "lett", "longes", "like".

m — Three minim strokes (three short vertical lines connected). Can be confused with "ni", "iu", "nn". Seen in "mallowes", "make", "muche".

n — Two minim strokes. Can be confused with "u" or parts of "m". Seen in "and", "nine", "narrowe".

o — Rounded letter, similar to modern "o" but sometimes slightly angular. Seen in "of", "on", "or", "one".

p — Has a descender below the baseline. The bowl is at the top. Seen in "putt", "powder", "potte".

q — Has a descender, rare letter. Seen in "quantitye", "Quinsie", "quarte".

r — Two forms:
  1. Short "r": a small angular stroke after round letters (o, e). Looks like a small "2" shape.
  2. Long "r": a descending stroke. More standard form used elsewhere.
  Seen in "rootes", "runninge", "roses".

s — Three forms:
  1. Long "s": looks like "f" but WITHOUT a cross-bar. Tall, extends above the line. Used at the beginning and middle of words: "seethe", "straine", "spoonefulls".
  2. Short/round "s": used at the end of words, looks like modern "s". Seen in "rootes", "ounces", "tymes".
  3. In "ss" combinations, often one long-s followed by a short-s.

t — Short ascender with a cross-bar. The cross-bar sometimes extends to connect to the next letter. Seen in "the", "to", "togeather". Often has a slight curve.

u/v — These are variant forms of the same letter in this period:
  "v" is used at the start of words: "verie", "vntill", "vinneger"
  "u" is used in the middle: "putt", "muche", "use"
  Both look like modern "u" or "v" depending on position.

w — A distinctive wide letter made of two "v" shapes joined. Seen in "water", "withe", "wine", "white".

x — Rare. Crossed strokes similar to modern "x". Seen in "waxe".

y — Has a descender that curves below the baseline to the left. The top is like a "v" shape. Seen in "yow", "ytt", "youre". Note: "ytt" = "it" (common spelling).

z — Very rare in these samples.

UPPERCASE LETTERS:

A — An enlarged version of lowercase with more flourish. Seen in "A", "Archangell", "And", "Applye".

B — Large rounded letter with a vertical stroke and two bowls. More ornate than lowercase. Seen in "Butt", "Boyle", "Bowlarmene".

C — Large curved stroke, sometimes with a decorative flourish. Seen in "Cinkfoud".

D — Large letter with backward-curving ascender (like lowercase but larger). Seen in "Decayed", "Docke".

F — Large double-stroke letter. "For" appears frequently and the F is very ornate with sweeping strokes. "Firste" shows the same.

G — Large with a descending loop. "Gargisine" shows elaborate form.

H — Tall with two vertical strokes. Seen in "Halle".

L — Tall vertical stroke with a foot. Seen in "Laye", "Liver", "Longes".

M — Wide letter with three or four strokes. "Make" and "Marye" show this.

Q — Very ornate with a long descending tail. "Quinsie" and "Queene" show elaborate forms.

R — Seen in "Redd", "Reasons".

S — Ornate with sweeping curves. Seen in "Sunne", "Sunn".

T — Cross-stroke at top with vertical stroke. "Take" appears at the start of most recipes — the T is large and decorative. "To" and "The" and "This" also show this form.

Y — "Yow" shows uppercase Y with a descending stroke.

================================================================
SPELLING CONVENTIONS
================================================================

1. DOUBLE LETTERS are extremely common and must be preserved:
   - ff at word start: "ffasten" (fasten)
   - tt: "putt", "ytt", "itt", "att", "sett", "hott", "nott"
   - ee: "teethe", "keepe", "sweete", "bee", "three", "thee"
   - ll: "wille", "untille", "stille", "all", "shall"
   - dd: "sodde", "Redd", "boddye"
   - oo: "toothe", "togeather" (sometimes), "goode"
   - pp: "puppe", "copperis", "toppe"
   - rr: "sugarr", "coorrance"
   - ss: "blossomes"

2. TERMINAL -e is added to many words: "keepe", "straine", "putte", "sweete", "colde", "warme", "fier" sometimes appears without.

3. "ytt" = "it" — extremely consistent throughout all pages.

4. "withe" = "with" — consistent terminal -e.

5. "yow" = "you" — consistent spelling.

6. "youre" = "your"

7. "inne" or "in" — both appear.

8. "att" = "at"

9. "lett" = "let"

10. "putt" = "put"

11. "sett" = "set"

12. "bee" = "be"

13. "owte" = "out" — seen in "withowt" (without), "owte" (out)

14. "i/y" interchange: "fier" (fire), "fyer" (fire) — both appear.

15. "-inge" ending: "runninge", "stoppinge", "swellinge", "moreninge"

16. "eache" = "each"

17. "muche" = "much"

18. "ounce" / "ounces" — standard spelling maintained.

19. "anie" / "annye" = "any"

20. "verie" = "very"

21. "togeather" = "together" — consistent.

================================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL MARKS
================================================================

1. Superscript letters are sometimes used for abbreviations.
2. "probatum" appears at the end of some recipes (Latin: "it has been proved/tested").
3. The tilde (~) or macron over vowels can indicate a missing "n" or "m".
4. Colons and periods are used inconsistently as punctuation.

================================================================
DOCUMENT STRUCTURE (RECIPE FORMAT)
================================================================

1. Each recipe begins with a TITLE in larger/more ornate script, often centered or set apart.
2. Titles follow the pattern: "For [ailment]" or "To [action]" or "A [thing] for [purpose]"
3. Recipes begin with "Take" (sometimes with a large ornate T).
4. Instructions use connectives: "and", "then", "withe", "untill".
5. Quantities specified: "a handefull", "a spoonefull", "a quarte", "a pinte", "an ounce".
6. "probatum" sometimes appears at end to mark tested recipes.

================================================================
CRITICAL READING STRATEGIES
================================================================

1. MINIM CONFUSION: Letters made of short vertical strokes (i, m, n, u) are the hardest to distinguish. Count minims carefully: m=3, n=2, u=2, i=1. Context helps.

2. LONG-s vs f: Long-s has NO cross-bar; f HAS a cross-bar. Both are tall. This is the most common confusion point.

3. Secretary d: The backward-looping ascender is distinctive. Don't mistake it for an "a" or "e" combination.

4. Secretary e: The reversed-3 shape is unlike modern "e". Don't mistake it for "o" or "c".

5. c vs t: In this hand, "c" and "t" can look similar. Look for the cross-bar on "t".

6. READ WORD-LEVEL FIRST: Identify common words you recognize (the, and, of, in, to, for, withe, ytt, putt, take) to calibrate your reading of the hand.

7. RECIPE VOCABULARY: These are medical/herbal recipes. Expect plant names, body parts, measurements, cooking verbs (boyle, seethe, straine, stampe, beate).

8. PRESERVE ORIGINAL SPELLING: Never modernize. If it says "ffasten" write "ffasten". If it says "ytt" write "ytt".
