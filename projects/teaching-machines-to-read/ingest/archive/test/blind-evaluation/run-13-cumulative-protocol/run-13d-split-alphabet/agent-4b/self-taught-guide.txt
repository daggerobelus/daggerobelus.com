SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Learned from Henslow MS688 (pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)
===========================================================

GENERAL HAND CHARACTERISTICS
- This is a late 16th / early 17th century English secretary hand
- The hand is relatively consistent and careful, likely a literate household scribe
- Text is recipe/medical remedy content, which provides contextual clues
- Headings use larger, more decorative letterforms; body text is smaller secretary
- The scribe uses extensive doubled consonants and early modern spelling conventions

ALPHABET — LOWERCASE LETTERFORMS

a — Two forms: (1) secretary 'a' resembling a small 2 with a curved back, open at top; (2) in headings, a more rounded italic 'a'. Easily confused with 'o' when hastily formed.

b — Tall ascender with a closed lower bowl. The ascender curves slightly to the right at top. Distinguished from 'l' by the bowl at baseline.

c — Small curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern 'c' but sometimes with a slight hairline entry stroke from above.

d — Secretary 'd': the ascender loops back to the LEFT, curving over the bowl in an arc. This is the most distinctive secretary letterform. Do not confuse with 'cl' — the loop is a single stroke.

e — Small form, often resembles a reversed '3' or a small circle with a tongue extending right. In ligature, often just a small loop. Can look like 'o' when closed.

f — Descends below the baseline. Has a crossbar at or near the baseline. The descender curves left. Often doubled ('ff') at word beginnings where modern English uses capital F.

g — Flat-topped loop above baseline with a descending tail curving left, then sometimes back right. The top is often a horizontal stroke.

h — Minim stroke with ascender; the second stroke curves down and right. In combinations like 'th' the crossbar of t connects to h.

i — Single minim stroke, sometimes dotted, sometimes not. Can be indistinguishable from the strokes of m, n, u without context.

k — Ascender with two angled strokes at baseline forming a pointed junction. Appears in words like 'take' and 'keepe'.

l — Tall single stroke with slight curve at top. Distinguished from 'b' by lacking a bowl. Can be confused with tall 'e' forms.

m — Three minim strokes. Must be distinguished from 'ni', 'iu', 'in' by counting strokes carefully and using word context.

n — Two minim strokes. Same caution as 'm' — context is essential.

o — Small round form, usually closed. Can look like 'a' or 'e' in hurried writing.

p — Descender below baseline with a closed bowl at x-height. The descender drops straight down or curves slightly left.

q — Appears rarely. Similar to 'p' but the descender curves right. Usually followed by 'u'.

r — Two common forms: (1) standard 'r' with a shoulder stroke; (2) round 'r' (resembling a '2') after 'o' and rounded letters. The round 'r' is frequent in this hand.

s — Three forms: (1) long 's' resembling 'f' but WITHOUT a crossbar — used at the beginning and middle of words; (2) short/round 's' at the end of words; (3) in combination 'ss' one may be long and one short. Distinguishing long 's' from 'f' is critical: look for the crossbar.

t — Short stroke with crossbar. The crossbar often extends right into the next letter. Much shorter than 'l'.

u/v — In this period, 'u' and 'v' are positional variants: 'v' at the start of words (even for vowel sounds: 'vntill', 'vppon'), 'u' in the middle. The forms are similar minims.

w — Formed by two 'v' shapes joined, sometimes with a connecting stroke at top. Quite wide.

x — Rare. Two crossing strokes.

y — Descending stroke below baseline with a tail curving left. The 'y' is used in 'ytt' (meaning 'it'), 'yow' (meaning 'you'), and 'youre'.

z — Very rare in this hand.

ALPHABET — UPPERCASE / MAJUSCULE LETTERFORMS

A — Large decorative form, often starting with a bold downstroke. Used in headings like "A watter" and "A plaister".

B — Large rounded form with two bowls. Appears in "Butt", "Boyle".

F — Often written as 'ff' even at word starts (lowercase doubled f serves as capital). When true capital F appears, it has elaborate flourishes.

Q — Large ornate letter with sweeping tail. Seen in "Quinsie", "Queene".

T — Large form with crossbar, often decorated. In headings: "Take" and "To Make" use prominent T.

Y — Capital Y with descending tail used in "Yow".

ABBREVIATIONS AND MARKS

- Tilde/macron over vowels can indicate missing 'n' or 'm'
- Period or colon may appear as section dividers, not grammatical punctuation
- 'wt' with a superscript mark = 'with' (but this scribe usually writes it out)
- Square brackets in transcription indicate damaged/unclear text: [eth], [ounces]

SPELLING CONVENTIONS

- Double consonants are very common: 'putt', 'lett', 'butt', 'hott', 'cutt', 'gett'
- 'ee' for long e: 'teethe', 'keepe', 'seethe', 'sweete', 'bee'
- 'ytt' = it; 'yow' = you; 'youre' = your
- 'withe' = with; 'owte' = out; 'withowt' = without
- '-inge' ending: 'runninge', 'stoppinge', 'swellinge'
- 'e' added to many word endings: 'putte', 'roote', 'mouthe'
- 'eache' = each; 'muche' = much
- 'ff' at word start = F: 'ffasten', 'fflower', 'ffoure'
- 'v' used for 'u' sound at word start: 'vntill', 'vppon'
- 'u' used for 'v' sound mid-word: 'euerie' = every, 'euelle' = evil
- 'probatum' = Latin tag meaning "it has been proved" — appears at end of proven remedies

COMMON WORDS AND THEIR FORMS

Take — The most frequent word, always at recipe start. Large 'T' with distinctive 'ake'.
and — Very frequent conjunction; the 'a' is often a quick stroke.
the — Common; 'th' ligature is reliable.
of — Small word; the round 'f' descends.
in — Two minims; context determines reading.
to — Short word with crossbar on 't'.
itt / ytt — 'it' spelled with doubled terminal 't'.
withe — 'with' spelled with terminal 'e'.
them / then — Distinguished by final stroke (m = three minims, n = two).
togeather — 'together' in this spelling.
thereof — compound; common in recipes.
handefull — 'handful' with medial 'e' and doubled 'l'.
spoonefull — similarly formed.
water / watter — sometimes single, sometimes doubled 't'.

CRITICAL DISTINCTIONS

1. Long 's' vs 'f': Long s has NO crossbar. If you see a crossbar, it is 'f'. Both descend below baseline.
2. Secretary 'd' vs 'cl': The looping ascender of 'd' curves left in one stroke. 'cl' would show two distinct vertical strokes.
3. 'c' vs 't': Both are short, but 't' has a crossbar.
4. 'u' vs 'n': Both are two minims. 'u' curves at bottom, 'n' has arches at top — but in secretary hand these are very similar. Use context.
5. 'e' vs 'o': Both can appear as small round forms. 'e' typically has a tongue or tail to the right.
6. Minim confusion (m/n/i/u): Count strokes carefully. 'mm' = six minims, 'nn' = four, 'mn' = five. Context is the only reliable guide.

READING STRATEGY

1. Start with headings — they are larger and clearer
2. Identify 'Take' at recipe starts as an anchor word
3. Look for common recipe words: 'boyle', 'seethe', 'straine', 'putt'
4. Use ingredient/body part vocabulary as context: 'rootes', 'water', 'throte'
5. When stuck on a word, identify individual letterforms bottom-up: pen strokes first, then letters, then the word
6. Check doubled consonants — this scribe doubles them frequently
7. Terminal '-e' is almost always present
8. Latin tags ('probatum') signal recipe endings
