LETTERFORM ALPHABET CHART
Brumwich MS 160, Page 10 (both sides of opening)
================================================================

METHODOLOGY
-----------
Anchor words used to establish letterforms:
- Recipe markers: "Take", "A water", "For", "of"
- High-frequency words: "the", "and", "of", "in", "with", "to", "it"
- Visible recipe titles and headings on both pages
- The hand is a mixed secretary/italic hand, typical of 17th-century English domestic manuscripts
- The scribe writes fairly quickly with moderate regularity; some letters shift form depending on position in word

================================================================
LOWERCASE LETTERS
================================================================

a
  Form: Open-topped secretary 'a', formed with a curved stroke on the left and a short vertical stroke on the right. Sometimes appears nearly as a 'u' when hastily written. Occasionally uses a rounder italic 'a' in certain positions.
  Examples: "and", "water", "a", "Take"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' or 'o' when written quickly; the open top is not always distinct.

b
  Form: Tall ascender with a looped top curving to the right, then a rounded bowl at the baseline. The ascender loop is fairly pronounced.
  Examples: "be", "before", "broad"
  Confusion risk: Ascender loop can resemble 'l' if bowl is cramped. Generally distinct.

c
  Form: Simple curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern 'c'. Relatively small and compact.
  Examples: "can", "colour", "receipt"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'e' when the scribe's 'e' is open, or with 'r' in tight spacing.

d
  Form: Secretary 'd' — the ascender loops back to the LEFT (characteristic secretary form), creating a curved stroke that returns to close the bowl. This is one of the most distinctive letters in this hand.
  Examples: "and", "good", "dried"
  Confusion risk: The leftward-looping ascender can look like 'e' + 'l' to unfamiliar readers. May also be confused with 'cl' combination.

e
  Form: Small, often formed with a curved stroke. Sometimes appears as a simple loop or a small backward 'c' shape. In final position, may trail off.
  Examples: "the", "Take", "water", "before"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — can resemble 'c', 'o', or even 'i' when very small. One of the trickiest letters in this hand.

f
  Form: Descends below the baseline with a long descender. Has a crossbar at or near the baseline. The top curves to the right. Resembles the secretary 'f' with a descender and crossbar.
  Examples: "for", "of", "fresh"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with long 's' (which also descends). Context usually disambiguates ("of" vs. "os").

g
  Form: A round or oval upper bowl with a descending tail that curves left or hooks beneath. The descender is moderately long.
  Examples: "good", "great", "sugar"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'q' if the tail direction is ambiguous. The bowl can look like 'o' if tail is faint.

h
  Form: Tall ascender (shorter loop than 'b'), with the second stroke curving down to the right at the baseline. The minim on the right side sometimes extends below the baseline slightly.
  Examples: "the", "this", "have"
  Confusion risk: Can look like 'li' or 'b' if written quickly. The rightward curve at the bottom helps distinguish it.

i
  Form: Single minim (short vertical stroke) with a dot above, though the dot is not always present or may drift. In this hand, the dot is inconsistent.
  Examples: "in", "it", "with"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — without its dot, indistinguishable from a single stroke of 'm', 'n', 'u', or 'w'. Minim confusion is the biggest challenge in this hand.

j
  Form: Rarely appears independently. When used, it is an 'i' with a descender curving left. May not be distinguished from 'i' in all contexts (j/i were not yet fully standardized).
  Examples: Rare in this text
  Confusion risk: Merged with 'i' in many contexts.

k
  Form: Tall ascender with two short diagonal strokes meeting at the midpoint on the right side. Relatively rare in this text.
  Examples: "Take" (final 'ke')
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'lc' or 'b' with an extra stroke.

l
  Form: Tall single stroke with a small loop or no loop at the top. Shorter and simpler than 'b', 'h', or 'd' ascenders.
  Examples: "let", "boyle", "still"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with tall 'e' ascenders, or with the first stroke of 'h' or 'b'.

m
  Form: Three minims (short vertical strokes) connected at the top. The connections are sometimes angular, sometimes rounded.
  Examples: "more", "them", "manner"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — three minims can be misread as 'ni', 'in', 'iu', 'wi', etc. The classic minim problem.

n
  Form: Two minims connected. Looks like a smaller version of 'm' or a taller 'u' without a clear curve.
  Examples: "and", "not", "in"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — easily confused with 'u', 'v', part of 'm', or 'ii'. Must rely on context.

o
  Form: Roughly circular or oval, closed. Generally well-formed and distinct in this hand.
  Examples: "of", "or", "good", "colour"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'a' when hastily closed, or 'e' when left slightly open.

p
  Form: Descender going below the baseline, with a rounded bowl on the right side at x-height. The descender is straight or slightly curved.
  Examples: "put", "place", "pepper"
  Confusion risk: Can be confused with 'q' (different bowl direction) or unclear 'b' if descender is misread as ascender.

q
  Form: Round bowl with a descender, tail curves to the right (opposite of 'g'). Almost always followed by 'u'.
  Examples: "quarter"
  Confusion risk: Can look like 'g' or '9'.

r
  Form: Two main forms in this hand: (1) A short vertical stroke with a rightward shoulder near the top (standard 'r'); (2) A '2'-shaped form (round 'r') used after rounded letters like 'o'. The round form is common in secretary hands.
  Examples: "receipt", "great", "or", "water"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — the round 'r' after 'o' can look like 'v' or 'z'. The standard 'r' can resemble 'v' or a partial 'n'.

s
  Form: Two forms: (1) Long 's' — a tall stroke resembling 'f' but WITHOUT a crossbar, used at the beginning and middle of words. (2) Short/round 's' — used only at the end of words, looks like the modern 's'.
  Examples: Long 's': "still", "sugar"; Short 's': "this", "waters"
  Confusion risk: HIGH — long 's' easily confused with 'f'. Must check for crossbar presence. Also can resemble 'l'.

t
  Form: Short vertical stroke with a crossbar. Shorter than ascenders (b, d, h, l). The crossbar sometimes extends to connect with the next letter.
  Examples: "the", "Take", "to", "that"
  Confusion risk: Can look like 'l' if crossbar is faint, or 'c' if stroke is too short.

u
  Form: A curved stroke, open at the top, like a modern 'u'. Formed with two minims connected at the bottom.
  Examples: "put", "use", "sugar"
  Confusion risk: VERY HIGH — nearly identical to 'n' in many instances. Classic minim confusion. Also can resemble 'v'.

v
  Form: Used primarily at the beginning of words (where modern English would use 'u' or 'v'). Formed with two diagonal strokes meeting at a point at the bottom. Slightly more angular than 'u'.
  Examples: "very", "vinegar"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'u' at word-initial position, or 'r' in some formations.

w
  Form: Essentially a double-'v', formed with four strokes or two connected 'u'/'v' shapes. Broader than most letters.
  Examples: "water", "with", "was"
  Confusion risk: Can look like 'vu', 'uv', or 'vv'. In tight writing, the middle strokes blur.

x
  Form: Two diagonal strokes crossing. Rare in this text.
  Examples: Rare
  Confusion risk: Low (rarely appears).

y
  Form: Similar to modern 'y' — two strokes meeting above the baseline, with a descender curving left or straight down. Sometimes has a dot above (like 'i').
  Examples: "you", "they"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'p' if descender is straight, or 'g' if the upper portion is rounded.

z
  Form: Rare. When present, formed with a horizontal stroke, diagonal, and horizontal again — similar to modern 'z' but sometimes more stylized.
  Examples: Very rare in this text
  Confusion risk: Can resemble round 'r' or '3'.

================================================================
CAPITAL LETTERS
================================================================

A
  Form: Two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak with a crossbar. Sometimes has a leftward serif or approach stroke. Fairly standard.
  Examples: "A water", "And"
  Confusion risk: Generally clear. Could be confused with 'H' if crossbar placement is ambiguous.

F
  Form: Large version of lowercase 'f' with an ornamental top curve and clear crossbar. Prominent when starting recipe lines.
  Examples: "For"
  Confusion risk: Could be confused with capital 'T' if the crossbar is unclear.

I
  Form: Single tall stroke, sometimes with a slight curve or flourish at top and bottom.
  Examples: Rare as a capital in this text
  Confusion risk: Could look like 'l' or 'J'.

T
  Form: Strong horizontal crossbar at the top with a vertical downstroke. The crossbar often extends well to both sides. This is one of the most prominent capitals in the text, used frequently for "Take."
  Examples: "Take", "The", "This"
  Confusion risk: Generally very clear. The wide crossbar distinguishes it.

R
  Form: Vertical stroke with a curved bowl and sometimes an extended leg. May have ornamental elements.
  Examples: "Receipt"
  Confusion risk: Can resemble 'K' or 'B' if the bowl is unclear.

S
  Form: Rounded capital 'S' with double curves. Sometimes fairly ornate.
  Examples: At start of sentences/recipes
  Confusion risk: Could resemble '8' or 'G' when heavily flourished.

W
  Form: Broad letter formed with multiple diagonal strokes. Often quite wide.
  Examples: "Water"
  Confusion risk: Generally clear due to width.

================================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL FEATURES
================================================================

1. THORN / 'ye': The scribe may use the letter thorn (resembling 'y') as an abbreviation for "the" — written as a 'y'-like character with a superscript 'e'. Read as "the", not "ye".

2. SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS: Words are sometimes abbreviated with superscript letters, e.g.:
   - 'w^th' = "with"
   - 'y^e' = "the"
   - 'y^t' = "that"
   - 'w^ch' = "which"

3. LONG 's': Used consistently at the start and middle of words. Only the final 's' in a word uses the modern round form.

4. AMPERSAND / BREVIGRAPH: The scribe uses an abbreviated form for "and" that may look like a stylized '&' or a looped character.

5. TILDES / MACRONS: A horizontal line above a vowel may indicate a missing 'n' or 'm' (e.g., a line over 'a' could mean 'an' or 'am').

6. CONTRACTIONS: Common words may be shortened with a mark of abbreviation (e.g., a curl or stroke indicating missing letters).

7. CATCHWORDS / MARGINALIA: Some marginal notes appear, possibly in the same or a different hand.

================================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (Highest to Lowest)
================================================================

CRITICAL RISK (most likely to cause transcription errors):
1.  m / n / u / i — MINIM CONFUSION: The single greatest challenge. These letters are formed with nearly identical short vertical strokes. 'm' = 3 minims, 'n' = 2 minims, 'u' = 2 minims (curved bottom), 'i' = 1 minim. Without dots on 'i' or clear context, a sequence like 'minim' is extremely hard to parse.

2.  long s / f — Both have descenders and vertical strokes. The ONLY difference is the crossbar: 'f' has one, long 's' does not. If the crossbar is faint or the ink bleeds, these are nearly identical.

3.  c / e — Both are small, curved, open shapes. When the scribe writes quickly, the small interior stroke of 'e' disappears, making it look like 'c'.

HIGH RISK:
4.  secretary d / cl — The leftward-looping ascender of 'd' can look like 'c' + 'l' to readers unfamiliar with secretary hand.

5.  r (round form) / v / z — The '2'-shaped round 'r' used after 'o' can be mistaken for other characters.

6.  a / o / u — All are small, round or curved letters that can blur together in hasty writing.

7.  t / l — If the crossbar of 't' is faint or missing, it becomes indistinguishable from 'l'.

MODERATE RISK:
8.  e / o — When 'e' is closed or 'o' is left slightly open.

9.  h / b — Similar ascender structure; the bowl/curve at the bottom is the distinguishing feature.

10. v / u — At word-initial position; 'v' is more angular, 'u' more curved, but the distinction is inconsistent.

11. y / p / g — All have descenders; the upper portion distinguishes them but can be ambiguous.

LOW RISK:
12. w / double-v — Usually clear from context and width.
13. k / lc — Rarely causes errors; 'k' is uncommon.
14. Capital letters — Generally well-differentiated due to size and flourishes.

================================================================
STRATEGIC NOTES FOR TRANSCRIPTION
================================================================

1. When encountering minim sequences (m/n/u/i), use WORD CONTEXT to disambiguate. Sound out possible readings. Check if the resulting word makes sense in the recipe context.

2. For long s vs. f: check whether a crossbar is present. If uncertain, consider whether 'f' or 's' makes sense in context.

3. For secretary 'd': remember the ascender loops LEFT. If you see what looks like 'cl', consider whether 'd' produces a real word.

4. For round 'r' after 'o': expect it. The combination 'or' will often look like 'ov' or 'oz' to modern eyes.

5. Recipe vocabulary helps: words like "Take", "water", "boyle", "sugar", "pepper", "dried", "fresh", "receipt", "quarter" recur frequently and can serve as anchors.
