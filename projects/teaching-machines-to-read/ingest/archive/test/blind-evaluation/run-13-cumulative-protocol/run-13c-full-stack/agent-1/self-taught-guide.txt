SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
(Based on study of Henslow MS688 training pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)
=====================================================================

1. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THIS HAND
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The Henslow MS688 scribe writes a fairly consistent secretary hand with
clear recipe titles in a larger, slightly more decorative script. The body
text is smaller but generally legible. The scribe is consistent in spelling
habits and letterforms across the five pages studied.

2. LOWERCASE LETTERFORMS
---------------------------------------------------------------------

a — Open-topped, resembles a modern lowercase a. Fairly consistent.
     Example: "Take" (appears at the start of every recipe)

b — Has a tall ascender with a loop or bow at baseline. The bow sits
     to the right of the ascender.
     Example: "bee" (page 08), "boyle" (page 16), "bloude" (page 14)

c — Small, open curve to the right. Can resemble a small t in some
     positions. Example: "colde" (page 16), "come" (page 20)

d — Secretary d: the ascender curves back to the LEFT (like a reversed
     modern d). This is one of the most distinctive secretary hand letters.
     Example: "drinke" (page 10), "dried" (page 20), "diseased" (page 14)

e — Small, often resembles a reversed modern 3 or a small loop.
     Example: "euelle" (page 08), "egge" (page 10)

f — Tall letter with a crossbar. Descends below the baseline. The crossbar
     distinguishes it from long-s, which has NO crossbar.
     Example: "for" (appears in many titles), "fyer" (page 10)

g — Has a descender that curves to the LEFT and back. Resembles a figure-8
     shape in some instances. Compare carefully with d (which also curves
     but differently). Example: "greace" (page 08), "gargolle" (page 14)

h — Has an ascender; the second stroke curves down to the right.
     Example: "handfull" (page 10), "heale" (page 20)

i — Single minim stroke, sometimes with a dot above (inconsistent).
     Must be distinguished from the minims of u, n, m by context and
     counting. Example: "in" (very common), "itt" (very common)

k — Tall letter with ascender. The lower strokes form an angular shape.
     Example: "keepe" (page 08), "killed" (page 20)

l — Tall letter WITHOUT crossbar (unlike f). Can be confused with long-s.
     Check: l often has a slight serif or hook at the top.
     Example: "lett" (page 14), "liquore" (page 08)

m — Three minims in a row. Must count carefully to distinguish from
     combinations of n+i, u+n, etc.
     Example: "make" (page 20), "mouthe" (page 14)

n — Two minims. Distinguish from u by context and comparison.
     Example: "narrowe" (page 14), "nine" (page 08)

o — Small round letter. In this hand, relatively consistent but can
     be confused with a in some positions.
     Example: "ounce" (page 16), "over" (page 10)

p — Has a descender below baseline. The bowl sits at the baseline level.
     Example: "putt" (page 08), "plaister" (page 14)

q — Rare. Has a descender with a stroke going right.
     Example: "quarte" (page 08), "Quinsie" (page 14)

r — Secretary r: often looks like a 2 or a v-shape. Modern r form also
     appears in some positions (especially after o). Watch for confusion
     with v. Example: "runninge" (page 08), "rootes" (page 10)

s — TWO FORMS:
     (a) Long-s: Used at the beginning and middle of words. Tall, like
         f but WITHOUT a crossbar. Example: "seethe" (page 08), "straine" (page 08)
     (b) Round-s: Used at the end of words. Looks like a modern s.
         Example: "eyes" (page 08), "tymes" (page 14)
     KEY: Long-s vs. f — check for crossbar. Long-s vs. l — compare tops.

t — Short ascender with a crossbar. Shorter than f, l, long-s.
     Example: "the" (very common), "togeather" (very common)

u — Two minims, like n but often with a slight connecting curve at bottom.
     At word beginnings, this scribe uses u (NOT v): "use," "uppon," "untill."
     Example: "untill" (page 08), "use" (page 20)

v — Used in the MIDDLE of words where modern English uses v.
     Example: "ouer" (page 14), "euerie" (page 08), "Liuer" (page 10)
     NOTE: This scribe does NOT follow the v-initial convention strictly.
     The scribe writes "untill" not "vntill," "use" not "vse."

w — Distinctive: two v-shapes joined together. Wider than other letters.
     Example: "water" (page 08), "wine" (page 10), "withe" (very common)

x — Rare. Cross shape. Example: "sixe" (page 08, page 16)

y — Has a descender. The tail curves left or straight down.
     Example: "yow" (=you, page 08), "ytt" (=it, very common)

z — Very rare. Not clearly observed in the training set.

3. CAPITAL LETTERS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
A — Large, ornate. Top has a sweeping stroke. Example: "A watter" (page 08),
     "A Gargisine" (page 16), "Applye" (page 16)

B — Large with clear double-bow. Example: "Bowlarmene" (page 08),
     "Boyle" (page 20), "Butt" (page 20)

C — Large open curve. Example: "Cinkfoud" (page 14)

D — Large with backward-curving ascender (like lowercase but bigger).
     Example: "Decayed" (page 08), "Deffnes" (referenced in error protocol)

F — Written as ff (double-f convention). Example: "Firste" (page 14),
     "ffasten" (page 08 — meaning "Fasten")

G — Large with a descending tail. Example: "Gargisine" (page 16)

H — Similar to lowercase but larger. Example: "Halle" (page 20)

I — Can look quite different from modern I. Beware of confusion.

L — Large version of lowercase l. Example: "Liver Longes" (page 10)

M — Large, multiple strokes. Example: "Make" (page 20), "Marye" (page 10)

Q — Ornate with large descender. Example: "Quinsie" (page 14)

R — Can look different from modern R. Example: "Redd" (page 20)

T — Large with horizontal stroke. Example: "Take" (very common),
     "To Make" (page 20), "The" (common)

Y — Large with descender. Example: "Yow" (page 20)

4. DOUBLED CONSONANTS — A CRITICAL PATTERN
---------------------------------------------------------------------
This scribe consistently doubles final consonants in many common words:
  putt (put), ytt (it), itt (it), pott (pot), knitt (knit), sitte (sit),
  lett (let), withe (with + e), togeather, moreninge, eveninge

ALWAYS check for doubled letters. If you see what might be two t's,
transcribe BOTH. This is not a scribal error — it is a consistent habit.

5. COMMON SPELLING PATTERNS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- "ytt" or "itt" = it (always doubled t)
- "putt" = put (always doubled t)
- "yow" = you
- "withe" = with (has a final e)
- "togeather" = together
- "euerie" = every
- "moreninge" = morning
- "runninge" = running (-inge ending, not -ing)
- "seethe" = to boil/simmer
- "annoynte" = anoint
- "witheall" = withal
- "verie" = very
- "faire" = fair
- "probatum" = Latin for "proven/tested" — appears after recipes
- "fyer" = fire
- "thereof" = thereof
- "againe" = again
- "boyle" = boil
- "straine" = strain
- "bee" = be
- "sodde" = boiled/seethed (past participle of "seethe")

6. ABBREVIATION PATTERNS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
This scribe uses very few abbreviations. Words are generally written out
in full. Do NOT assume abbreviations are present — transcribe the full
word if you see the full word.

Occasional abbreviation marks (tildes or macrons over letters indicating
a missing n or m) may appear but are rare in this hand.

7. RECIPE STRUCTURE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- Title line in larger script, often centered or offset
- Body begins with "Take" (capitalized, sometimes ornate)
- Ingredients listed with quantities ("a handfull," "two ounces," etc.)
- Instructions use "seethe," "boyle," "straine," "putt," "mingell"
- Recipes may end with "probatum" (proven)

8. KEY DANGERS (from training study)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- Long-s / f / l confusion: ALWAYS check for crossbar (f only)
- Minim counting: u, n, m, i are formed from identical short strokes
- Secretary d: curves LEFT (don't confuse with g, which curves differently)
- r vs. v: secretary r can look like v — compare within manuscript
- ff = capital F at word starts, but ff can also appear mid-word
- Do NOT normalize spelling (don't change "putt" to "put")
- Do NOT replace unknown words with familiar ones
- The scribe uses u at word beginnings (NOT v): "use" not "vse"
- Preserve all punctuation marks visible in the manuscript

9. MEASUREMENT AND QUANTITY TERMS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- "handfull" / "handefull" = handful
- "spoonefull" / "spoonefulls" = spoonful(s)
- "quarte" = quart
- "pinte" = pint
- "pottell" = pottle (half gallon)
- "quartene" = quartern (quarter pint)
- "ounce" / "ounces" = ounce(s)
