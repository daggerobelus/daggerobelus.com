SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
(Based on study of Henslow MS688 training pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)
=====================================================================

GENERAL OBSERVATIONS
This manuscript is a recipe book from 1601, written in English secretary hand.
The scribe is fairly consistent and relatively legible, with clear recipe
titles and a regular hand. Recipes begin with "Take" and are separated by
titles that name the ailment being treated.

=====================================================================
SECTION 1: LOWERCASE LETTERFORMS
=====================================================================

a — Open-topped, resembles a modern lowercase a but sometimes more angular.
     Clear in: "and," "age," "annoynte," "awaye"

b — Has a tall ascender with a round bowl at the bottom. The ascender loops
     back to connect. Clear in: "bee," "beate," "boyle," "bloude"

c — Small, open curve to the right, similar to modern c. Can look like t
     in some positions. Clear in: "colde," "come," "candye"

d — Has a looped ascender that curves back to the LEFT (distinctive of
     secretary hand). The ascender loops over the top and comes back down
     on the left side. Clear in: "drinke," "dayes," "dothe"
     NOTE: This is very different from modern d. The loop goes leftward.

e — Small, like a reversed figure-3 or a tight curl. Sometimes looks like
     a modern e, sometimes more like a small loop. Clear in: "the," "euelle,"
     "euerie"

f — Has a crossbar through a vertical stroke that descends below the
     baseline. Clear in: "for," "fyer," "freshe," "fine"
     NOTE: ff at the start of a word = capital F (e.g., "ffasten")

g — Descender curves back up to the right. Somewhat like a figure-8 with
     the bottom loop going right. Clear in: "greace," "gargolle," "goode"

h — Tall ascender with a curved stroke going right at the bottom.
     Clear in: "his," "holde," "handefull," "heale"

i — A single short vertical stroke (minim) with a dot above. Sometimes
     the dot is absent or looks like a tick. Clear in: "in," "itt," "into"

k — Tall ascender with angular strokes at the bottom forming the kick.
     Clear in: "keepe," "kinges," "killed"

l — Tall vertical stroke, similar to long-s but generally has a slight
     flag or serif at the top. Clear in: "lett," "longes," "liquore"

long-s — Tall letter that looks like f WITHOUT a crossbar. Used at the
     beginning and middle of words. Clear in: "sage," "seethe," "straine"
     NOTE: This is the most important letter to distinguish. It looks like
     f but has NO crossbar through it. At word endings, round-s is used
     instead.

m — Three minims (short vertical strokes) joined at the top. Clear in:
     "make," "muche," "mouthe," "milke"

n — Two minims joined at the top. Clear in: "nine," "narrowe," "neste"
     NOTE: n and u can be very hard to distinguish. Both are two minims.
     Context and comparison are essential.

o — Round, like modern o. Clear in: "one," "ounce," "ouer," "owte"

p — Descender goes below the baseline, bowl at the top right.
     Clear in: "putt," "potte," "powder," "plaister"

q — Rare. Has a descender like p but going the other direction.
     Clear in: "quarte," "quantitye," "Quinsie"

r — In secretary hand, r has a distinctive form: it can look like a
     modern v or like a small angular stroke. It sits on the baseline with
     a short downstroke and an angular shoulder. Clear in: "runninge,"
     "rootes," "rose"
     NOTE: r and v can look similar in this hand. Compare carefully.

round-s — Used at the end of words. Looks like modern s.
     Clear in: "eyes," "rootes," "ounces"

t — Short vertical stroke with a crossbar, sits on the baseline. Does
     not extend as far above the line as b, d, or l. Clear in: "the,"
     "take," "them," "thereto"
     NOTE: tt (doubled t) is very common in this scribe: "putt," "ytt,"
     "itt," "pott," "lett," "butt"

u — Two minims, like n but with the connecting stroke at the bottom
     instead of the top (in theory). In practice, u and n look almost
     identical. Clear in: "use," "untill," "uppon"
     NOTE: This scribe uses u at the start of words (NOT v): "untill,"
     "use," "uppon" — do NOT change these to v.

v — Looks like modern v but can resemble r. Clear in: "verie," "vntill"
     NOTE: Actually this scribe seems to use u initially more often.
     Check each instance.

w — A distinctive letter with multiple strokes creating a shape like
     two v's joined. Clear in: "water," "withe," "when," "washe"

x — Rare. A cross shape.

y — Has a descender and often a dot above. Looks like a modern y with
     a longer curved descender. Clear in: "yow," "youre," "ytt"

z — Very rare in these pages.

=====================================================================
SECTION 2: CAPITAL LETTERS
=====================================================================

A — Larger, more angular version of lowercase a with extra flourishes.
     Clear in: "A watter," "A plaister," "A Gargisine," "Applye"

B — Large looped letter with a prominent bowl. Clear in: "Bowlarmene,"
     "Boyle," "Broome," "Butt"

C — Large sweeping curve. Clear in: "Cinkfoud," "Cannterberries"

D — Large letter with prominent leftward-looping ascender.
     Clear in: "Decayed," "Deffnes," "Docke"

F — Written as ff (double-f). This is the standard secretary convention.
     Clear in: "ffasten," "Firste," "Fistula," "For" (sometimes also ff)

G — Large ornate letter. Clear in: "Gargisine," "greene"

H — Large with prominent ascender. Clear in: "Halle"

I — Can be ornate and look unlike modern I. Not very common as initial.

L — Large with a looped top. Clear in: "Liver," "Longes," "Laye"

M — Large with three downstrokes. Clear in: "Make," "Marye"

P — Large with descender. Clear in: pronoun usage

Q — Very distinctive large capital with a long tail.
     Clear in: "Quinsie," "Queene"

R — Large and can look very different from modern R. May have angular
     strokes. Clear in: "Redd," "Reasons"

S — Can appear as a large round S or as a large long-s form.
     Clear in: "Sunne," "Sunn"

T — Large, often with a prominent crossbar. Clear in: "Take," "To,"
     "The," "This"

Y — Large with descender. Clear in: "Yow"

=====================================================================
SECTION 3: COMMON ABBREVIATIONS AND CONVENTIONS
=====================================================================

1. ff = capital F (at word start)
2. Double consonants are common: tt (putt, ytt, itt, pott, butt, lett, att)
3. -inge ending is very common: runninge, moreninge, eveninge, swellinge
4. The scribe uses "withe" for "with" consistently
5. "ytt" and "itt" both used for "it"
6. "yow" for "you"
7. "euerie" for "every" (u/v not changed)
8. "togeather" for "together"
9. "uppon" for "upon" (with u, NOT v)
10. "owte" for "out"
11. "probatum" = "proven/tested" (Latin tag used to validate recipes)
12. Punctuation: colons and periods are used but sparingly. Commas appear.

=====================================================================
SECTION 4: KEY PITFALLS AND WARNINGS
=====================================================================

1. LONG-s vs. f: The most critical distinction. Long-s has NO crossbar.
   f HAS a crossbar. Both are tall. If in doubt, look for the crossbar.

2. u vs. n: Both are two minims. The connecting stroke location differs
   but can be very subtle. Use context and cross-reference with known
   words.

3. Secretary d: The leftward loop is distinctive. Don't confuse with g
   (which loops right/below) or with l.

4. r vs. v: Secretary r can look like v. When you see a "v" in the
   middle of a word, consider whether r would make more sense.

5. This scribe uses u at word starts (not v): "untill," "use," "uppon."
   Do NOT change these to v-initial spellings.

6. Double-f can appear mid-word, not just as a capital F convention.
   Example: "Deffnes" (deafness).

7. Spelling is NOT modern. Preserve exactly what the scribe writes:
   "muche," "togeather," "moreninge," "withe," "seethe," etc.

8. The word "Take" begins almost every recipe and is a reliable anchor
   for identifying T, a, k, e letterforms.

=====================================================================
SECTION 5: RECIPE STRUCTURE
=====================================================================

Recipes follow a consistent pattern:
1. Title line: "For [ailment]" or "To [action]" or "A [type] for [ailment]"
2. Body begins with "Take" followed by ingredients and instructions
3. Measurements: "a quarte," "a handefull," "a spoonefull," "an ounce"
4. Connecting phrases: "and putt to ytt," "and boyle," "and straine"
5. Application instructions: "laye itt to," "washe," "drinke thereof"
6. Sometimes ends with "probatum" (proven)

This structure helps orient you when reading a new page: look for the
title lines first, then anchor on "Take" to begin deciphering each recipe.
