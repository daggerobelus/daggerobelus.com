SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
(Based on study of Henslow MS688 training pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)
=====================================================================

1. GENERAL OBSERVATIONS
-----------------------
The Henslow MS688 is written in a clear, somewhat formal secretary hand from
approximately 1601. The scribe is consistent and reasonably legible. The text
consists of medical and household recipes, each beginning with a title and
then typically starting with "Take" as the first word of instructions.

2. LOWERCASE LETTERFORMS
-------------------------

a — Open-topped, two strokes: a curved back and a vertical stroke. Fairly
    recognizable. Sometimes resembles a modern 'a' but often more angular.

b — Has a tall ascender with a loop at top, followed by a rounded bowl.
    Can resemble 't' when the crossbar of t is faint. Check the bowl shape.

c — Small, open curve. Can be confused with 't' in the middle of words.
    Secretary c is often just a curved stroke without a full loop.

d — Has a looped ascender that curves to the RIGHT (backward-leaning loop).
    This is the secretary-hand diagnostic 'd'. The loop goes to the right,
    unlike modern d where the ascender goes straight up. Confirmed in:
    "sodde," "dreggans," "bloude," "putt," "diseased," "bloude."

e — Small, often looks like a reversed '3' or a simple curved stroke.
    Frequently appears doubled: "teethe," "seethe," "bee," "keepe."

f — Has a crossbar through its stem. This distinguishes it from long-s.
    At word starts, ff = capital F: "ffasten," "fyer," "freshe."
    The ff convention: two f's at a word's beginning represent a capital F.

g — Has a descender that curves BACK under the word. Compare to 'd':
    g's descender curves, d's loop goes to the right. Confirmed in:
    "runninge," "kinges," "moreninge."

h — Tall ascender with a curved stroke descending to the right at bottom.
    The descender can look like a tail. Confirmed in: "teethe," "the," "them."

i — A single minim stroke (short vertical). May have a dot or tick above.
    Part of the dangerous minim group (i, u, n, m). Context and comparison
    are essential.

k — Has a tall ascender and two diagonal strokes at bottom. Fairly
    distinctive. Confirmed in: "keepe," "Take," "make."

l — A tall stroke, taller than minims. Can be confused with long-s.
    Key difference: l typically has a small serif or foot; long-s does not.
    Confirmed in: "halfe," "bloude," "allome," "lettinge."

m — THREE minim strokes. Can look like "ni," "in," "uu," or other combinations.
    Count minims carefully. Confirmed in: "them," "muche," "make."

n — TWO minim strokes. Often confused with 'u' (also two minims). The
    difference is subtle: n has the connecting stroke at TOP, u at BOTTOM.
    Confirmed in: "runninge," "hunnye," "annoynte."

o — Small round letter. Fairly standard but can be confused with 'a' in
    some contexts. Confirmed in: "rosemarie," "too," "moreninge."

p — Has a descender going below the line, with a bowl at the writing line.
    Confirmed in: "putt," "potte," "powder."

q — Rare. Has a descender. Seen in "quarte," "quantite," "Quinsie."

r — Secretary r can take two forms: (1) a standard form with a shoulder,
    and (2) a form that looks somewhat like a modern 'v' or '2'. The
    second form appears especially after 'o'. Do NOT confuse with 'v'.
    Confirmed in: "rosemarie," "runninge," "rootes."

s — TWO FORMS:
    (a) Long-s: Tall, used at the start and middle of words. Looks like
        an 'f' WITHOUT a crossbar. Can be confused with 'l' or 'f'.
        Confirmed in: "sage," "seethe," "sodde," "spoonefulls."
    (b) Round-s: Used at word endings. Looks like a modern lowercase 's'.
        Confirmed in: "teethe," "eyes," "dayes."

t — Short letter with a crossbar. The crossbar may be faint. Can be
    confused with 'b' when the crossbar is unclear. Often doubled at
    word endings: "putt," "ytt," "itt." Confirmed in: "Take," "teethe."

u — TWO minim strokes, like 'n'. The connecting stroke is at the BOTTOM.
    In secretary hand, 'u' and 'v' are NOT always positional (u medial,
    v initial). This scribe (Henslow) uses 'u' at the start of words:
    "untill," "use," "uppon" — NOT "vntill," "vse," "vppon."

v — Looks different from 'u'. Typically more angular, with strokes meeting
    at a point. Confirmed in: "verie," "vaine," "vinneger."

w — Wide letter, essentially two v's joined. Fairly distinctive.
    Confirmed in: "watter," "withe," "water," "washe."

x — Rare in these texts. Standard cross shape.

y — Has a descender that curves down and to the left. Distinct from 'g'.
    The dot above is sometimes present. Confirmed in: "ytt," "yow," "youre."

z — Not observed in training texts.

3. CAPITAL LETTERS
------------------
A — Large, ornate form. Confirmed in: "A watter," "A Gargisine," "A plaister."
B — Large with loops. Confirmed in: "Bowlarmene," "Broome," "Butter," "Boyle."
C — Sweeping curve. Confirmed in: "Cinkfoud," "Clary."
D — Large with backward loop. Confirmed in: "Decayed," "Deffnes," "Docke."
F — Written as 'ff' (double-f convention). Confirmed in: "For," "Firste."
G — Large ornate form. Confirmed in: "Gargisine."
H — Not commonly seen as capital in these texts.
I — Can look quite different from lowercase. Watch carefully.
L — Large form with a loop. Confirmed in: "Liver," "Longes," "Langdebeefe."
M — Large with loops. Confirmed in: "Make," "Marye," "Methridatum."
P — Large with descender. Confirmed in: "purge."
Q — Ornate, large. Confirmed in: "Queene," "Quinsie."
R — Can look very different from modern R. Confirmed in: "Redd."
S — Ornate capital form. Confirmed in: "Sunne," "Sunn."
T — Large with prominent crossbar. Confirmed in: "Take," "To," "The," "This."
Y — Confirmed in: "Yow."

4. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONVENTIONS
---------------------------------
- ff = capital F at the start of words (e.g., "ffor" = "For," "ffasten" = "Fasten")
- The tilde or macron over vowels can indicate a missing 'n' or 'm'
- This scribe generally writes words out in full; abbreviations are rare
- Doubled final consonants are a CONSISTENT scribal habit: putt, ytt, itt, pott,
  knitt, sitte, etc. ALWAYS preserve these.
- "ytt" and "itt" both mean "it" — preserve the exact form used

5. SPELLING HABITS (Henslow scribe)
-------------------------------------
- Double vowels: "teethe," "seethe," "keepe," "bee"
- -inge endings: "runninge," "moreninge," "eveninge"
- "withe" for "with"
- "togeather" for "together"
- "euerie" for "every" (e = short-e, u = v sound)
- "yow" for "you"
- "youre" for "your"
- "muche" for "much"
- u at word starts (not v): "use," "untill," "uppon"

6. KEY DIAGNOSTIC CHECKS
--------------------------
When uncertain about a letter:

(a) Long-s vs. f vs. l:
    - f has a CROSSBAR; long-s and l do NOT
    - long-s often has a slight curve; l is straighter
    - Check position: long-s at start/middle of words, round-s at end

(b) u vs. n (minim confusion):
    - Count minims carefully
    - Compare to same letter combinations elsewhere
    - 'nn' = 4 minims; 'un' = 4 minims; 'nu' = 4 minims
    - Context and comparison to other instances is essential

(c) d vs. g:
    - d: looped ascender curving right, straighter descender
    - g: descender curves BACK under the line
    - Check endings: -inge vs. -inde (both are real possibilities)

(d) t vs. b:
    - t has a crossbar; b has a closed bowl
    - If crossbar is faint, t and b look similar at word starts

(e) r vs. v:
    - Secretary r (especially after o) can resemble v
    - Compare to confirmed r's and v's in the same text

7. COMMON RECIPE-BOOK VOCABULARY
----------------------------------
These words appear frequently and can be used as anchor points:
- "Take" — almost always begins recipe instructions
- "and" — very common conjunction
- "the" — common article
- "putt" / "ytt" / "itt" — very frequent (note doubled t)
- "withe" — this scribe's "with"
- "togeather" — this scribe's "together"
- "boyle" — boil
- "straine" — strain
- "then" — then (not abbreviated as "yn")
- "thereof" — thereof
- "probatum" — Latin: "it has been proven" (marks tested recipes)
