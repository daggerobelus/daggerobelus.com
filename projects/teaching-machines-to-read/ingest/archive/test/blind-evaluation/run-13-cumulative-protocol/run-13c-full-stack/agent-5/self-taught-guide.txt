SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
Based on Study of Henslow MS688 Training Pages (8, 10, 14, 16, 20)
=====================================================================

1. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS
The Henslow hand is a relatively clear secretary hand from c.1601. The scribe
writes with consistent letterforms and predictable habits, making it a good
training text. Lines are well-spaced and recipe titles are set off from body text.

2. THE ALPHABET — LOWERCASE LETTERS

a — Open-topped form, resembles a modern "a" but often with a more angular
    top stroke. Can be confused with "o" when written hastily.

b — Has a tall ascender with a loop at the top, descending into the bowl.
    The bowl sits on the baseline. Can resemble "t" if the crossbar is faint.

c — Small, open to the right, similar to modern "c". Can be confused with
    "t" in medial position.

d — Secretary "d" has a distinctive looped ascender that curves backward
    (leftward). The loop differentiates it from "l". At word endings, can
    resemble "g" — check the descender direction.

e — Small, often appears as a tight loop. Very common in terminal position
    (the scribe adds "-e" to many words: "mouthe", "throte", "bloude").

f — Tall letter with a crossbar. Distinguished from long-s by the crossbar.
    Double-f (ff) = capital F at word beginnings: "ffasten", "ffellons".
    Also appears medially: "Deffnes", "stiffe".

g — Has a descending stroke that curves back to the right (unlike "d" which
    has a straighter descender). The curved return stroke is the key
    differentiator from "d".

h — Tall ascender, open bowl at bottom. The descender sometimes dips
    below the baseline.

i — A single minim stroke with a dot (often faint or absent). Part of
    the minim cluster (i, u, n, m).

j — Not commonly distinguished from "i" in this period.

k — Rare in this hand. When it appears ("keepe"), it has a tall ascender
    with angular strokes to the right.

l — Tall letter, no crossbar (unlike f), no loop at top (unlike d).
    Can be confused with long-s. Check context and compare to confirmed
    examples. In this hand, l often has a slight rightward lean at the top.

m — Three minims. Must be carefully counted to distinguish from "nn", "ni",
    "in", etc.

n — Two minims. Can be confused with "u" (also two minims). In this scribe's
    hand, "n" tends to have sharper peaks at the top of the minims, while
    "u" has a rounder bottom.

o — Round, sits on the baseline. Can be confused with "a" (open top) or
    mistaken in combinations: "oo" vs "ol".

p — Has a descender going below the baseline and a round bowl. Distinguished
    from other descender letters by its bowl shape.

q — Rare. Has a descender and a small bowl. Seen in "quantite", "Quinsie",
    "quarte".

r — Secretary "r" can look quite different from modern "r". In this hand,
    it often appears as a short stroke that could be confused with "v".
    Compare carefully to confirmed examples.

s — TWO FORMS:
    Long-s: Used at the beginning and middle of words. Tall, resembles "f"
    but WITHOUT a crossbar. Can be confused with "l".
    Examples: "sage", "seethe", "sweete"
    Round-s: Used at the end of words. Resembles modern "s".
    Examples: "dayes", "eyes", "rootes"

t — Has an ascender with a crossbar. The crossbar may be faint, making it
    look like "b" or "l". In this hand, the crossbar often extends to the
    right. Frequently doubled at word endings: "putt", "ytt", "itt".

u — Two minims with a rounded bottom. Can be confused with "n".
    May appear where modern English uses "v": "euerie" = "every",
    "euenynde" = "eventide". But NOTE: this scribe also uses "u" at word
    beginnings (not always switching to "v").

v — Similar to "u" but used positionally (often at word starts in some
    conventions). In this hand, "v" tends to have a more angular bottom
    than "u". But do NOT assume all initial-u is "v" — read what you see.

w — Wide letter, looks like two "v"s joined. Distinctive and usually
    clear. "water", "withe", "waxeth".

x — Rare. Cross-shaped.

y — Has a descender curving to the left below the baseline. Clearly
    distinguished from other letters. Common in "yow" (you), "ytt" (it),
    "youre" (your).

z — Very rare in these pages.

3. CAPITAL LETTERS

Capital T — Large decorative form in "Take" at recipe openings. Has wide
    horizontal strokes. Very distinctive.

Capital F — Written as "ff" (double-f convention): "ffasten", "ffellons".
    This is standard for the period.

Capital A — Larger version of lowercase with more angular strokes. Seen
    in "A watter", "A Gargisine", "A plaister".

Capital B — Large with two bowls. Seen in "Bowlarmene", "Boyle", "Butt".

Capital Q — Large decorative form. Seen in "Quinsie", "Queene".

Capital Y — Seen in "Yow" — larger version of lowercase y.

Capital R — Can look unfamiliar. Watch for unusual initial forms.

4. SCRIBE HABITS IN HENSLOW MS688

SPELLING HABITS:
- Doubles final consonants frequently: "putt", "ytt", "itt", "sitte", "knitt"
- Adds terminal "-e" to many words: "mouthe", "throte", "bloude", "fier"
- Uses "-inge" endings: "runninge", "moreninge", "swellinge", "stoppinge"
- "withe" for "with", "togeather" for "together", "euerie" for "every"
- "yow" for "you", "youre" for "your"

PUNCTUATION:
- Uses colons (:) and periods (.)
- Commas appear between list items
- Recipe titles sometimes end with decorative marks

LAYOUT:
- Recipe titles are centered or set apart
- "Take" begins most recipe bodies
- Some indentation for continuation lines

ABBREVIATIONS:
- This scribe generally writes words out in full
- Does not heavily abbreviate

5. COMMON WORDS AND THEIR APPEARANCES
"Take" — begins nearly every recipe, large capital T
"and" — written out, small, sometimes runs into adjacent words
"the" — written out in full, not abbreviated
"of" — small, sometimes hard to distinguish
"in" — two minims, context helps
"to" — small
"ytt" / "itt" — "it" with doubled t
"putt" — "put" with doubled t
"withe" — "with" plus terminal e
"them" — written out fully, NOT abbreviated as "ym"
"then" — written out fully, NOT abbreviated as "yn"
"thereof" — written as one word
"togeather" — "together"
"verie" — "very"
"euerie" — "every"
"probatum" — Latin for "proved/tested", appears at end of some recipes

6. KEY CAUTIONS
- Do NOT modernize spelling: preserve exactly what you see
- Do NOT normalize u/v — read what the scribe wrote
- Do NOT assume ff only appears at word beginnings
- Long-s is NOT f (no crossbar) and NOT l
- Count minims carefully for n/u/m/i clusters
- Recipe books contain specialized vocabulary — unfamiliar words may be
  real terms for herbs, diseases, or preparations
- "probatum" is a real word meaning the recipe has been tested
