SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
Based on study of Henslow MS688 training pages (08, 10, 14, 16, 20)

============================================================
GENERAL OBSERVATIONS
============================================================

This hand is a relatively clear English secretary hand, likely late 16th or early 17th century. The text consists of medical/household recipes. The scribe is fairly consistent but has some distinctive habits that must be learned.

============================================================
INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS
============================================================

LOWERCASE LETTERS:

a - Written with a rounded body, sometimes open at the top. Can resemble a modern 'a' but sometimes looks like a 'u' if written hastily.

b - Has an ascender loop that curves to the right. The bowl is on the right side. Fairly recognizable.

c - Small, rounded, open to the right. Can be confused with 'e' when written small.

d - Secretary 'd' is distinctive: the ascender loops back to the LEFT (not right like modern 'd'). The loop curves leftward and back down. This is one of the most characteristic secretary hand letters.

e - Very small, often just a small curved stroke. Can look like a tiny loop or a backward '3'. Sometimes hard to distinguish from 'c'.

f - Has a descender that goes below the line. The crossbar extends to the right. Double 'ff' is used at the start of words where modern English would use capital 'F' (e.g., "ffasten" for "fasten").

g - Has a distinctive descending loop. The body sits on the line with a tail curving left beneath.

h - The ascender has a loop. The second stroke curves down and sometimes extends below the baseline slightly. Can sometimes be confused with 'b' if the second stroke is unclear.

i/j - Simple minim stroke, sometimes with a dot. 'j' is not commonly distinguished from 'i' in this period. 'i' before another minim letter can be hard to isolate.

k - Has an ascender and two diagonal strokes extending from mid-height. Appears in words like "take," "keepe," "make."

l - Tall ascender with a loop at top, similar to 'h' but without the second curved stroke.

m/n - Composed of minim strokes. 'm' has three minims, 'n' has two. These are among the hardest to distinguish, especially adjacent to 'i', 'u'. Context is essential.

o - Rounded, usually closed. Fairly standard.

p - Has a descender going below the line. The bowl is to the right of the stem. Generally recognizable.

q - Similar to 'p' but the bowl is to the LEFT of the descender. Appears in words like "quantitye," "quarte," "Quinsie."

r - Two main forms: (1) a short stroke with a shoulder, resembling modern 'r'; (2) a '2'-shaped form used after 'o' and rounded letters. Both appear throughout.

s - THREE forms observed:
  - Long 's': tall, looks like 'f' but WITHOUT a full crossbar. Used at the beginning and middle of words.
  - Round 's': modern-looking 's', used at the END of words.
  - Capital 'S': a large swash form used to begin words.

t - Short ascender, crossed. The cross-stroke sometimes connects to the next letter. Can be confused with 'c' when the ascender is short.

u/v - In this period, 'u' and 'v' are largely interchangeable. 'v' tends to appear at the start of words ("verie," "vntill"), 'u' in the middle. The forms look similar — both are minim-based.

w - Formed from two 'v' shapes joined together. Fairly wide and recognizable.

x - Rare. Two crossing diagonal strokes.

y - Has a descender that curves to the left beneath the baseline. The body resembles a modern 'y'. The scribe uses 'y' frequently: "ytt" (it), "yow" (you).

z - Rare in these texts.

CAPITAL LETTERS:

A - Large swash letter, often with a decorative leftward stroke.
B - Large with two bowls, sometimes ornamental.
F - Written as double 'ff' in lowercase rather than a true capital.
Q - Large decorative letter with a long descending tail.
R - Distinctive large form with a decorative leg.
T - Large with a wide crossbar and decorative elements. The 'T' in "Take" is highly recognizable as a large ornamental letter that begins most recipe entries.
Y - Large form of lowercase 'y' with pronounced descender.

============================================================
SPELLING CONVENTIONS
============================================================

- Double vowels are common: "teethe," "seethe," "keepe," "barlye"
- Final 'e' is used extensively: "mouthe," "throte," "fier," "colde"
- 'ytt' = "it"; 'yow' = "you"; 'withe' = "with"
- 'tt' doubling is frequent: "putt," "ytt," "butt," "att," "lett"
- 'ff' at word start = modern 'f': "ffasten" = "fasten"
- 'v' and 'u' are interchangeable: "verie," "vntill," "euerie"
- '-inge' ending is standard: "runninge," "stoppinge," "swellinge"
- '-tion' appears as '-cion' or '-sion'
- 'ounce' can appear as a symbol or abbreviation

============================================================
ABBREVIATIONS AND MARKS
============================================================

- A tilde (~) or macron over a vowel often indicates a missing 'n' or 'm'
- 'p' with a crossed descender = 'per' or 'par'
- 'p' with a curved stroke = 'pro'
- Superscript letters indicate abbreviation (e.g., 'w^th' = with)
- The word "ounces" sometimes appears as a symbol resembling a 'z' with a line through it
- "probatum" (meaning "proven/tested") appears at the end of recipes as a mark of efficacy

============================================================
COMMON WORDS IN RECIPE TEXTS
============================================================

"Take" - begins nearly every recipe, written with a large ornamental 'T'
"putt" - put (always doubled 't')
"ytt" - it
"yow" - you
"withe" - with
"togeather" - together
"thereto" / "thereof" - to that / of that
"lett" - let
"handefull" - handful
"spoonefull" - spoonful
"runninge water" - running water
"probatum" - proven (recipe endorsement)
"boyle" / "boyled" - boil / boiled
"straine" - strain
"sweete" - sweet
"verie" - very
"moreninge" - morning
"eveninge" - evening
"quantitye" - quantity

============================================================
READING STRATEGIES
============================================================

1. Look for "Take" at the start of recipes — the large ornamental 'T' is a reliable anchor point.
2. Read the recipe title first (usually centered or offset) to understand context.
3. Use ingredient/medical vocabulary to help decode unclear words.
4. Watch for long 's' vs 'f': long 's' lacks the full crossbar that 'f' has.
5. Count minims carefully for m/n/i/u distinctions.
6. Secretary 'd' loops LEFT — this is the single most diagnostic letterform.
7. When stuck on a word, try sounding it out with period spelling conventions.
8. Double letters are very common; do not reduce them to single letters.
9. The scribe is consistent: once you learn a letterform from one page, it applies throughout.

============================================================
POTENTIAL CONFUSION POINTS
============================================================

- Long 's' vs 'f': check for the crossbar (f has it, long s does not or has only a partial one)
- 'c' vs 'e': context and size help distinguish
- 'n' vs 'u': minim counting and context
- 'm' vs 'in' or 'ni': minim counting
- 'Secretary d' vs 'e' + 'l': the leftward loop of 'd' can look like 'el' if misread
- 'y' vs 'thorn' (old English 'th'): in this hand, 'y' is simply 'y', not thorn
