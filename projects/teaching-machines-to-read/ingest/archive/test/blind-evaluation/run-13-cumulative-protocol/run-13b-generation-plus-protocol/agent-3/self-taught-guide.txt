SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING SECRETARY HAND
(Based on Henslow MS688 Training Pages 08, 10, 14, 16, 20)
=====================================================

GENERAL OBSERVATIONS
This scribe writes in a fairly consistent secretary hand from circa 1601.
The recipes are medical/household remedies. The scribe is systematic:
recipe titles are centered and often use a slightly larger script, while
the body text follows a standard layout beginning with "Take" (often
with an elaborated capital T).

=====================================================
SECTION 1: INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS
=====================================================

LOWERCASE LETTERS:

a - Open-topped, sometimes resembles a modern 'a' but can also look
    like 'u' when written hastily. The top loop is often open.

b - Has a tall ascender with a loop at top, descending into a round bowl.
    Can be confused with initial 't' when the crossbar of t is faint.

c - Small, round, open to the right. Can resemble 't' in mid-word.

d - Two forms observed: (1) looped secretary d with the ascender curving
    back to the left, (2) a more upright form. The descender is straighter
    than g. The looped form is most common.

e - Small, like a reversed modern 3 or a backwards c with an interior stroke.
    Often just a small loop. The scribe's e is very consistent.

f - Has a crossbar/horizontal stroke through the stem. Distinguished from
    long-s by this crossbar. Capital F is written as 'ff' (double-f convention).

g - Has a descender that curves back, unlike d which descends straighter.
    Compare "euenynge" vs potential "euenynde" readings.

h - Tall ascender, the lower portion curves out to the right. Clear and
    fairly modern-looking in this hand.

i - A single minim stroke (short vertical). Can be confused with parts
    of u, n, m. Sometimes has a small dot or tick above it.

k - Has a tall ascender with two strokes meeting at the baseline. Appears
    in "Take," "make," "like," "keepe."

l - Tall, often just a single ascending stroke. Can look like long-s but
    typically has a slight serif or flag at top. Context and comparison needed.

long-s - Tall like l and f, but NO crossbar (unlike f) and no distinct
    serif (unlike l). Appears within words and at word beginnings: "seethe,"
    "streamewater," "spoonefull." Round-s appears at word endings: "eyes,"
    "dayes."

m - Three minims side by side. Can be confused with 'ni,' 'in,' 'iu,' etc.
    Count minims carefully.

n - Two minims. The pair nn (four minims) can be confused with 'un' or 'nu.'

o - Round, closed loop. Can sometimes be confused with 'a' when open.

p - Descender goes below the line, with a round bowl at the baseline.

r - Two forms: (1) a short stroke that can resemble 'v' in secretary hand,
    (2) a more rounded 2-shaped form after o. The first form is common
    and can be mistaken for 'v.'

t - Short ascender with a crossbar. The crossbar can be faint, causing
    confusion with other letters. Distinguished from 'b' by lacking the
    loop at top.

u/v - The scribe uses BOTH u and v. Evidence from transcriptions: the scribe
    writes "untill" (not "vntill"), "use" (not "vse"), "uppon" — the scribe
    appears to use 'u' at word beginnings where some scribes would use 'v.'
    But also writes "verie" and "vntill" in some instances — need to check
    each occurrence individually. DO NOT automatically convert u->v or v->u.

w - A distinctive letter in secretary hand: looks like two v's joined together.
    Wide and angular. Clearly identifiable in words like "water," "withe," "waxe."

x - Rare. Looks like a modern x. Seen in "waxe."

y - Has a descender that curves down and to the left. The scribe consistently
    uses "ytt" for "it," "yow" for "you." The y is clear and distinctive.

CAPITAL LETTERS:

A - Can look like a large lowercase 'a' or have a distinctive calligraphic
    form with an initial approach stroke. Seen in "A watter," "A plaister,"
    "Applye."

B - Large with a decorative initial stroke. Seen in "Boyle," "Butt," "Butter."
    The loop is more elaborate than lowercase b.

C - Large curved letter. Seen in "Cinkfoud."

D - Seen in "Decayed," "Deffnes," "Docke" — has a larger loop than lowercase.

F - Written as 'ff' (double-f convention). "ffasten" = Fasten, "ffellons" = Felons.
    The ff at the start is the standard secretary hand capital F.

G - Seen in "Gargisine" — large, elaborated form.

H - Seen in "Halle" — distinctive tall form.

L - Seen in "Liver," "Longes," "Langdebeefe" — larger than lowercase.

M - Seen in "Make," "Marye" — wide with three main strokes.

Q - Seen in "Quinsie," "Queene" — distinctive with a tail.

R - Can look different from modern R. Seen in "Redd," "Reasons."

S - Seen in "Sunne" — large, swooping form different from long-s.

T - The most frequent capital — "Take" appears at the start of nearly every
    recipe. Very elaborate with a large curved stroke. Easy to identify.

Y - Seen in "Yow" — larger version of lowercase y.

NUMERALS:
Not heavily present in training materials. Watch for early modern numeral
forms that differ from modern: '2' can look like 'a,' '1' can look like '&.'

=====================================================
SECTION 2: COMMON ABBREVIATIONS AND CONVENTIONS
=====================================================

- ff = capital F at word beginnings (ffasten = Fasten)
- ytt = "it" (not an abbreviation, but a consistent spelling)
- itt = "it" (variant, also with doubled t)
- yow = "you"
- withe = "with" (the scribe adds a final -e)
- bee = "be" (doubled e)
- att = "at" (doubled t)

The scribe in Henslow tends to write words OUT IN FULL rather than using
thorn abbreviations (no yt for "that," no ye for "the"). This is important:
if you see what looks like a full word, transcribe the full word.

=====================================================
SECTION 3: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
=====================================================

DOUBLED FINAL CONSONANTS: This is a major feature of this scribe's hand.
- putt (put), ytt/itt (it), pott (pot), att (at), sitte (sit)
- Also in the middle of words: "Butter," "togeather," "toppe"
- ALWAYS preserve these doublings.

FINAL -e: Added to many words where modern English drops it:
- "withe" (with), "sweete" (sweet), "muche" (much), "warme" (warm)
- "faire" (fair), "bloude" (blood), "youre" (your), "mouthe" (mouth)

-inge ENDINGS: Very common — "runninge," "moreninge," "eveninge," "stoppinge"

VOWEL PATTERNS:
- "ee" for modern "ee" or "ea": "teethe" (teeth), "seethe," "keepe," "sweete"
- "oo" for modern "oo" or "o": "goode," "toothe," "bloode," "spoonefull"
- "ou" in places modern English has single vowel: "bloude," "dothe"
- "ie" / "ye" endings: "annye," "euerie"

CONSONANT PATTERNS:
- "th" consistently: "withe," "mouthe," "teethe," "throte"
- "wh" spelled out: "when," "where," "white"

=====================================================
SECTION 4: VOCABULARY AND DOMAIN TERMS
=====================================================

Common recipe ingredients encountered:
- rosemarie, rewe (rue), sage, barlye, figges, dates
- mallowes, cammamell (chamomile), violetts, wormeworde
- copperis (copperas — iron sulfate)
- Bowlarmene (bole armeniac — a clay)
- gommasticke (gum mastic)
- Langdebeefe (bugloss — an herb)
- sugarr candye (sugar candy)
- Manus Christi (a medicinal confection)

Medical terms:
- Quinsie (quinsy — peritonsillar abscess)
- Fistula (a deep sore or abnormal passage)
- kinges euelle (king's evil — scrofula)
- sweate, gargolle (gargle), plaister (plaster/poultice)

Recipe structure:
- Title centered or in larger script
- Body begins with "Take" (capital T)
- Ingredients listed with quantities
- Instructions follow
- Often ends with "probatum" (proven/tested)

=====================================================
SECTION 5: COMMON PITFALLS TO AVOID
=====================================================

1. Do NOT modernize spelling — preserve exactly what you see
2. Do NOT reduce doubled consonants (putt -> put is WRONG)
3. Do NOT substitute familiar words for unfamiliar ones
4. Do NOT impose u/v conventions the scribe doesn't follow
5. Do NOT add abbreviations the scribe doesn't use
6. Watch for long-s / l / f confusion — check for crossbar
7. Count minims carefully for u/n/m/i sequences
8. Secretary 'r' can look like 'v' — compare to confirmed examples
9. Final d and g can look similar — check descender shape
10. Early modern numerals may not look like modern ones
