SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING HENSLOW MS688 SECRETARY HAND
==========================================================
Based on study of 5 training pages (pp. 8, 10, 14, 16, 20)

SECTION 1: LETTERFORM IDENTIFICATION
======================================

LOWERCASE LETTERS:
- a: rounded, sometimes open at top; can resemble o when hastily written
- b: tall ascender with rounded bowl at baseline
- c: small rounded form, open to the right; can resemble t in the middle of words
- d: looped ascender that curves back to the left (distinctive secretary d); the loop sometimes makes it look like g
- e: secretary e is distinctive — often looks like a reversed modern e, almost like a small backward loop
- f: tall letter WITH a crossbar; the crossbar distinguishes it from long-s and l
- g: descender curves back underneath; compare carefully to d (d loops left above, g curves below)
- h: tall ascender with a shoulder stroke descending to baseline
- i: single minim stroke; often has a dot or tick above but not always
- k: tall ascender with angular strokes at baseline
- l: tall letter, NO crossbar (distinguishes from f); can look like long-s
- m: three minim strokes — count carefully
- n: two minim strokes — count carefully; nn (four minims) can be confused with un or nu
- o: small rounded form at mid-height
- p: descender with rounded bowl at baseline
- r: in secretary hand, r can look like v — a short stroke with a shoulder; compare to confirmed r elsewhere
- s: TWO FORMS:
  * Long-s: used at start and middle of words; tall like l and f but NO crossbar and no flag
  * Round-s: used at word endings; looks like modern s
- t: short ascender with crossbar; can be confused with b (check for crossbar vs. full loop)
- u: single minim with curved bottom; looks identical to n — context and comparison are essential
- v: only at word starts in some conventions, but THIS scribe (Henslow) uses "u" at word starts too (use, untill, uppon) — do NOT automatically convert u to v
- w: distinctive wide letter made of angular strokes; ornate in this hand
- x: crossed strokes
- y: descender with a dot sometimes; "ytt" and "yow" are very common
- z: rare

CAPITAL LETTERS:
- A: large, often with angular initial stroke; the top curves over
- B: large rounded form with vertical stroke
- C: large sweeping curve
- D: large looped form
- F: written as ff (double-f convention); a single large F also appears but ff is standard
- G: large descending curve
- H: large angular form
- I: can look very different from lowercase; sometimes resembles other capitals
- L: large form with foot
- M: large angular form
- Q: large with long descending tail
- R: can look unlike modern R; sweeping forms; do NOT confuse with other capitals
- S: large sweeping initial curve
- T: large ornate form, distinctive cross-stroke; "Take" is the most common word starting recipes
- Y: large with distinctive descender

NUMERALS:
- Watch for: "2" can look like "a"; "1" can look like "&"
- When a character precedes a unit of measurement (ounces, spoonfuls), consider if it is a numeral

SECTION 2: MINIM RULES
========================
The letters u, n, m, and i are all made of short vertical strokes (minims):
- i = 1 minim
- u or n = 2 minims
- m = 3 minims

When minims cluster together, count them carefully:
- 4 minims could be: nn, un, nu, ni, in, uu, etc.
- 6 minims could be: mm, mun, num, etc.
Compare to confirmed instances elsewhere on the page.

SECTION 3: COMMON SPELLING PATTERNS IN THIS HAND
=================================================

DOUBLED CONSONANTS:
This scribe consistently doubles final consonants:
- putt (put), ytt/itt (it), pott (pot), lett (let), sitte (sit), knitt (knit)
- Always preserve the doubling — it is intentional

TERMINAL -E:
Many words end in -e that modern English drops:
- throte (throat), mouthe (mouth), colde (cold), warme (warm), owte (out)
- withe (with), eache (each), fyer (fire), bloude (blood)

DISTINCTIVE SPELLINGS:
- ytt / itt = "it" (used interchangeably)
- yow = "you"
- withe = "with"
- witheall = "withal"
- togeather = "together"
- euerie = "every"
- moreninge = "morning"
- eveninge / euenynde = "evening" / "eventide"
- annoynte = "anoint"
- straine = "strain"
- muche = "much"
- hunnye = "honey"
- seethe = "seethe/boil"
- spoonefull(s) = "spoonful(s)"
- handefull = "handful"
- verie = "very"
- probatum = "proven" (Latin tag at end of recipes)

-INGE ENDINGS:
This scribe uses -inge for -ing: runninge, stoppinge, moreninge, eveninge

SECTION 4: ABBREVIATIONS
==========================
This scribe uses relatively few abbreviations. Most words are written out in full.
- "probatum" appears as a complete Latin word at recipe ends
- Watch for: possible superscript abbreviation marks, but this scribe mostly spells things out
- Do NOT assume abbreviations like "yt" for "that" or "yr" for "your" unless you clearly see them

SECTION 5: LONG-S vs. F vs. L
===============================
This is one of the trickiest distinctions:
- Long-s: tall, NO crossbar, NO top serif/flag — appears at start and middle of words
- f: tall, HAS crossbar — the crossbar may be faint but is present
- l: tall, NO crossbar — may have a slight foot or serif at bottom

When you see a tall letter:
1. Look for a crossbar → if present, it is f
2. If no crossbar, consider context: does s or l make more sense?
3. Compare to confirmed instances of s, f, and l elsewhere on the page

SECTION 6: RECIPE STRUCTURE
============================
Recipes in this manuscript follow a consistent pattern:
1. Title line: "For [condition]" or "A [type] for [condition]" or "To [verb] [object]"
2. Body begins with "Take" (almost always)
3. Ingredients listed with quantities
4. Preparation instructions
5. Sometimes ends with "probatum" (proven)

Titles are often centered or set apart with decorative marks or spacing.
"Take" is sometimes written with an ornate capital T and often followed by a comma.

SECTION 7: KEY WARNINGS
=========================
1. Do NOT modernize spelling — preserve exactly what you see
2. Do NOT substitute familiar words for unfamiliar ones
3. COUNT MINIMS carefully in every word with u, n, m, i clusters
4. PRESERVE doubled consonants
5. Check d vs. g carefully at word endings (descender shape)
6. Watch for long-s/l/f confusion — check for crossbar
7. The scribe uses "u" at word beginnings (use, untill, uppon) — do NOT convert to v
8. Read letter by letter, THEN build the word
9. Mark genuine uncertainty with [?]
10. If a reading seems too smooth or modern, re-examine — early modern text should look strange
