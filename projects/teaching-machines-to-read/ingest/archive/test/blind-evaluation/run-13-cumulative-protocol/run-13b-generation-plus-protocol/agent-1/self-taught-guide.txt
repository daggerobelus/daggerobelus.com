SELF-TAUGHT GUIDE TO READING HENSLOW MS688 (1601)
Based on study of five pages (pp. 8, 10, 14, 16, 20)

================================================================
1. GENERAL SCRIPT CHARACTERISTICS
================================================================

This is an English secretary hand from 1601. The script is relatively
neat and consistent, with clear recipe headings in a slightly larger
or more decorative hand. The scribe writes in a flowing style with
frequent ligatures (connected letters).

================================================================
2. INDIVIDUAL LETTER FORMS
================================================================

LOWERCASE LETTERS:

a - Has a rounded form, sometimes open at the top. Can look like 'u'
    when written quickly.

b - Has a tall ascender with a curved bowl. Generally clear.

c - Small curved letter, open on the right. Can be confused with 'e'
    in some positions.

d - Uses the secretary 'd' with a looped ascender that curves back to
    the left (not the modern form). The loop can make it resemble a
    different letter entirely.

e - Small, often appears as a simple curved stroke. In secretary hand,
    'e' can look like a backward '3' or a small loop. Very commonly
    confused with 'c' or 'i'.

f - Has a descender below the line and an ascender above. Distinguished
    from long-s by having a crossbar. Often doubled at word beginnings:
    "ffasten" (p.8), "fower" (p.14), "fowre" (p.10), "figges" (p.16).

g - Has a descender with a loop or tail below the line. Generally
    recognizable.

h - Tall ascender, sometimes with a loop. The minim strokes in the
    lower part can be confused with 'li' or 'b'.

i - A single minim (short vertical stroke) with or without a dot.
    Easily confused with minims of m, n, u. Context is essential.

k - Has a tall ascender with an angular lower portion. Appears in
    "keepe," "take," "make."

l - Tall single stroke, sometimes with a small loop at top. Can be
    confused with 'b' or 'h' when the loop is present.

m - Three minims. Can be very hard to distinguish from 'ni', 'iu', etc.

n - Two minims. Easily confused with 'u' (also two minims). Context
    is the main way to distinguish them.

o - Round, generally clear. Sometimes slightly open at top.

p - Has a descender and a bowl. Generally clear, though the descender
    can be confused with 'q'.

q - Appears with its tail; seen in "quarte," "quantitye," "Quinsie."

r - Multiple forms: (1) a short stroke, sometimes with a shoulder;
    (2) after 'o', can appear as a '2'-shaped form. Both appear in
    this manuscript.

s - TWO FORMS:
    (1) Long-s: looks like 'f' but WITHOUT a full crossbar. Used at
        the start and middle of words: "seethe," "straine," "sodde."
    (2) Round-s: modern form, used at the end of words: "rootes,"
        "leaves," "ounces."
    CRITICAL: Do not confuse long-s with 'f'. Look for the crossbar.

t - Short vertical stroke with a crossbar. Can be hard to distinguish
    from 'c' when written carelessly.

u/v - In this period, 'u' and 'v' are positional variants:
    'v' typically appears at the start of words: "verie," "vntill,"
    "vinneger"
    'u' appears in the middle: "untill," "putt," "muche"
    The scribe is somewhat inconsistent: "untill" and "vntill" both
    appear.

w - Distinctive form, usually clear. Appears in "water," "waxeth,"
    "withe."

x - Rare. Appears in "waxeth" (p.14).

y - Has a descender; often used where modern English uses 'i':
    "ytt" (= it), "yow" (= you), "daye" (= day). Also used in
    "throte" variant "throte."

z - Rare in these pages.

UPPERCASE LETTERS:

A - Appears in "A watter" (p.8), "A Gargisine" (p.16), "Applye" (p.16).
    Decorative with swooping strokes.

B - Appears in "Bowlarmene" (p.8), "Broome" (p.8), "Boyle" (p.20).
    Large rounded form.

C - Appears in "Cinkfoud" (p.14). Curved, can be ornate.

D - Appears in "Decayed" (p.8), "Docke" (p.10). Has a distinctive
    looped form.

F - Appears in "For" (many instances), "Firste" (p.14), "Fistula" (p.20).
    Large with a sweeping descender.

G - Appears in "Gargisine" (p.16). Decorative.

H - Appears in "Halle" (p.20). Clear tall form.

L - Appears in "Liver" (p.10), "Longes" (p.10), "Laye" (p.20).

M - Appears in "Make" (p.20), "Marye" (p.10).

Q - Appears in "Quinsie" (p.14, 16), "Queene" (p.10). Very decorative
    with a large swooping tail.

R - Appears in "Redd" (p.20), "Reasons" (p.10).

S - Appears in "Sunne" (p.16). Can be ornate.

T - Appears in "Take" (many instances), "To" (p.10, 20), "This" (p.20).

Y - Appears in "Yow" (p.20). Has a long descending tail.

================================================================
3. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS
================================================================

This manuscript uses relatively few abbreviations:

- "ytt" = "it" — very consistent throughout
- "yow" = "you" — consistent
- "att" = "at" (p.16: "att a tyme")
- Possible truncation with marks — in p.14, "folow[eth]" appears
  to be cut off at line end, suggesting the scribe sometimes
  abbreviated final syllables.
- "[ounces]" in p.16 may be an abbreviation symbol for the word

================================================================
4. SPELLING CONVENTIONS
================================================================

DOUBLE LETTERS: The scribe frequently doubles consonants:
- Double f at word start: "ffasten" (p.8)
- Double t: "putt," "ytt," "att," "butt," "nott"
- Double l: "wille," "stille," "untille," "vntill"
- Double e: "teethe," "seethe," "keepe," "sweete," "barlye"
  Note: words ending in silent-e often have double e before final
  consonant clusters.
- Double o: "toothe," "togeather" (but only one o), "goode"

CHARACTERISTIC SPELLINGS:
- "withe" = with
- "muche" = much
- "eache" = each
- "hotte" / "hott" = hot
- "colde" = cold
- "owte" = out
- "thereto" / "thereof" / "therein" — compound prepositions preserved
- "moreninge" = morning
- "eveninge" = evening
- "boddye" = body
- "annoynte" = anoint
- "-inge" ending very common: "runninge," "stoppinge," "swellinge"
- "bee" = be
- "soe" or "so" = so (both appear)
- "doe" or "dothe" = do/does
- "bloude" = blood
- "breade" = bread
- "greace" / "grease" — both forms appear
- "verie" = very
- "fier" = fire
- "fyne" / "fine" = fine (both appear)

WORD BOUNDARIES:
- "togeather" = together (written as one word)
- "allreadye" = already (one word)
- "withein" = within
- "withowt" = without
- "thereto" = thereto (one word)
- "thereof" = thereof (one word)
- "undernethe" = underneath

================================================================
5. COMMON VOCABULARY IN RECIPE CONTEXT
================================================================

Recipe structure words:
- "Take" — begins almost every recipe
- "probatum" — Latin, meaning "proved/tested," marks end of recipe
- "boyle" / "boyled" = boil
- "seethe" / "sodde" = boil/boiled (archaic)
- "straine" = strain
- "stampe" = stamp/pound (to crush)
- "bruse" = bruise (to crush)
- "stille" = distill
- "annoynte" = anoint
- "gargole" / "gargolle" = gargle

Measurements and quantities:
- "handefull" = handful
- "spoonefull" / "spoonefulls" = spoonful(s)
- "quarte" = quart
- "pottell" = pottle (half gallon)
- "quartene" = quartern
- "ounce" / "ounces"
- "pinte" = pint

- "coorrance" = currants
- "malmesye" = malmsey (wine)

- "cammamell" = chamomile
- "Archangell" = archangel (plant: dead-nettle)
- "perwinkell" = periwinkle
- "Cinkfoud" = cinquefoil (plant)

- "barrowes grease" = boar's grease

Body parts:

- "longes" = lungs

- "chinne" = chin

- "arme" = arm

================================================================
6. PUNCTUATION AND FORMATTING
================================================================

- Colons (:) used as the primary pause mark, more common than periods
- Periods appear but are less common
- Commas appear after "Take" and between list items
- Recipe titles are written in a slightly larger, more decorative hand
- Decorative dots or dashes sometimes appear around titles
- Line breaks within recipes do NOT correspond to sentence breaks —
  the text flows continuously across lines
- "probatum" appears at the end of some recipes as validation

================================================================
7. CONFUSABLE LETTER PAIRS (CRITICAL)
================================================================

These are the most dangerous confusions in this hand:

1. Long-s vs. f — Both have ascenders and descenders. Look for the
   crossbar: 'f' has a clear crossbar; long-s does not (or has only
   a partial one on the left side).

2. c vs. e — Both are small curved letters. 'e' tends to have a more
   closed or looped form.

3. u vs. n — Both are two minims. 'u' has a curve at the bottom;
   'n' has a curve at the top. In practice, context is often the
   only reliable guide.

4. m vs. in/ni — 'm' is three minims; 'in' or 'ni' is also
   effectively three minims. Again, context matters.

5. Secretary 'd' vs. 'e' — The looped ascender of secretary 'd' can
   look like an 'e' attached to the previous letter.

6. 'y' vs. 'p' — Both have descenders. 'y' tends to have a more
   angular or forked top; 'p' has a rounder bowl.

7. Double-f ("ff") vs. capital F — At word beginnings, "ff" is the
   scribe's way of writing what we'd consider a capital F.
   Example: "ffasten" (p.8).

================================================================
8. TIPS FOR TRANSCRIPTION
================================================================

1. Always read for SENSE first. If a word doesn't make sense in the
   recipe context, re-examine the letter forms.

2. Check the recipe structure: "Take [ingredient], [ingredient]...
   and [verb] them..." This pattern can help identify unclear words.

3. When encountering an unclear word, check if it appears elsewhere
   in the manuscript in a clearer form.

4. Preserve ALL original spellings, even when inconsistent (the scribe
   spells the same word differently at times).

5. Watch for the "-inge" suffix — it's extremely common and can help
   you identify word endings.

6. The scribe uses "ytt" consistently for "it" and "yow" for "you."

7. "withe" (= with) appears constantly; do not modernize it.

8. Latin terms like "probatum" should be transcribed as written.

9. Pay attention to word-initial 'v' for what we'd spell with 'u':
   "verie," "vntill," "vnto."

10. The scribe's 'w' is distinctive and generally clear — it should
    not be confused with other letters.
