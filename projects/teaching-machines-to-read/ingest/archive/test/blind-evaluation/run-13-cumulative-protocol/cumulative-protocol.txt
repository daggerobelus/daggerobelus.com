CUMULATIVE ERROR PROTOCOL FOR EARLY MODERN MANUSCRIPT TRANSCRIPTION
====================================================================
Compiled from blind transcription errors on two manuscripts:
  - Henslow MS688 (1601 recipe book, secretary hand)
  - Sedley MS534 (early modern recipe book, different scribe)

This protocol synthesizes lessons from both manuscripts. It is designed
for transcribing a new, unseen manuscript (Brumwich MS160).

Priority levels:
  [UNIVERSAL]  = error appeared in BOTH manuscripts (highest priority)
  [RECURRING]  = appeared in one manuscript but likely general (medium)
  [SCRIBE-SPECIFIC] = tied to one scribe; may not apply (lower, but noted)

====================================================================
SECTION 1: CRITICAL MINDSET RULES
====================================================================
These behavioral rules address the most damaging error patterns found
across both manuscripts. Internalize these before you begin.

RULE 1: TRANSCRIBE WHAT YOU SEE, NOT WHAT YOU EXPECT [UNIVERSAL]
  Both manuscripts showed agents substituting familiar words for
  unfamiliar ones. On Henslow, "tarre" became "burne" because burning
  makes sense in a recipe. On Sedley, "fundaments" became "forehead"
  because it sounded medical. In both cases, agents read for meaning
  instead of reading for form. Your job is to decode letterforms, not
  to write plausible recipes. If your reading seems strange, that may
  mean you got it RIGHT — early modern spelling is strange.

RULE 2: DO NOT NORMALIZE SPELLING [UNIVERSAL]
  The single most common error category across both manuscripts was
  agents "correcting" the scribe's spelling toward modern forms:
    - Doubled consonants reduced: putt->put, ytt->yt, pott->pot
    - Unusual vowel sequences changed: voilett->violett, trenchar->trencher
    - Archaic forms modernized: euenynde->euenynge, moreninge->morninge
    - Word boundaries regularized: Takeone->Take one
  PRESERVE THE EXACT SPELLING. If the scribe writes "putt," you write
  "putt." If it looks like a double letter, transcribe the double letter.
  When in doubt, keep what you see rather than what looks "normal."

RULE 3: UNKNOWN WORDS ARE NOT ERRORS — THEY ARE INFORMATION [UNIVERSAL]
  On Henslow, "langdebeeffe" (bugloss) was mangled into "land a goose"
  and "and a greate" because agents did not know the word. On Sedley,
  "calcinated" became "centred/seirced/sifted" and "Alcemus" became
  "Nutmeggs." When you encounter a word you do not recognize, DO NOT
  replace it with a familiar word. Instead:
    (a) Sound it out letter by letter
    (b) Write down what the letters spell, even if it is not a word
        you know
    (c) Check the vocabulary list in Section 3 below
    (d) If still uncertain, transcribe your best letter-by-letter
        reading and flag it with [?]

RULE 4: CONSENSUS DOES NOT MEAN ACCURACY [UNIVERSAL]
  On Sedley, ALL FIVE agents read "glory" where the manuscript said
  "older the better." All five read "Mr Nowill" where it said "A woman."
  On Henslow, ALL FIVE read "Caunterberries" for "Cannterberries."
  Multiple agents converging on the same wrong answer is a known failure
  mode. Do not assume your reading is correct because it "sounds right."
  Always verify against the actual letterforms.

RULE 5: READ LETTER BY LETTER, THEN BUILD THE WORD [UNIVERSAL]
  Both error analyses found that agents grasp an approximate visual
  impression and then generate the most contextually plausible word.
  A paleographer does the opposite: decipher each letter individually,
  then assemble them into a word, then check whether that word makes
  sense. When a passage is difficult, slow down and go letter by letter.
  Compare each letterform to confirmed examples elsewhere on the page.

RULE 6: MARK GENUINE UNCERTAINTY HONESTLY [UNIVERSAL]
  On Sedley, agents produced confident wrong readings instead of
  admitting uncertainty. "Comforts appetites" for "provokes appetite"
  sounds plausible but is wrong. On Henslow, agents wrote "burne" for
  "tarre" with no hesitation marker. If you cannot confidently decipher
  a word, use [...] for illegible text or [word?] for uncertain readings.
  An honest gap is better than a confident fabrication.

RULE 7: DO NOT OVER-APPLY PALEOGRAPHIC RULES [UNIVERSAL]
  On Henslow, agents over-applied the u/v positional convention,
  "correcting" genuine initial-u words to v: use->vse, uppon->vppon,
  untill->vntill. On Sedley, agents over-read abbreviation marks,
  writing "yt" for "then" and "yr" for "your" when the manuscript had
  the full words. The manuscript is the authority, not the rule. If the
  scribe wrote "use" with a u, transcribe "use." If the scribe wrote
  "then" in full, transcribe "then." Rules describe tendencies, not
  laws.

RULE 8: WATCH FOR EYE-SKIP IN REPETITIVE PASSAGES [RECURRING - Sedley]
  On Sedley, when multiple body parts appeared in quick succession
  (belly, reines, back, stomach), agents jumped between lines and
  merged separate clauses. In recipe manuscripts, ingredients and body
  parts repeat frequently. When you notice repetition, slow down and
  track exactly which word belongs to which line.

SECTION 2: SPECIFIC LETTERFORM WARNINGS
====================================================================
Confusions documented across both manuscripts. The more manuscripts
a confusion appears in, the more likely it is general to secretary hand.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2A. HIGH PRIORITY — Appeared in BOTH manuscripts [UNIVERSAL]
----------------------------------------------------------------------

MINIM CONFUSION (u, n, m, i)
  Minims are the short vertical strokes that make up u, n, m, and i.
  In secretary hand they can be nearly identical.
    - nn vs. un: "Cannterberries" read as "Caunterberries" (Henslow)
    - u vs. o adjacent to minims: "Buorrage" read as "Boorrage" (Henslow);
      similar minim-area confusion on Sedley
    - "them" read as "yt" or "yr" (Sedley) — minim sequence misinterpreted
  RULE: When you see a cluster of minims, count them carefully. Compare
  to the same letter combinations elsewhere in the manuscript.

DOUBLED CONSONANTS DROPPED
  Both manuscripts showed agents reducing double consonants to single:
    - Henslow: putt->put, ytt->yt, knitt->knit, sitte->sit
    - Sedley: pott->pot
  RULE: Look carefully for doubled letters. If you see what might be
  two t's or two l's, transcribe both. The scribe is consistent about
  doubling — learn the pattern early and apply it throughout.

SPELLING NORMALIZATION (general)
  Both manuscripts showed agents modernizing early modern spellings:
    - Henslow: moreninge->morninge, euenynde->euenynge, woole->wolle
    - Sedley: trenchar->trencher, oliue->olive
  RULE: Preserve the scribe's exact spelling. If it looks archaic or
  unusual, that is probably what the scribe wrote.

d vs. g AT WORD ENDINGS
  - Henslow: "euenynde" universally read as "euenynge" (d->g)
  RULE: Final d and g can look similar. Check the downstroke: d typically
  has a straighter descender; g curves back. Compare to confirmed d's
  and g's elsewhere.

VOCABULARY-DRIVEN SUBSTITUTION
  Both manuscripts showed agents replacing unknown words with familiar
  ones:
    - Henslow: langdebeeffe->land a goose; tarre->burne
    - Sedley: calcinated->centred; Alcemus->Nutmeggs; fundaments->forehead
  RULE: If your reading produces a common English word where a rare one
  might belong, double-check. Recipe books contain specialized terminology.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2B. MEDIUM PRIORITY — Appeared in one manuscript but likely general
----------------------------------------------------------------------

LONG-s CONFUSION
  - Long-s vs. l: "sitte" read as "lye" (Henslow). The guide warns about
    long-s vs. f, but long-s vs. l is equally dangerous.
  - Long-s vs. f: standard warning (both manuscripts)
  RULE: Long-s is tall like l and f but has no crossbar (unlike f) and
  no serif/flag at top (unlike l in some hands). When you see a tall
  letter at the start of a word, consider all three possibilities: s, f, l.

t vs. b AT WORD STARTS [RECURRING - Henslow]
  - "tarre" read as "burne" / "bare" by 3/5 agents
  RULE: Initial t and b can look similar when the crossbar of t is faint.
  Check the ascender shape.

DOUBLE-f (ff) IN MEDIAL POSITION [RECURRING - Henslow]
  - "Deffnes" was catastrophically misread by all 5 agents
  - Agents know ff = capital F at word beginnings, but not in the middle
    of words
  RULE: Double-f can appear mid-word (e.g., Deffnes = Deafness, stiffnes,
  offring). Do not assume ff only occurs at the start of words.

r vs. v [RECURRING - Sedley]
  - "reines" read as "veines" by 3/5 agents
  RULE: Secretary-hand r can look like v. When you read a "v," check
  whether "r" would make sense. "Reines" (kidneys) and "veines" are
  both medical terms — only letterform analysis, not context, can
  distinguish them.

NUMERAL CONFUSION [RECURRING - Sedley]
  - "2" consistently read as "a" (5/5 agents, multiple instances)
  - "1" read as "&" (5/5 agents)
  RULE: Early modern numerals can look very different from modern ones.
  The numeral 2 may resemble a lowercase "a"; the numeral 1 may
  resemble an ampersand. When you see "a" or "&" before a unit of
  measurement (ounces, pounds, spoonfuls), consider whether it might
  be a numeral instead.

CAPITAL I / INITIAL LETTER CONFUSION [RECURRING - Sedley]
  - "Iron" read as "boxe" / "browne" (5/5 agents)
  - Capital R in "Runinge" read as "Op" in "Openings" (5/5 agents)
  - "A woman" read as "Mr Nowill" (5/5 agents)
  RULE: Capital letters in secretary hand can look radically different
  from their modern forms. When you see a word-initial letterform that
  seems odd, consider alternative capital letters.

c vs. t [RECURRING - Sedley]
  - "especially" read as "espetially" by 2/5 agents
  RULE: Secretary c and t can be confused, especially in the middle of
  words.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2C. LOWER PRIORITY — Scribe-specific but worth noting
----------------------------------------------------------------------

oo vs. ol [SCRIBE-SPECIFIC - Henslow]
  - "woole" read as "wolle" by 4/5 agents
  In a hand where o and l are similar height, this confusion may recur.

d vs. l [SCRIBE-SPECIFIC - Henslow]
  - "horsedounge" read as "horselounge" by 3/5 agents
  Similar to long-s/l confusion but involves d.

w vs. sm [SCRIBE-SPECIFIC - Sedley]
  - "wash" read as "smale" / "smalt" by 3/5 agents
  If the new manuscript's w has angular strokes, this may recur.

SECTION 4: SCRIBE HABIT DETECTION
====================================================================
Before transcribing the full manuscript, study the page and identify
the scribe's personal habits. Every scribe is different. Spend time
on the following checks BEFORE you begin your transcription.

STEP 1: BUILD A LETTERFORM ALPHABET
  Find words you can identify with confidence (recipe titles, common
  words like "Take," "the," "and"). Use these to establish what each
  letter looks like in THIS scribe's hand. Pay special attention to:
    - How does this scribe write: s (long-s vs. round-s), f, l?
    - How does this scribe write: u, n, m, i (the minims)?
    - How does this scribe write: d, g, r, v?
    - How does this scribe write: t, b, c?
    - How does this scribe write capital letters (especially F, R, I, A)?
    - How does this scribe write numerals (1, 2, 3, 4, etc.)?

STEP 2: CHECK FOR DOUBLED CONSONANTS
  Look at a few confirmed words. Does this scribe double final
  consonants (putt, ytt, pott)? If so, expect that pattern throughout
  and preserve it.

STEP 3: CHECK u/v AND i/j CONVENTIONS
  Does this scribe use v at the start of words and u in the middle?
  Or does the scribe use u everywhere? Do not impose a convention the
  scribe does not follow.

STEP 4: CHECK ABBREVIATION USAGE
  Does this scribe abbreviate "the," "that," "your," "them"? Or does
  the scribe write them out in full? If the scribe writes full words,
  do not transcribe abbreviations.

STEP 5: CHECK WORD SPACING
  Does this scribe run words together (like "Takeone")? If so, preserve
  the manuscript spacing rather than imposing modern word boundaries.

STEP 6: CHECK CAPITALIZATION PATTERNS
  Does this scribe capitalize ingredient nouns? Recipe titles? Only
  sentence-initial words? Do not over-capitalize or under-capitalize
  based on your expectations.

STEP 7: CHECK PUNCTUATION
  Does this scribe use periods, commas, or other marks? Note their
  forms and preserve them. Do not drop punctuation you can see.


====================================================================
SECTION 5: POST-TRANSCRIPTION VERIFICATION CHECKLIST
====================================================================
After completing your transcription, review it against this checklist.
Items marked [UNIVERSAL] caught errors on both manuscripts and are
highest priority.

[ ] DOUBLED CONSONANTS CHECK [UNIVERSAL]
    Search your transcription for common words: put, it, sit, knit, pot.
    Did you preserve doubled consonants where the manuscript has them?
    If the scribe doubles final consonants, every instance should be
    doubled in your transcription.

[ ] NORMALIZATION CHECK [UNIVERSAL]
    Read through your transcription looking for any word where you
    chose the "modern" spelling over what the manuscript shows. Flag
    these and re-examine the manuscript. Common traps:
      - Vowel sequences (oi vs. io, uo vs. oo, ea vs. e)
      - Word endings (-ar vs. -er, -de vs. -ge, -inge vs. -ing)
      - Doubled vs. single letters

[ ] UNKNOWN WORD CHECK [UNIVERSAL]
    Are there any words you replaced with a more familiar synonym?
    Go back and try reading them letter by letter again. Check
    against the vocabulary list in Section 3.

[ ] VOCABULARY CROSS-REFERENCE [UNIVERSAL]
    Scan your transcription for these common recipe-book categories:
      - Herbs and plants (check Section 3A)
      - Body parts and diseases (check Section 3B)
      - Chemical/pharmaceutical terms (check Section 3C)
    Do any of your readings seem like they should be one of these terms
    but are slightly off? Re-examine those words.

[ ] NUMERAL CHECK [RECURRING - Sedley]
    Look for every "a" or "&" that appears before a unit of measurement
    (ounces, pounds, spoonfuls, pennyworth, dramme). Could any of these
    be numerals (1, 2, 3, etc.) instead?

[ ] u/v CHECK [RECURRING - Henslow]
    Did you write "vse," "vppon," or "vntill" anywhere? Go back and
    check whether the manuscript actually has a v or a u. Do not impose
    the v-initial convention if the scribe does not use it.

[ ] ABBREVIATION CHECK [RECURRING - Sedley]
    Did you write "yt," "yr," or other abbreviations? Check whether the
    manuscript actually uses abbreviation marks or writes the full word.

[ ] LONG-s CHECK [RECURRING - Henslow]
    Did you write any words starting with l or f where the initial
    letter might be long-s instead? Especially check: any word where
    "l" + following letters could spell a different word with "s."

[ ] CAPITAL LETTER CHECK [UNIVERSAL]
    Review your capital letters. Did you:
      - Capitalize words the manuscript leaves lowercase? (Henslow:
        "strawberrie" over-capitalized; Henslow: "Flixe" under-capitalized)
      - Recognize the ff = capital F convention correctly?
      - Identify unusual capital forms (I, R, A are especially tricky)?

[ ] WORD BOUNDARY CHECK [RECURRING - Henslow]
    Did you split any words that the manuscript writes as one? Did you
    join any words that the manuscript writes separately?

[ ] PUNCTUATION CHECK [UNIVERSAL]
    Did you preserve all periods, commas, and other punctuation marks
    visible in the manuscript? Do not drop punctuation.

[ ] LINE-SKIP CHECK [RECURRING - Sedley]
    In passages with repetitive content (lists of ingredients, body
    parts, remedy applications), verify you have not merged text from
    different lines or skipped a line.

[ ] SEMANTIC PLAUSIBILITY RED FLAG [UNIVERSAL]
    Read your transcription once for sense. If everything reads
    smoothly and makes perfect modern sense, BE SUSPICIOUS. Early
    modern manuscripts contain unfamiliar words, strange spellings,
    and archaic constructions. A transcription that reads too
    smoothly may be over-normalized.

SECTION 6: DECISION FRAMEWORK FOR AMBIGUOUS LETTERFORMS
====================================================================
When you encounter a letterform you cannot confidently identify,
follow this procedure:

STEP 1: ISOLATE THE LETTER
  Focus on the single ambiguous letterform. What are the possible
  letters it could be? List them.

STEP 2: COMPARE WITHIN THE MANUSCRIPT
  Find the same letter in a word you CAN read with confidence.
  Does it match? Check multiple confirmed examples — scribes vary
  their own letterforms, so one comparison is not enough.

STEP 3: TRY ALL CANDIDATE READINGS
  For each possible letter, what word would result? Write out each
  possibility. For example, if you are unsure whether a letter is
  d or g:
    - With d: "euenynde"
    - With g: "euenynge"
  Do NOT just pick the one that sounds more familiar.

STEP 4: CHECK AGAINST VOCABULARY
  Does any candidate reading match a known early modern term from
  Section 3? If one reading produces a recognized period word and
  the other produces a common modern word, the period word may be
  correct.

STEP 5: CHECK AGAINST SCRIBE HABITS
  Does the scribe have a habit that favors one reading? For example:
    - If the scribe consistently doubles final consonants, and one
      reading has a doubled consonant, favor that reading.
    - If the scribe consistently uses a particular letter at word
      endings, favor the reading that matches the habit.

STEP 6: APPLY CONTEXTUAL REASONING (with caution)
  Context can help ONLY when letterform analysis is truly inconclusive.
  Use context to choose between two letterform-plausible readings, NOT
  to override what the letters clearly show. Beware of:
    - Choosing the more familiar word (this was wrong more often than right)
    - Choosing a word that "makes sense" over one that looks right
    - Substituting a medical synonym (e.g., "Comforts" for "provokes")

STEP 7: IF STILL UNCERTAIN, FLAG IT
  Write your best letter-by-letter reading followed by [?]. This is
  always better than guessing confidently. Example: "calcinated [?]"
  A wrong answer with no flag is worse than a right answer with a flag.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
QUICK REFERENCE — MOST DANGEROUS CONFUSIONS (both manuscripts):
----------------------------------------------------------------------
  long-s / l / f     — check crossbar (f has one, s and l do not)
  u / n / m / i      — count minims carefully, compare to confirmed forms
  d / g              — check descender shape (straight vs. curved)
  r / v              — secretary r can look like v; compare to confirmed r
  t / b              — check ascender and crossbar
  nn / un / nu       — count minims; 4 minims could be nn, un, or nu
  oo / ol / uo       — compare o-height to l-height in this hand
  ff (medial)        — can appear mid-word, not just as capital-F convention
  numerals / letters — 2 resembles "a"; 1 resembles "&"
  Capital I / b / long-s — capital I can look very different from lowercase
  Capital R / Op     — capital R may not look like modern R
