HAND-SPECIFIC ALPHABET CHART
Manuscript: Bulkeley MS 169, Page 17
================================================================

GENERAL OBSERVATIONS:
This is a clear, well-formed mixed hand (secretary/italic) from the
late 16th or early 17th century. The writing is relatively large and
well-spaced, making it one of the more legible hands in this set.
The text appears to be copied from a printed herbal (likely Gerard's
Herball, 1597) about lavender spike. The scribe writes with a fairly
consistent hand, with brown ink on paper. Line spacing is generous.
The page is numbered "7" at the top right.

a:

- Example words: "Lavander" (line 1), "and" (throughout), "against" (line 5),

COMMON COMBINATIONS AND LIGATURES
================================================================

th: Very common. 't' and 'h' are often closely joined. The scribe does
    not appear to use thorn on this page — 'th' is spelled out.

-ing/-inge: Very common ending. The 'ing' is written clearly with
    the 'g' descending below the line.

-es: Terminal '-es' is generally spelled out rather than using the -es graph.

&: Ampersand is used frequently ("& drye", "& spirituall", "& that").

================================================================
DISTINCTIVE FEATURES OF THIS HAND
================================================================

1. Very clear, well-spaced hand — more legible than many secretary hands.
2. Mixed hand combining secretary 'd', 'e', 'r', 's' with italic features.
3. Generous line spacing reduces interference from ascenders/descenders
   of adjacent lines.
4. Consistent letterforms — the scribe does not show much variation,
   suggesting a careful, deliberate copying hand.
5. Punctuation includes periods and commas; ampersand (&) used for "and".
6. The text appears to be copied from a printed herbal source.
7. Page numbered "7" in the top right corner.

================================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (highest to lowest)
================================================================

1. e/o — HIGHEST RISK
   Why: The secretary 'e' has a round loop that can closely resemble 'o',
   especially in words where context does not immediately resolve ambiguity.
   Distinguishing features: 'e' tends to have a slight point or angle on
   the left side of the loop; 'o' is more uniformly round.

2. long-s/f — HIGH RISK
   Why: Both have tall vertical strokes. The crossbar on 'f' is the
   distinguishing mark, but it can be subtle.
   Distinguishing features: Look for crossbar strokes on both sides of
   the vertical for 'f'; long-s lacks this.

3. MINIMS (i/n/m/u) — MODERATE-HIGH RISK
   Why: Minim confusion is inherent to secretary hand. However, this
   scribe's relatively wide spacing mitigates the risk somewhat.
   Distinguishing features: Spacing, dots over 'i' (inconsistent),
   word-shape.

4. Secretary r/v — MODERATE RISK
   Why: The 2-shaped secretary 'r' can look like 'v'.
   Distinguishing features: 'r' is more compact; 'v' has a wider opening.

5. c/t — MODERATE RISK
   Why: When the crossbar on 't' is faint, it can resemble 'c'.
   Distinguishing features: The crossbar on 't'.

6. d loop / e+l — LOW-MODERATE RISK
   Why: Secretary 'd' with its characteristic loop could be misread
   as 'cl' or 'el'.
   Distinguishing features: The loop is a single continuous stroke.

7. y/thorn — LOW RISK
   Why: Thorn resembles 'y' and represents 'th'. However, this scribe
   appears to spell out 'th' rather than use thorn.
   Distinguishing features: Context resolves this; thorn precedes
   common words.

8. g/y descenders — LOW RISK
   Why: Both have descending strokes, but 'g' has a clear bowl above
   the line.
   Distinguishing features: The bowl of 'g' is distinctive.
