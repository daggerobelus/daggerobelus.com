My lorde of Caunterberries
.........Brothe for a Consumptione..........
Take like as before sauinge putt thereto a handfull of violett leaues
langdebeefe leaues Borrage leaues and Strawberrie leaues, knitt
togeather of euerie one a like quantitye...........

..........For the pyles..........
Take one handefull of mollet leaues, and wrappe them in a Coleworte
leafe, and rake them in the [embers?] vntill they bee well rosted
then take them, and putt awaye the Coleworte leafe, and grinde
them in a morter, and putt to yt oyle of roses, and grinde them togeather
then take it and laye itt to the pyles withe a trusser, and vse this
morninge and euenynge...........

...........For the flixe...........
Take horsedounge new made and frie itt withe sweete butter
and putt it in a clothe and laye itt to the fundament: as hott
as you maye suffer ytt, and sitt vppon vntill you haste ease.

...........For Deafnes..........
Take blacke woole whiche growes vppon the flanke
of a sheepe and [hare?], and mingell them togeather
and putt them into the eare.

================================================================
CONFIDENCE NOTES
================================================================

General: This is one of the most legible manuscripts in the set. The large,
bold hand with generous spacing makes most words readable with high
confidence. The page contains four recipes attributed to "My lorde of
Caunterberries" (the Lord of Canterbury — likely the Archbishop).

Specific notes:

- "Caunterberries" (line 1): High confidence. The letterforms clearly
  show C-a-u-n-t-e-r-b-e-r-r-i-e-s. This is an early modern spelling
  of "Canterbury's." "canterbury" is attested in the vocab reference.

- "sauinge" (line 3): High confidence. Clearly readable. Means "saving"
  or "except" — i.e., "Take like as before, except put thereto..."

- "langdebeefe" (line 4): High confidence for the letterforms. This is
  "langue de boeuf" (ox tongue / bugloss), a plant name. Not found as
  a single word in the vocab reference, but the letterforms are very clear.

- "knitt" (line 5): High confidence. Doubled consonant clearly visible.
  The word means "knit/tie together."

- "mollet" (line 7): Moderate-high confidence. The letterforms show
  m-o-l-l-e-t. This likely refers to mullein (Verbascum), a plant
  traditionally used for piles. "mullet"/"mullett" are attested in the
  vocab reference. The 'o' vs 'u' distinction is the main uncertainty —
  the scribe may have written 'u' which I am reading as 'o'. If "mullet"
  is the intended reading, the letterforms could support it (minim
  confusion between o and u).

- "[embers?]" (line 8): Moderate confidence. The word appears to read
  "embers" (referring to hot coals), confirmed in vocab reference. The
  initial letter is partially obscured by the descender from the line
  above. Reading "embers" based on context (roasting in coals) and
  visible letterforms.

- "grinde them togeather" (line 10): High confidence. Both words clearly
  readable. "grinde" confirmed in vocab reference. "togeather" confirmed
  in vocab reference.

- "trusser" (line 11): High confidence for the letterforms (t-r-u-s-s-e-r).
  Not found in vocab reference as "trusser" but "truss"/"trusse" are
  attested. A trusser would be a cloth or bandage for applying a poultice.

- "horsedounge" (line 14): High confidence. Clearly readable. Confirmed
  in vocab reference.

- "fundament" (line 15): High confidence. Clearly readable. Confirmed
  in vocab reference. Refers to the buttocks/anus.

- "haste" (line 16): High confidence. Confirmed in vocab reference. The
  spelling "haste" (meaning "have") is an early modern form.

- "[hare?]" (line 18): Low-moderate confidence. The word after "and"
  before the comma is partially unclear. It could be "hare" (as in
  rabbit fur) or another word. "hare" is attested in the vocab reference.
  The context (mixing with black wool) could support either "hare" (hair
  of a hare) or another reading.

- "eare" (line 19): High confidence. Confirmed in vocab reference.
