REVISED TRANSCRIPTION PROTOCOL
================================================================================
Based on systematic error analysis of 5 independent blind transcriptions.
To be used alongside the Self-Taught Paleography Guide.
================================================================================

This protocol addresses specific, documented error patterns found when agents
transcribed early modern English manuscripts using only the self-taught guide.
Read this BEFORE beginning your transcription. Return to the verification
checklist AFTER completing your first draft.

================================================================================
PART 1: CRITICAL MINDSET RULES
================================================================================

RULE 1: PRESERVE, DO NOT NORMALIZE
Your #1 job is to write down EXACTLY what the scribe wrote, even when it
looks "wrong" by modern standards or differs from expected early modern
conventions. If you think you see "putt" with two t's, write "putt" — do
not "correct" it to "put." If the scribe wrote "voilett" and not "violett,"
write "voilett."

Documented problem: In testing, ALL five agents systematically normalized
unusual spellings. Every doubled consonant was reduced (putt->put, itt->it,
ytt->yt, knitt->knit). Every unusual vowel sequence was changed to a
familiar one (voilett->violett, moreninge->morninge). This was the single
largest source of error.

RULE 2: THE MANUSCRIPT IS THE AUTHORITY, NOT THE RULE
The self-taught guide describes common conventions (like v at word starts,
u in the middle). But individual scribes may not follow these conventions.
If the manuscript clearly shows a "u" at the start of a word, write "u" —
do NOT change it to "v" because the guide says v goes at the beginning.

Documented problem: All five agents over-applied the u/v rule, changing
"use" to "vse," "uppon" to "vppon," and "untill" to "vntill" when the
scribe actually wrote "u." The guide describes a tendency, not a law.

RULE 3: PRESERVE WORD BOUNDARIES AS WRITTEN
If two words are run together in the manuscript with no space, transcribe
them run together. If a word is split by a space in the manuscript, preserve
the split. Do not impose modern word spacing.

Documented problem: "Takeone" (no space in manuscript) was split into
"Take one" by all five agents. "alike" was split into "a like" by four
agents.

RULE 4: WHEN IN DOUBT, WRITE WHAT YOU SEE, NOT WHAT YOU EXPECT
If you see a letterform and your brain says "that must be X because the
word should be Y," STOP. Look at the actual letterform again. Is it really
X, or does it look more like Z? Your expectation of what word "should" be
there is the enemy of accurate transcription.


================================================================================
PART 2: SPECIFIC LETTERFORM WARNINGS
================================================================================

These warnings address specific confusions documented in testing. Read each
one carefully.

WARNING 1: LONG S CAN LOOK LIKE "L" (not just "f")
The self-taught guide warns extensively about long-s vs. f confusion. But
in testing, the more dangerous confusion was long-s vs. L. When a word
begins with what looks like "l," consider whether it might be a long-s.

Test: Does the word make sense with "s"? Does it make sense with "l"?
Example from testing: "sitte" was read as "lye" by 4/5 agents because
the long-s looked like an "l" and the double-t minims were then misread
as "y." Always check: could this initial "l" actually be a long-s?

WARNING 2: DOUBLE-F (FF) IN THE MIDDLE OF WORDS
The guide discusses "ff" as a way to write capital F at the start of words.
But "ff" also appears in the MIDDLE of words (e.g., "Deffnes" = deafness,
"langdebeeffe" = langdebeef). When you see what looks like a confusing
cluster of tall strokes in the middle of a word, consider whether it might
be "ff." Two f-like strokes together = ff.

Documented problem: "Deffnes" was read as "Deafnes," "Diues," "Difues,"
"Dflues," and other garbled forms because agents could not parse the
medial "ff."

WARNING 3: D vs. G AT WORD ENDINGS
In some hands, final "d" and "g" can look very similar. When a word ends
in what looks like "g," also consider "d."

Documented problem: "euenynde" (an archaic spelling of "evening") was
universally read as "euenynge" because agents expected a "-g" ending.
The scribe actually wrote a "d."

WARNING 4: T vs. B AT WORD BEGINNINGS
The letters "t" and "b" can be confused — both have vertical ascenders.
The key difference: "b" has a bowl/loop to the right at the baseline;
"t" has a crossbar. If you're unsure, look for the crossbar.

Documented problem: "tarre" (= tar) was read as "burne" by 3/5 agents.
The "t" was read as "b," and then agents confabulated a plausible recipe
word ("burn") to make sense of the remaining letters.

WARNING 5: OO vs. OL vs. UO (ROUND-LETTER MINIM CONFUSION)
When you see two round shapes together, carefully distinguish:
- "oo" = two round loops (e.g., "woole")
- "ol" = a round loop followed by an ascender (e.g., "Colewort")
- "uo" = a minim pair followed by a round loop (e.g., "Buorrage")

Documented problem: "woole" was read as "wolle" by 4/5 agents (oo->ol).
"Buorrage" was read as "Borrage" or "Boorrage" by all agents (uo->o/oo).

WARNING 6: DOUBLED FINAL CONSONANTS
This scribe (and many early modern scribes) frequently doubles final
consonants: "putt," "itt," "ytt," "knitt," "mollett." If you see what
might be a doubled final consonant, KEEP IT. Do not reduce "tt" to "t."

Documented problem: Every single doubled final consonant was reduced by
all five agents. This was a universal, systematic error.


================================================================================
PART 3: HERBAL AND MEDICAL VOCABULARY
================================================================================

Recipe manuscripts use specialized vocabulary. Not knowing these words
causes catastrophic misreadings because agents cannot anchor their letter
recognition. Learn these terms:

HERBS AND PLANTS commonly found in early modern recipes:
- langdebeef / langdebeefe = bugloss (Echium vulgare), a common herb
- borage / buorrage = borage (Borago officinalis)
- mallow / mollett = mallow leaves
- violet / voilett = violet
- coltsfoot, comfrey, fennell, camomill, wormewood, sallendine
- colewort = cabbage/kale family plant

MEDICAL TERMS:
- pyles = piles (hemorrhoids)
- flixe = flux (diarrhea/dysentery)
- deffnes = deafness
- consumptione = consumption (tuberculosis/wasting disease)
- fundamente = fundament (anus/buttocks)
- imbers = embers (hot ashes, used for roasting)
- tarre = tar
- trusser = a cloth pad or dressing for applying medicine

RECIPE VERBS:
- rake = to rake out of ashes/embers
- grinde = grind
- sitte = sit
- suffer = endure/tolerate

NOTE: If you encounter a word you cannot parse, consider whether it might
be a plant name, medical term, or recipe term you don't know. An unfamiliar
word is more likely to be specialized vocabulary than a word you should
re-interpret as common English.


================================================================================
PART 4: SCRIBE HABIT DETECTION
================================================================================

Before you begin transcribing the full page, scan for the scribe's personal
habits. Early modern scribes are surprisingly consistent in their OWN
conventions, even if those conventions differ from other scribes.

Look for:
1. Does this scribe double final consonants? (putt, itt, ytt)
   If yes, expect it throughout and preserve it.

2. Does this scribe use "u" or "v" at word starts?
   Don't assume the convention; check what THIS scribe does.

3. Does this scribe use capital F or "ff" for F?
   If "ff" is used at word starts, also watch for "ff" medially.

4. Does this scribe run words together or space them clearly?
   If words run together, preserve that in your transcription.

5. What does this scribe's long-s look like vs. "l" and "f"?
   Study a few clear examples before proceeding.

6. Does this scribe end words in "-inge" or "-ing"?
   Does this scribe write "-nde" or "-nge"?

Once you identify these habits, apply them consistently.


================================================================================
PART 5: POST-TRANSCRIPTION VERIFICATION CHECKLIST
================================================================================

After completing your first-draft transcription, go through this checklist:

[ ] 1. DOUBLED CONSONANTS: Did I preserve all doubled letters as written?
       Search your transcription for "put," "it," "yt" — should any be
       "putt," "itt," "ytt"? Go back and check the manuscript.

[ ] 2. U/V CHECK: Did I change any u's to v's or vice versa based on the
       "rule" rather than what's actually written? Look at each word-initial
       u/v and verify against the manuscript.

[ ] 3. LONG-S CHECK: For every word starting with "l," verify it's not a
       long-s. For every "f," verify it has a crossbar and isn't a long-s.

[ ] 4. WORD BOUNDARIES: Did I add spaces where the manuscript has none?
       Did I merge words that the manuscript keeps separate? Go back and
       check any questionable word breaks.

[ ] 5. UNFAMILIAR WORDS: Did I change any unfamiliar words into familiar
       ones? If you wrote a common English word where the manuscript seemed
       to show something unusual, go back and look again. The unusual
       reading may be correct.

[ ] 6. FINAL LETTERS: For each word ending in d/g, e/no-e, or s/r, verify
       the final letter against the manuscript. These are common sites of
       error.

[ ] 7. CAPITALIZATION: Check each capital letter against the manuscript.
       Don't capitalize ingredient names unless the manuscript does.

[ ] 8. PUNCTUATION: Did I preserve commas, periods, and other marks as
       they appear? Did I add or drop any?

[ ] 9. INGREDIENT NAMES: For each herb, plant, or medical term, verify
       the spelling letter by letter. Do not normalize to a "standard"
       spelling. If the scribe wrote "Buorrage," write "Buorrage" — not
       "Borrage."

[ ] 10. READ ALOUD: Read your transcription aloud. Does anything sound
        like it might be a misreading when spoken? (e.g., "lye upon" for
        what should be "sit upon" — the context doesn't quite work.)


================================================================================
PART 6: DECISION FRAMEWORK FOR AMBIGUOUS LETTERFORMS
================================================================================

When you cannot determine a letter with certainty, use this hierarchy:

1. LOOK AT THE LETTERFORM ITSELF — what does it most closely resemble?
2. LOOK AT THE SAME LETTER ELSEWHERE in the manuscript — does this scribe
   write it the same way in a word you CAN read?
3. CONSIDER THE WORD — does the reading make sense as an early modern English
   word, medical term, or plant name?
4. CONSIDER THE CONTEXT — does the reading make sense in a recipe?
5. ONLY AS A LAST RESORT — consider what "should" be there based on modern
   expectations. This is the least reliable guide.

If you are still unsure after all five steps, write your best reading and
mark it with [?] or use square brackets around uncertain letters: togeath[er].

DO NOT silently substitute a familiar word for an uncertain reading. A
transcription with honest uncertainty markers is far more useful than one
with confident-looking errors.
