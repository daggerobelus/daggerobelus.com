HAND-SPECIFIC ALPHABET CHART
Manuscript: Brumwich MS 160, Page 10 (opening showing pages 6–7)
Scribe: Primary hand — a fairly neat, running secretary hand with italic influences

============================================================
INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS
============================================================

a:
  Letterform: Single-compartment 'a' formed with a short curved stroke from the right, looping down and around to close or nearly close. Sometimes appears as an open loop. Fairly consistent.
  Example words: "water" (line 1, left page), "Take" (line 6, left page), "also" (various), "a" (throughout)
  Confusion risks: Can resemble 'o' when hastily written and the loop does not close fully. Also similar to 'u' in some contexts.
  Variants: Generally one form; slightly more rounded in some words.

b:
  Letterform: Tall ascender with a looped top curving right, then a bowl formed by a downstroke curving left at the baseline. The ascender is often quite tall.
  Example words: "be" (left page, mid section), "being" (left page), "bright" (left page bottom section), "before" (left page mid)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'h' since both have tall ascenders, but 'b' has a closed bowl and 'h' has an open descending stroke. Also 'l' can look similar when the bowl is minimal.
  Variants: Consistent form.

c:
  Letterform: A simple curved stroke, open to the right. Formed with a single downstroke curving right then left. Resembles modern 'c' but sometimes angular.
  Example words: "Excelent" (line 1, left page), "cause" (left page), "Coriander" (left page lower), "come" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: Can look like 'r' or 't' in secretary hand; in this hand, fairly distinct due to its rounded form.
  Variants: Uppercase 'C' is larger and more ornate, with a sweeping initial stroke.

d:
  Letterform: Secretary-style 'd' with a looped ascender that curves back to the left (leftward loop). The bowl sits at the baseline. The loop often extends well above the line.
  Example words: "decayed" (title, left page), "day" (left page), "diner" (left page lower), "draw" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: The looped ascender can look like 'cl' to a modern reader. Can also be confused with 'e' when written hastily because of the backward loop.
  Variants: Consistent secretary 'd' form throughout.

e:
  Letterform: Secretary-style reversed loop — formed as a backward-facing oval or reversed 'c'. Often just a small curved stroke. In this hand, usually quite small and can appear as a simple hook.
  Example words: "Excelent" (line 1), "eyes" (title), "the" (throughout), "helpe" (line 1)
  Confusion risks: High risk of confusion with 'o' in this hand, as both are small round strokes. Also can be mistaken for 'a' when open.
  Variants: Generally consistent; sometimes more angular at the end of words.

f:
  Letterform: Long descending stroke below the baseline with a crossbar. Similar to long-s but distinguished by the crossbar cutting through the descender.
  Example words: "of" (throughout), "fenill" (left page lower), "fine" (left page), "for" (left page lower), "feather" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: High risk with long-s (ſ). The crossbar on 'f' is the key distinguishing feature. When ink is heavy, can be hard to tell apart.
  Variants: Consistent form; 'ff' at start of words represents capital F.

g:
  Letterform: A two-part letter: a rounded bowl at the baseline with a descender that curves to the left, forming a loop below the line. The descender loop is distinctive.
  Example words: "good" (left page), "glasse" (left page), "grated" (right page), "ginger" (right page)
  Confusion risks: The top loop can be confused with 'q' but the descender loops differently. Generally distinct.
  Variants: Fairly consistent.

h:
  Letterform: Tall ascender (similar to 'b' and 'l') with the body formed by a curving stroke that descends to the right and sometimes loops back below the baseline. The descender-like final stroke is characteristic.
  Example words: "helpe" (line 1), "handfull" (left page), "honey" (left page), "halfe" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'b' (both have tall ascenders) and with 'li' (the descending stroke can look like a separate letter). The open body distinguishes it from 'b'.
  Variants: Sometimes the descending stroke is more pronounced, sometimes minimal.

i:
  Letterform: A short minim stroke (vertical stroke), usually with a dot or tittle above. In this hand, the minim is quite short.
  Example words: "in" (throughout), "it" (throughout), "into" (left page), "sight" (title)
  Confusion risks: Very high risk with minims: 'i', 'u', 'n', 'm' are all formed from combinations of minim strokes. Without clear dotting, 'i' can be lost in a sequence of minims. Also confused with 'l' when slightly elongated.
  Variants: Generally consistent; sometimes undotted.

j:
  Letterform: Not commonly used as a distinct letter in this period. Where it appears, it looks like an extended 'i' with a descender curving left below the baseline.
  Example words: Appears primarily in Roman numerals (e.g., "iij")
  Confusion risks: Confused with 'i'.
  Variants: N/A

k:
  Letterform: Tall ascender with an angular body — two diagonal strokes meeting at the midline, giving a somewhat elaborate appearance.
  Example words: "Take" (multiple occurrences), "make" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: Generally distinctive due to its angular form. Can occasionally be confused with 'h' if written hastily.
  Variants: Fairly consistent.

l:
  Letterform: Tall ascender with a simple downstroke. Sometimes loops at the top. The simplest of the tall letters.
  Example words: "let" (left page lower), "lawne" (right page), "lay" (left page lower), "little" (left page)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'b' and 'h' when the ascender is looped. The lack of a bowl (unlike 'b') and lack of a descending body stroke (unlike 'h') distinguish it.
  Variants: Sometimes looped, sometimes a plain stroke.

m:
  Letterform: Three minim strokes connected at the baseline. Generally broader than 'n' (which has two minims).
  Example words: "much" (left page), "make" (left page lower), "morning" (left page), "mouth" (left page)
  Confusion risks: Minim confusion: 'm' can be misread as 'in', 'ni', or 'iu'. Counting minims carefully is essential.
  Variants: Consistent.

n:
  Letterform: Two minim strokes connected. Similar to 'm' but narrower.
  Example words: "in" (throughout), "one" (right page), "not" (right page lower), "nine" (left page)
  Confusion risks: Minim confusion with 'm', 'u', 'in', etc.
  Variants: Consistent.

o:
  Letterform: A closed or nearly closed circular stroke. Fairly round in this hand.
  Example words: "of" (throughout), "one" (right page), "or" (throughout), "other" (left page title)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'e' (both are small round letters) and 'a' (when the 'a' is very rounded). This is a moderate risk in this hand.
  Variants: Fairly consistent; sometimes slightly open at the top.

p:
  Letterform: A descender going below the baseline with a bowl at the line level. The descender is straight.
  Example words: "powder" (left page lower), "put" (throughout), "pinte" (left page), "paire" (right page)
  Confusion risks: The 'special p' (for per-, pre-, pro-, par-) has a modified descender. Standard 'p' can be confused with 'q' but 'q' is very rare in English.
  Variants: Standard 'p' and abbreviated forms (per, pre, pro, par).

q:
  Letterform: Similar to 'p' but the descender curves to the right. Quite rare except in "quarter" and similar.
  Example words: "quarter" (left page), "quantity" (left page lower)
  Confusion risks: With 'p' — the direction of the descender stroke is the distinguishing feature.
  Variants: Consistent.

r:
  Letterform: In this hand, 'r' appears in two forms: (1) a short upstroke with a rightward hook at the top, and (2) a 2-shaped form. The first form is more common.
  Example words: "rose" (left page), "red" (throughout), "roses" (left page), "rather" (right page lower)
  Confusion risks: The short 'r' can be confused with 'v' or 'n' in some contexts. The 2-shaped 'r' can look like 'z'.
  Variants: Two forms as described; the hook-form is dominant.

s:
  Letterform: Two forms: (1) Long-s (ſ) — a tall stroke with a slight curve, used at the beginning and middle of words, looks like an 'f' without a crossbar. (2) Round-s — a modern-looking 's', used at the end of words.
  Example words: "sight" (long-s, title), "eyes" (round-s, title), "sugar" (long-s, left page), "roses" (round-s, left page)
  Confusion risks: Long-s confused with 'f' — this is a very high risk. The presence or absence of the crossbar is the key. Round-s is generally clear.
  Variants: Two forms as described (positional variants).

t:
  Letterform: A short stroke with a crossbar. The downstroke is about minim height (does not ascend far). The crossbar often connects to the next letter.
  Example words: "the" (throughout), "take" (throughout), "together" (left page), "them" (left page)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'c' or 'r' when the crossbar is faint. Also the 't' crossbar sometimes looks like a separate mark.
  Variants: Fairly consistent; sometimes taller.

u:
  Letterform: A minim stroke that curves at the bottom to connect to the next letter. Similar to modern 'u'.
  Example words: "putt" (left page), "upon" (left page lower), "use" (right page)
  Confusion risks: Minim confusion with 'n', 'i', and in sequences with 'm'. Also 'u' and 'v' are used interchangeably: 'v' at the start of words, 'u' medially.
  Variants: Consistent.

v:
  Letterform: A pointed stroke similar to modern 'v'. Used at the start of words where modern English uses 'u'.
  Example words: "vertue" (line 1, left page), "very" (left page), "vpon" (if present)
  Confusion risks: With 'u' (used interchangeably by position). Can also look like 'r' when hastily written.
  Variants: Consistent.

w:
  Letterform: A double-v form, wider than surrounding letters. Two pointed downstrokes connected.
  Example words: "water" (title, left page), "white" (left page), "with" (throughout), "well" (left page)
  Confusion risks: Generally distinct due to its width. Can occasionally look like 'vv' or be confused with 'w' in very different scripts.
  Variants: Consistent.

x:
  Letterform: Two crossing strokes. Relatively rare.
  Example words: "Excelent" (line 1, left page)
  Confusion risks: Generally distinct.
  Variants: N/A

y:
  Letterform: Similar to modern 'y' — a short stroke above the baseline with a descender curving to the left below. In this hand, the descender often has a pronounced leftward loop.
  Example words: "ye" (= "the", throughout), "you" (left page lower), "eyes" (title), "your" (right page)
  Confusion risks: Can be confused with thorn (þ) which also looks like 'y'. When written as "ye" it represents "the" (thorn + e). Can also be confused with 'p' if the descender is straight.
  Variants: Consistent.

z:
  Letterform: Not observed frequently in this manuscript.
  Example words: Possibly in "hazardes" if present
  Confusion risks: Can look like the 2-shaped 'r'.
  Variants: N/A

============================================================
COMMON COMBINATIONS AND LIGATURES
============================================================

th: Very common. The 't' and 'h' are often connected with a flowing stroke. Also written with thorn (þ) appearing as 'y' — transcribe as "the" with italicized 'th' when thorn is used.

sh: The long-s followed by 'h' — "shred" (left page), "should" (left page lower). The long-s flows into the 'h' ascender.

ch: Appears in "each" (left page), "much" (left page). The 'c' connects to the 'h' with a looping stroke.

ff: Double-f represents capital F. Seen in some words.

wh: Very common — "when" (title), "white" (left page), "whole" (left page). The 'w' flows into the 'h'.

-es: Terminal '-es' sometimes written as a graph (looped downstroke). This scribe sometimes uses the graph and sometimes writes out 'es' in full.

-er: The '-er' graph appears as a hook-shaped upstroke after the main letter body. Seen in "water" (throughout), "after" (left page lower).

ye: Thorn + e = "the". Very frequent in this manuscript. Written as a 'y' with a superscript 'e'.

yt: Thorn + t = "that". May appear.

============================================================
DISTINCTIVE FEATURES OF THIS HAND
============================================================

1. The hand is a running secretary with some italic influences — letters often connect fluidly.
2. Recipe titles are sometimes centered and written slightly larger.
3. Each new recipe is marked with a cross or dagger symbol (+) before the title.
4. The scribe uses "ye" (thorn) extensively for "the".
5. Ink is brown/dark brown, fairly consistent in density.
6. The writing is moderately sized and fairly legible for secretary hand.
7. Superscript letters appear in abbreviations (ye, yr, etc.).
8. Doubled consonants are frequent: "putt," "handfull," "spoonfull."
9. Some words have terminal 'e' where modern English does not.
10. The left page is numbered "6" and the right page "7" at the top outer corners.

============================================================
CONFUSION RISK RANKING (Highest to Lowest)
============================================================

1. LONG-S (ſ) vs. F
   Why similar: Both are tall strokes descending below or near the baseline. The only difference is the crossbar on 'f'. In this hand, the crossbar can be faint or partially formed.
   Distinguishing feature: Look for the crossbar cutting through the descender on 'f'. Long-s has at most a slight leftward spur, not a full crossbar.

2. E vs. O
   Why similar: Both are small round strokes in this hand. The secretary 'e' is a reversed loop while 'o' is a closed circle, but when written quickly they can appear identical.
   Distinguishing feature: The 'e' typically has a slight opening or tail on the right side; 'o' is more symmetrically closed.

3. MINIM CONFUSION: I / U / N / M
   Why similar: All are formed from short vertical (minim) strokes. In rapid writing, 'in' can look like 'm', 'un' like 'uu', 'ni' like 'm', etc.
   Distinguishing feature: Count minims carefully. 'i' should have a dot/tittle. 'u' curves at the bottom. Context helps but letterform analysis must come first.

4. B vs. H vs. L
   Why similar: All three have tall ascenders. 'b' has a closed bowl, 'h' has an open descending stroke, and 'l' is a simple tall stroke.
   Distinguishing feature: Check for a bowl (b), a descending rightward stroke (h), or a plain stick (l).

5. C vs. T vs. R
   Why similar: In secretary hand, 'c', 't', and short 'r' can all appear as short strokes at minim height. 't' has a crossbar, 'c' is curved, 'r' has an upward hook.
   Distinguishing feature: Look for the crossbar (t), the curve (c), or the hook (r).

6. D (secretary) vs. CL
   Why similar: The secretary 'd' with its leftward-looping ascender can look like 'cl' to a modern reader.
   Distinguishing feature: Secretary 'd' is a single letter — the loop connects directly to the bowl without a break.

7. Y vs. THORN (þ)
   Why similar: Identical in appearance. Both look like modern 'y'. The distinction is contextual: "ye" = "the" (thorn), "ye" = "ye" (actual y) only rarely.
   Distinguishing feature: If followed by 'e' and meaning "the," it is thorn. Transcribe as *th*e per conventions.

8. A vs. U
   Why similar: When 'a' is written open and 'u' has a slight curve at the start, they can look similar.
   Distinguishing feature: 'a' typically has a more defined overhead stroke; 'u' is formed entirely below the midline.
