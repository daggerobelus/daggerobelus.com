SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from studying one manuscript page: Lady Sedley's Receipt Book (MS534), page 13 (folios 20-21), dated 1686.

This guide was built entirely by comparing the manuscript image to its verified transcription. No external paleography resources were consulted.

================================================================================
PART 1: GENERAL OBSERVATIONS ABOUT THE HAND
================================================================================

This is a late 17th-century English secretary-influenced hand, fairly neat and consistent. The writing is in dark brown/black ink on paper. The page is a two-page opening (folios 20 and 21), with folio 20 on the left and folio 21 on the right. The hand is relatively legible compared to earlier secretary hands, but retains several archaic letterforms that can trip up a modern reader.

Key general features:
- The script mixes "italic" and "secretary" letter forms. Some letters appear in a more modern/italic style while others retain older secretary shapes.
- The writer is consistent within this page -- the same letters generally look the same way each time they appear.
- Lines are fairly evenly spaced. Words are generally separated by clear gaps, though some words run close together.
- The pen is a quill, producing strokes that vary from thin (upstrokes) to thick (downstrokes).

================================================================================
PART 2: THE ALPHABET -- LOWERCASE LETTERS
================================================================================

For each letter, I describe its form as observed in the manuscript, give examples of words where it appears, and note potential confusions.

--- a ---
Form: A round, open-bowled letter. The bowl is formed with a curved stroke on the left, and the letter is closed (or nearly closed) at the top right with a short connecting stroke. It looks like a modern lowercase "a" but sometimes the bowl is left slightly open.
Examples: "a" (the article, appearing many times), "Take" (first word of each recipe), "earthen", "plaister"
Confusion risk: Generally distinctive. When written quickly, can resemble "o" if the closing stroke is unclear.

--- b ---
Form: A tall ascender stroke going up, then the pen comes back down and forms a rounded bowl at the baseline. The ascender is relatively straight. The bowl sits to the right of the stem.
Examples: "be" (line 18: "when they be well"), "belly" (line 32), "back" (line 33), "bigger" (line 23), "but" (line 24), "boyled" (line 27)
Confusion risk: The ascender can look like "l" or "h" if the bowl is poorly formed. In this hand, "b" is generally clear.

--- c ---
Form: A simple curved stroke open to the right, much like a modern "c". It is small and sits at the baseline.
Examples: "calcinated" (line 16), "coales" (line 20), "colour" (line 23-24), "collicke" (line 32), "cordiall" (line 52)
Confusion risk: Can look like "e" if the interior stroke of "e" is faint. Also resembles the start of "t" in some formations.

--- d ---
Form: This is one of the most distinctive letters in this hand. The "d" has a LEFTWARD-LOOPING ascender. The bowl is formed first (like an "a" or "o"), then the pen sweeps up and to the LEFT in a curved ascender before coming back down. This creates a distinctive backward-leaning loop above the letter.
Examples: "Lead" (line 16), "dust" (line 16), "said" (line 20), "dipp" (line 28), "dried" -- virtually every "d" in the manuscript shows this leftward loop.
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- This secretary "d" looks nothing like a modern "d". A reader unfamiliar with this form might mistake it for "e" or even "cl". The looped ascender going LEFT is the key identifier. This is probably the single most important letterform to learn.

--- e ---
Form: A small letter formed with a short horizontal or slightly curved middle stroke inside a curved body. It often looks like a tiny backward "3" or a small rounded shape with a visible crossbar. At the end of words, "e" sometimes appears as a simple upward flick.
Examples: "the" (appears dozens of times), "olive" (line 15), "these" (line 17), "fire" (line 20)
Confusion risk: Can look like "c" when written quickly. Terminal "e" (at the end of words) can be very small and easy to miss.

--- f ---
Form: The "f" has a descender that drops BELOW the baseline, like a modern "f", with a crossbar through the middle. The top curves to the right. The overall shape is tall, crossing both above and below the line.
Examples: "fire" (line 20), "fingers" (line 27), "fastned" (line 22), "flux" (line 33)
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- "f" and long-s (see "s" entry) look extremely similar. Both have vertical strokes that extend above and below the line. The key difference: "f" has a visible crossbar through the middle, while long-s does not. See the "s" entry for more detail.

--- ff ---
NOTE: Double-f at the start of a word is used where modern English would use a capital "F". This is a convention of the period, not a quirk of this individual writer.
Examples: "ffellons" (line 41, meaning "felons"), "ffor" (line 60, meaning "For")
This is important: when you see "ff" at the beginning of a word, read it as a capital "F".

--- g ---
Form: A letter with a descender. The top looks like a small "a" or "o" shape, with a tail that descends below the baseline and curves to the left, sometimes forming a loop.
Examples: "glazed" (line 18), "grey" (line 23), "good" (line 43), "gillyflowers" (line 49)
Confusion risk: Generally distinctive due to the descender. Could potentially be confused with "y" if the descender is ambiguous -- but "g" tends to have a rounder top bowl.

--- h ---
Form: A tall ascender, similar to "b", but instead of a closed bowl, the second stroke curves outward to the right and does NOT close back to meet the stem. The right stroke sweeps away.
Examples: "halfe" (line 21), "houre" (line 21), "heales" (line 40), "head" (line 43), "handfull" (line 58)
Confusion risk: Can look like "b" if the right stroke curves close to the stem. In this hand, "h" is generally open on the right while "b" closes its bowl.

--- i ---
Form: A short minim stroke (a single short vertical line). May or may not have a dot above it. In this manuscript, dots above "i" are inconsistent.
Examples: "it" (appears many times), "in" (appears many times), "fire" (line 20), "stirring" (line 21)
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- This is the hardest letter in secretary hand. A single minim can be "i", or part of "m", "n", "u", or "w". In a sequence of minims, you must use context and word knowledge to figure out which letters are present. For example, "stirring" contains a sequence of minims in the middle (for "rri") that could look like a string of identical strokes.

--- j ---
Form: Rare in this text. When it appears, it looks like an "i" with a descender curving left below the baseline.
Examples: "juice" (line 64, 65) -- though in this period "juice" may be written with an "i" form.
Confusion risk: Often interchangeable with "i" in this period.

--- k ---
Form: A tall ascender with two strokes branching from the stem at the midline -- one going up-right and one going down-right, meeting the stem at roughly the same point.
Examples: "kidney" (line 39), "back" (line 40)
Confusion risk: Generally distinctive.

--- l ---
Form: A tall, straight ascender. It is simply a single vertical stroke extending above the line of other lowercase letters, sometimes with a very slight rightward curve at the top.
Examples: "Lead" (line 16), "well" (line 16), "still" (line 21), "till" (line 23), "laid" (line 30)
Confusion risk: Can look like the stem of "b", "h", or "k" without their distinguishing features. In isolation, hard to tell from a long "t" or the ascender of "d" (but "d" loops left).

--- m ---
Form: Three connected minim strokes. Looks like three small humps or arches connected at the baseline.
Examples: "must" (line 24), "matter" (line 25), "mingle" (line 51), "Mortar" (line 65)
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- Three minims in a row. Must be distinguished from "ni", "in", "iu", "ui", etc. by context. In this hand, "m" tends to have its three strokes evenly spaced and of equal height.

--- n ---
Form: Two connected minim strokes. Looks like two small humps.
Examples: "not" (line 24), "neither" (line 27), "enough" (line 28), "paine" (line 31)
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- Two minims. Could be confused with "u" (which is also two minims but with a connecting curve at the bottom rather than humps at the top). In this hand, "n" has pointed/humped tops while "u" has a rounded trough.

--- o ---
Form: A simple round or oval closed shape, much like a modern "o".
Examples: "of" (appears many times), "one" (line 15), "olive" (line 15), "oyle" (line 15), "on" (line 26)
Confusion risk: Can look like "a" if the closing stroke of "a" is too rounded, or like "e" in some formations.

--- p ---
Form: A descender stroke going below the baseline, with a bowl or lobe to the right at the top (at the x-height level). The bowl may be open or closed.
Examples: "pound" (line 15), "put" (line 19), "plaister" (lines 12, 30), "pott" (line 18), "provokes" (line 30)
Confusion risk: Generally distinctive. The descender and right-side bowl are clear markers.

--- q ---
Form: Rare. A descender with the bowl on the LEFT side, mirror image of "p". The tail often sweeps right below the baseline.
Examples: "quarter" (line 48)
Confusion risk: Could be confused with "g" due to the descender, but "q" is always followed by "u".

--- r ---
Form: This letter appears in TWO forms in the manuscript:
  1. A short stroke with a small rightward shoulder/hook at the top (modern-looking "r"). Used in most positions.
  2. A "2-shaped r" that looks like a "2" -- used sometimes after "o" or round letters.
Examples: "fire" (line 20, form 1), "your" (line 22, form 1), "reines" (line 33)
Confusion risk: The 2-shaped "r" can be mistaken for a "z" or the number "2". When in doubt, if you see a "2"-like shape after "o" or another round letter, it is likely "r".

--- s ---
Form: This letter appears in TWO forms:
  1. LONG-S: Used at the beginning and middle of words. Looks like an "f" WITHOUT a crossbar. It is a tall letter with an ascender, and it descends slightly below the baseline. The stroke is essentially a single long curved vertical line.
  2. ROUND-S (terminal s): Used at the END of words. Looks like a modern lowercase "s" or sometimes a rounded form.
Examples of long-s: "said" (line 20 -- the initial "s" is long), "still" (line 21), "stirring" (line 21), "sundry" (line 11), "sope" (line 17)
Examples of round-s: "vses" (line 11 -- final "s"), "ounces" (line 16 -- final "s"), "coales" (line 20)
Confusion risk: CRITICAL -- Long-s vs. "f" is one of the biggest challenges. BOTH are tall with strokes extending up and down. The ONLY reliable difference is the crossbar: "f" has one, long-s does not. If you see a tall letter that looks like "f" but has no crossbar, it is long-s. Study "fastned" (line 22) where both appear: "f" starts the word with a crossbar, while "s" in "fastned" is short-s (end-of-syllable position in compound).

--- t ---
Form: A short letter (NOT tall like "l") with a crossbar. The vertical stroke rises just slightly above the x-height, and the crossbar runs through it. It is noticeably shorter than letters like "l", "b", "h", or "d".
Examples: "the" (passim), "Take" (line 15), "to" (line 22), "till" (line 23), "turned" (line 23)
Confusion risk: Can be confused with "l" if the crossbar is faint. The key is that "t" is SHORT and has a crossbar, while "l" is TALL and has no crossbar. Also, "t" is shorter than "d", "b", and "h".

--- th ---
NOTE: In this manuscript, "th" often appears as a ligature where the "t" and "h" are written together in one connected stroke. The crossbar of "t" flows directly into the ascender of "h". This is common in words like "the", "that", "them", "then", "there", "this".
Examples: "the" (passim), "that" (line 19), "then" (line 22, 26), "therein" (line 29), "thereof"

--- u/v ---
Form: In this period, "u" and "v" are somewhat interchangeable, but there is a pattern:
  - "v" is used at the BEGINNING of words where modern English would use "u": "vses" (line 11, = "uses"), "upermost" (line 19, = "uppermost"), "vse" (line 66, = "use"), "vnnilla" (line 43, = "vanilla" or possibly another word)
  - "u" is used in the MIDDLE of words: "dust" (line 16), "upon" (line 19), "your" (line 22), "but" (line 24)
  - "v" at the start of a word looks angular, like a modern "v" -- a pointed bottom with two strokes going up.
  - "u" in the middle looks like two minims with a curve at the bottom.
Confusion risk: If you see "v" at the start of a word, consider whether it represents "u" in modern spelling. This is systematic in this period.

--- w ---
Form: Looks like two "v" shapes joined together, or like a modern "w". It is wider than most letters.
Examples: "well" (line 16), "when" (line 18), "with" (line 21), "wood" (line 22), "weakness" (line 39), "water" (line 48)
Confusion risk: Can sometimes look like "vv". Generally clear from context.

--- x ---
Form: Two crossing diagonal strokes, like a modern "x".
Examples: "flux" (line 33)
Confusion risk: Rare and generally clear.

--- y ---
Form: Two strokes meeting at a point, with a descender dropping below the baseline curving left. Looks somewhat like a modern "y".
Examples: "your" (line 22), "you" (line 24), "yeares" (line 29), "kidney" (line 39)
Confusion risk: Can be confused with "g" if the descender is looped, or with "p" if the first stroke is vertical. Usually distinctive.

--- z ---
Form: Rare. A diagonal stroke with horizontal strokes at top and bottom, like a modern "z".
Examples: "glazed" (line 18)
Confusion risk: Could be confused with 2-shaped "r". Context is key.

PART 3: THE ALPHABET -- UPPERCASE/CAPITAL LETTERS
================================================================================

Capital letters in this manuscript are generally larger and more decorative than their lowercase counterparts. Many are recognizably similar to modern capitals. Key observations:

--- A ---
A large letter with two angled strokes meeting at a peak, and a crossbar. Decorative with a wider stance than lowercase.
Example: "A Salve" (line 11), "A Good Cordiall" (line 45), "Aches" (line 40)

--- C ---
A large curved letter open to the right, similar to modern "C" but sometimes with more decorative flourishes.
Example: "Cowslip" (line 48), "Cinamon" (line 49), "Clarett" (line 50), "Collicke" (line 32), "Childbed" (line 45, 53)

--- G ---
Distinctive. A large curved letter that looks like "G" but with an extended horizontal bar or crossbar partway through.
Example: "Good" (line 45)

--- I ---
A tall vertical stroke, sometimes with serifs or crossbars at top and bottom. Can also appear with a decorative curve.
Example: "Iron" (line 21), "Impostumes" (line 41)

--- L ---
A tall stroke with a horizontal base extending to the right. Sometimes has a loop at the top.
Example: "Lead" (line 16), "Leaden" (line 12), "Lemon" (line 64)

--- M ---
Large, with three main strokes. Looks like a bigger version of lowercase "m" with decorative elements.
Example: "Mortar" (line 65)

--- P ---
A large descender stroke with a prominent bowl or loop at the top right.
Example: "Pills" (line 55)

--- R ---
A tall stroke with a bowl at the top and a leg extending to the right.
Example: "Runinge" (line 39)

--- S ---
At the start of titles or prominent words, "S" appears as a large round form (not long-s).
Example: "Salve" (line 11), "Spanish" (line 17), "Stick" (line 22), "Stomach" (line 30, 31), "Searclothes" (line 28), "Syrrup" (line 49), "Sallandine" (line 62)

--- T ---
A tall stroke with a wide crossbar. Sometimes the crossbar has decorative flourishes.
Example: "Take" (line 15), "Tetter" (line 60), "Thumbe" (line 63)

--- X ---
Roman numerals: "XXVII" (line 13), "XXVIII" (line 46), "XXIX" (line 56) -- these use standard large Roman numeral forms.

NOTE on "ff" for "F": As mentioned above, this hand uses double-f ("ff") instead of a capital "F". So "ffor" = "For" and "ffellons" = "Felons" (line 41, 60).

================================================================================
PART 4: ABBREVIATIONS AND SPECIAL MARKS
================================================================================

1. LINE-END WORD BREAKS (=)
When a word is too long to fit at the end of a line, it is broken with an equals sign "=" at the end of the line, and the word continues on the next line.
Examples:
  - "incor=" at end of line 18, continues as "porated" at start of line 19 (= "incorporated")
  - "soverai=" at end of line 51, continues as "gne" at start of line 52 (= "soveraigne")

2. THE AMPERSAND (&)
The ampersand sign "&" is used for "and". It appears frequently throughout the text.
Examples: "& when they be well" (line 18), "& a halfe" (line 21), "& if it" (line 26)
In the manuscript, it looks like a stylized character, sometimes resembling a curvy "7" or a looped character.

3. SUPERSCRIPT / CARET INSERTIONS (^)
Text inserted above the line with a caret mark is transcribed with "^".
Example: "h^eales" (line 41) -- the writer wrote "hales" and inserted "e" above the line to correct it to "heales".

4. THE ABBREVIATION "&c"
This stands for "et cetera" (etc.).
Example: "&c" at the very end of the text (line 68).

5. THE "t'is" CONTRACTION
"t'is" = "it is". The apostrophe replaces the initial "i".
Examples: "t'is boyled" (line 27), "t'is a remedy" (line 32)

6. NUMBERS
Numbers appear both written out and as numerals:
  - "one pound" (line 15), "12 ounces" (line 16), "20 yeares" (line 29)
  - "1 pound" (line 15) -- note that both "one pound" and "1 pound" appear on the same line
  - "3 or 4" (lines 66, 67)
Roman numerals are used for recipe numbers: "XXVII", "XXVIII", "XXIX"

PART 5: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
================================================================================

Early modern English spelling differs from modern English in systematic ways. Key patterns observed in this text:

1. DOUBLE LETTERS where modern English has singles:
  - "stirringe" for "stirring" (line 21)
  - "collour" for "colour" (line 24)
  - "stirringe" for "stirring" -- note the terminal "-inge" ending
  - "dipp" for "dip" (line 28)
  - "Syrrup" for "syrup" (line 49)
  - "Alloes" for "aloes" (line 58)
  - "ffellons" for "felons" (line 41)

2. THE "-inge" ENDING instead of modern "-ing":
  - "stirringe" (line 21)
  - "continueinge" (line 20)
  - "stirringe" (line 24, "stirrings" context)
  - "Runinge" (line 39)
  - "dressinge" (line 67)

3. "Y" WHERE MODERN ENGLISH HAS "I" or vice versa:
  - "oyle" for "oil" (line 15)
  - "boyled" for "boiled" (line 27)
  - "layd" for "laid" (line 31)
  - "bloudy" for "bloody" (line 33)

4. TERMINAL "-E" added to words that no longer have it:
  - "coales" for "coals" (line 20)
  - "houre" for "hour" (line 21)
  - "halfe" for "half" (line 21)
  - "sope" for "soap" (line 17, also different vowel)
  - "Thumbe" for "thumb" (line 63)

5. DIFFERENT VOWEL SPELLINGS:
  - "sope" for "soap" (line 17)
  - "oyle" for "oil" (line 15)
  - "cloathes" for "cloths/clothes" (line 28)
  - "hearbs" for "herbs" (line 66)
  - "reines" for "reins" (line 33)
  - "paine" for "pain" (line 31)

6. "V" FOR INITIAL "U":
  - "vses" = "uses" (line 11)
  - "vse" = "use" (line 66)
  - "vnnilla" = "vanilla" (line 43) -- note also the double "n"
  - "upermost" = "uppermost" (line 19) -- inconsistency: uses "u" here

7. ARCHAIC OR VARIANT WORD FORMS:
  - "plaister" for "plaster" (line 12, 30)
  - "calcinated" for "calcined" (line 16)
  - "trenchar" for "trencher" (line 26, 27)
  - "Searclothes" for "cere-cloths" (line 28) -- waxed bandages
  - "Collicke" for "colic" (line 32)
  - "Impostumes" for "imposthumes/abscesses" (line 41)
  - "Sallandine" for "celandine" (line 62)
  - "Clovegillyflowers" for "clove gillyflowers" (clove pinks/carnations) (line 49)
  - "Alcemus" (line 50) -- possibly "alkermes" (a medicinal cordial)
  - "Tetter" (line 60) -- a skin disease/eruption

PART 6: PAGE LAYOUT AND FORMATTING
================================================================================

1. PAGE NUMBERS
Page/folio numbers appear at the top of each page. "20" appears at the top left of the left page, "21" would appear at the top of the right page.

2. RECIPE TITLES
Recipe titles are written in a slightly larger or more prominent hand, often on their own line. They serve as headings.
Examples:
  - "A Salve for sundry vses called / the Leaden plaister" (lines 11-12)
  - "A Good Cordiall For A woman in Childbed" (line 45)
  - "Pills for the head" (line 55)
  - "ffor A Tetter" (line 60)

3. RECIPE NUMBERS
Roman numerals appear centered below the recipe title.
  - "XXVII" (line 13)
  - "XXVIII" (line 46)
  - "XXIX" (line 56)

4. RECIPE STRUCTURE
Each recipe follows a pattern:
  - Title (sometimes spanning two lines)
  - Roman numeral
  - Body beginning with "Take..." (the standard opening for recipe instructions)

5. PARAGRAPH/RECIPE SEPARATION
Recipes are separated by blank space. There is a visible gap between the end of one recipe and the title of the next.

6. LINE SPACING
Lines are evenly spaced within each recipe. The writer maintains a consistent baseline.

7. TWO-PAGE SPREAD
The manuscript is an opening of two pages. The text flows from the bottom of the left page (folio 20) to the top of the right page (folio 21), continuing the same recipe ("the Leaden plaister" continues from folio 20 to 21).

PART 7: COMMONLY CONFUSED LETTER PAIRS
================================================================================

These are the pairs most likely to cause misreadings, ranked by danger:

1. LONG-S vs. F (most dangerous)
   Both are tall, both extend above and below the line.
   Key: "f" has a crossbar; long-s does not.
   Test: Look at "fastned" (line 22): the "f" at the start has a crossbar. Compare to the "s" in "still" (line 21): same tall shape, no crossbar.

2. SECRETARY "D" vs. OTHER LETTERS
   The leftward-looping "d" looks like no modern letter.
   Key: Look for the loop going LEFT from the top of a round bowl.
   Test: "said" (line 20), "Lead" (line 16) -- in each case, what might look like an odd "e" or "cl" is actually "d".

3. MINIM CONFUSION: i, m, n, u
   All are made of short identical vertical strokes.
   Key: Count the minims and use word context. "m" = 3, "n" = 2, "u" = 2 (rounded at bottom), "i" = 1.
   Test: "continueinge" (line 20) -- the "nue" section is a thicket of minims. "stirring" (line 21) -- the "rri" section.

4. C vs. E (moderate danger)
   Both are small, rounded, open to the right.
   Key: "e" has a visible interior horizontal stroke; "c" does not.
   Test: Compare "c" in "calcinated" (line 16) with "e" in "the" (passim).

5. U vs. V (contextual)
   Not visually confusing, but the same sound is represented differently by position.
   Key: "v" at the start of words = modern "u". "u" in the middle of words = modern "u".
   Test: "vses" (line 11) = "uses"; "upermost" (line 19) = "uppermost" (note inconsistency).

6. LONG-S vs. LOWERCASE L
   Long-s is tall and thin; "l" is also tall and thin.
   Key: Long-s usually curves slightly and may descend below baseline; "l" stays on or above the baseline.
   Test: Compare "s" in "still" with "l" in "still" -- both tall, but the long-s has a slight leftward curve at the bottom.

7. T vs. L (moderate danger)
   Both have vertical strokes.
   Key: "t" is SHORT with a crossbar. "l" is TALL without a crossbar.
   Test: Compare "t" in "the" with "l" in "Lead" -- "l" extends noticeably higher.

================================================================================
PART 8: READING STRATEGY -- STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS
================================================================================

When encountering a new manuscript page in a similar hand, follow this process:

1. ORIENT YOURSELF
   - Look at the overall page layout. Identify page numbers, titles, recipe numbers.
   - Identify where recipes begin ("Take...") and end (blank space before next title).

2. START WITH WHAT YOU CAN READ
   - Common short words are good anchors: "the", "of", "and"/"&", "in", "to", "it", "a".
   - Recipe openings ("Take") and structural words ("pound", "ounces") are predictable.
   - Titles and Roman numerals help orient you.

3. USE LETTER-BY-LETTER DECODING FOR HARD WORDS
   - When stuck, go letter by letter using the alphabet descriptions above.
   - Watch out for the dangerous pairs: long-s/f, secretary d, minim sequences.
   - Count minims carefully in any ambiguous sequence.

4. USE CONTEXT AND RECIPE VOCABULARY
   - This is a recipe book. Expect: ingredients (herbs, plants, minerals, animal products), quantities (pound, ounce, handful, pint, dramme), preparations (boil, stir, strain, stamp, mingle, incorporate), body parts (stomach, belly, back, head, kidney, reines), medical terms (flux, colic, ache, swelling, impostume, tetter).
   - "Take" almost always begins a recipe instruction.
   - "&" appears very frequently between items in lists.

5. EXPECT EARLY MODERN SPELLING
   - Do NOT assume modern spelling. Sound out the words.
   - Be ready for: -inge endings, double consonants, y/i swaps, v for initial u, terminal -e.
   - If a word does not make sense in modern spelling, try pronouncing it aloud -- it may be a phonetic variant.

6. CHECK YOUR WORK
   - Does the sentence make grammatical sense?
   - Does the recipe instruction make practical sense (mixing, heating, applying)?
   - Do quantities/measurements seem reasonable?

PART 9: SPECIFIC WORD EXAMPLES FOR PRACTICE
================================================================================

Here are specific words from the manuscript with notes on how they appear in the hand:

"plaister" (line 12): p-l-a-i-s-t-e-r. Note the long-s (no crossbar) followed by "t" (short with crossbar). These two letters appear side by side.

"calcinated" (line 16): c-a-l-c-i-n-a-t-e-d. The final "d" has the secretary leftward loop.

"incorporate" (line 17): Long word with many minims in "in" and "or". The "c" is small and open.

"continueinge" (line 20): The "-inge" ending is characteristic. The "nue" sequence is challenging minims.

"Searclothes" (line 28): Capital "S" is round. This is a compound word: sear + clothes (= cere-cloths, waxed bandages).

"Clovegillyflowers" (line 49): A very long compound word. "Clove" + "gilly" + "flowers". The long-s does not appear here; the "s" in "flowers" is terminal round-s.

"Sallandine" (line 62): = celandine (the herb). Note "S" capital, double "l", and "-ine" ending.

================================================================================
PART 10: SUMMARY OF MOST CRITICAL RULES
================================================================================

If you remember nothing else, remember these:

1. SECRETARY "D" loops LEFT. It looks nothing like a modern "d". This is the most distinctive letterform.

2. LONG-S looks like "f" but has NO crossbar. Check for the crossbar to distinguish them.

3. MINIMS (i, m, n, u) are identical short strokes. Count them and use context.

4. "ff" at the start of a word = capital "F".

5. "v" at the start of a word often = "u" in modern spelling.

6. Spelling is NOT modern. Sound out words phonetically. Expect "-inge" for "-ing", "-our" for "-or", "-e" added to word ends, "y" for "i" and vice versa.

7. The ampersand "&" = "and" and appears constantly.

8. Line-end word breaks use "=" instead of a hyphen.

9. Recipes begin with "Take" and are numbered with Roman numerals.

10. When stuck, use recipe context: ingredients, quantities, body parts, and medical applications are the standard vocabulary.
