SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE FOR EARLY MODERN ENGLISH MANUSCRIPTS
====================================================================
Derived from studying 10 manuscript pages across 8 different scribes
(recipe/medical books, c. 1580-1690)

This guide was produced by studying manuscript images paired with their
correct transcriptions, with no external paleography references. Every
observation below is grounded in specific examples from the training
materials.

====================================================================
PART 1: INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS -- THE ALPHABET
====================================================================

The single most important thing to understand is that early modern
handwriting uses a mix of "secretary hand" (an older style) and
"italic hand" (a newer, more familiar style). Most scribes in this
collection use a mixed hand, drawing some letters from each system.
The result is that some letters look almost modern, while others are
nearly unrecognizable to a modern reader.

LOWERCASE LETTERS
-----------------

a:  Generally looks like a modern lowercase 'a', though sometimes
    written as a simple round loop without the hook at top. In some
    hands (Brumwich, RCP-502) it can be a single stroke that looks
    like a small 'u' open at the top.
    Examples: "water" (appears dozens of times across all MSS),
    "Take" (ubiquitous recipe opener)

b:  Usually recognizable -- an upstroke with a loop at the top
    descending to a bowl at the baseline. The bowl may be open.
    In Bulkeley, the ascender has a pronounced leftward loop.
    Examples: "be" (Bulkeley: "it is not then to be vsed safely"),
    "blood" (Bulkeley), "back" (Sedley)

c:  Looks like a modern 'c' in most hands -- a simple curved stroke
    open to the right. Can be confused with 'e' when written hastily.
    In some hands (Brumwich), it is angular rather than rounded.
    Examples: "Cleane" (Brumwich), "coales" (Sedley), "cold" (St. John)

d:  THIS IS A KEY LETTER. Two very different forms appear:
    (1) Italic 'd': looks like a modern 'd' with a straight or
        slightly curved ascender leaning right. Used by Sedley,
        Ayscough, Gibson.
    (2) Secretary 'd': the ascender loops LEFT and back, creating
        a shape that looks almost like a modern cursive 'e' or
        even a Greek delta. The loop goes up and to the left, then
        curves back right. Used prominently by Brumwich, Bulkeley,
        RCP-502. This is one of the most confusing letters for
        modern readers.
    Examples: "diseases" (Bulkeley: secretary d), "distilled"
    (Bulkeley: secretary d at start), "drinke" (Gibson: italic d)

e:  Usually a simple curved stroke, like a small backward 'c' or
    a loop. In secretary hand, it often looks like a reversed '3'
    or a small angular mark. At the end of words, final 'e' is
    often a small upward flick that can be nearly invisible.
    Examples: "the" (universal), "made" (Bulkeley), "there" (Sedley)

f:  Looks like a modern 'f' but CRITICALLY -- it descends below
    the baseline, often with a leftward hook. The crossbar may be
    part of the natural pen stroke rather than a separate mark.
    Initial 'f' and double 'ff' are important: "ff" at the start
    of a word represents a capital F. This is a universal convention.
    Examples: "ffellons" = "Fellons" (Sedley), "ffor" = "For" (Sedley),
    "ffrankincense" = "Frankincense" (RCP-504)

g:  The descender usually curls to the left in a closed or open
    loop below the baseline. The upper part looks like a modern 'g'
    or sometimes like a 'y' shape. In some hands (Bulkeley) the
    descender is a straight stroke going left.
    Examples: "good" (universal), "giuen" (Bulkeley), "grease" (Fanshawe)

h:  Generally recognizable, but the second stroke (the arch) often
    does not fully close, making it look like 'li' in hasty writing.
    In Brumwich, the arch sometimes barely rises from the baseline.
    Examples: "halfe" (universal), "herbes" (Bulkeley), "houres" (Ayscough)

i/j: These are essentially the same letter in this period. A simple
    downstroke, sometimes with a dot, sometimes without. At the start
    of words, 'I' and 'J' are interchangeable: "Iuce" = "Juice"
    (Gibson, RCP-504), "Iulip" (Gibson). The letter 'j' as distinct
    from 'i' is not consistently used. When 'i' appears next to
    other minim letters (m, n, u), it can be very hard to
    distinguish.
    Examples: "Iuce" = "Juice" (Gibson), "Juniper" (RCP-502)

k:  Relatively rare. When it appears, it usually has a recognizable
    form with an ascender and two diagonal strokes. Can look like
    'lc' in hasty writing.
    Examples: "keep" (Ayscough, Gibson), "kinde" (Bulkeley)

l:  A simple ascender stroke. The danger is confusing it with 'b'
    (which has a bowl) or with a tall 'e' or 's'. In some hands,
    'l' has a small loop at the top similar to 'b' but without
    the closed bowl below.
    Examples: "let" (universal), "laid" (Sedley), "Lauander" (Bulkeley)

m:  Three downstrokes (minims) connected at top. The fundamental
    problem: m, n, u, i are all made of minims (short vertical
    strokes), and in many hands they are virtually identical.
    Context is essential to distinguish them.
    Examples: "medicine" (Brumwich), "melt" (Fanshawe), "mix" (Gibson)

n:  Two minims connected. Same caution as 'm' above -- often
    indistinguishable from 'u' without context. In some hands
    (Bulkeley), 'n' has a slightly more angular top.
    Examples: "not" (universal), "neither" (Bulkeley), "nine" (Sedley)

o:  Generally a round or oval shape, recognizable in most hands.
    Can be open at the top, especially in hasty writing, where
    it may resemble 'a' or 'u'.
    Examples: "of" (universal), "one" (universal), "oyle" (Sedley)

p:  Descends below the baseline. The bowl may be open, sometimes
    making it look like a simple stroke with a small mark. In
    Brumwich, the descender is long and straight.
    Examples: "pound" (Sedley), "put" (universal), "powder" (Brumwich)

q:  Rare outside "quantity" and "quarter." Descender goes below
    baseline, usually curving right (opposite of 'g'). Sometimes
    written with a long tail.
    Examples: "quantity" (Fanshawe), "quarter" (Sedley), "quart" (RCP-502)

r:  TWO VERY DIFFERENT FORMS:
    (1) Long 'r': looks like a modern 'r' with a shoulder -- used
        at the beginning of words and after consonants.
    (2) Round 'r' (or "2-shaped r"): looks like the number 2 or a
        'z'. Used after 'o' and rounded letters. This is extremely
        common and very confusing. When you see what looks like a '2'
        or 'z' after an 'o', it is almost certainly 'r'.
    Examples: "water" (the 'r' at end often uses the 2-shaped form),
    "your" (round r after 'ou'), "more" (round r after 'o')
    The 2-shaped r is visible in Brumwich, Bulkeley, and RCP-502.

s:  THREE DIFFERENT FORMS -- this is one of the hardest letters:
    (1) Long 's': looks like an 'f' without the crossbar, or
        sometimes indistinguishable from 'f'. Descends below
        baseline. Used at the beginning and middle of words.
        THIS IS THE MOST COMMON FORM in these manuscripts.
    (2) Round 's' (modern 's'): used at the END of words. Looks
        like the familiar modern 's'.
    (3) Capital 'S': usually an ornate form with loops, recognizable.
    The long 's' / 'f' confusion is one of the biggest challenges.
    Distinguishing rule: 'f' has a crossbar or a mark through it;
    long 's' does not. But in practice, the crossbar can be subtle.
    Examples: "sicknes" (Bulkeley: long s at start, round s at end),
    "substance" (Bulkeley: long s initially), "diseases" (Bulkeley),
    "sope" (Sedley), "safely" (Bulkeley: long s, then f, then l)

t:  Usually recognizable but can be very short, sometimes barely
    taller than the other minims. The crossbar is often a connecting
    stroke to the next letter rather than a separate mark. At the
    end of words, 't' may have no visible crossbar.
    Examples: "that" (universal), "together" (universal), "take" (universal)

u/v: In this period, 'u' and 'v' are positional variants of the
    SAME letter. The rule: 'v' is used at the BEGINNING of words,
    'u' is used in the MIDDLE of words. This is nearly universal
    across all ten manuscripts.
    Examples: "vnto" = "unto" (Bulkeley), "vsed" = "used" (Bulkeley),
    "uery" = "very" (Brumwich), "uirtue" = "virtue" (Brumwich),
    "haue" = "have" (Bulkeley), "euery" = "every" (Bulkeley)
    Note that some scribes (Sedley, Fanshawe, Ayscough) use a more
    modern distribution, writing 'v' for the consonant sound even
    mid-word, but the u/v interchange is still common.

w:  Generally recognizable. Often written as two 'v' shapes joined,
    looking like a modern 'w'. In some hands, the first stroke loops,
    making it look like 'vv'. Can be large and ornate at the start
    of words.
    Examples: "water" (universal), "with" (universal), "when" (universal)

x:  Rare. Usually looks like a modern 'x' -- two crossing strokes.
    Examples: "mix" (Gibson, Ayscough)

y:  Descender goes below the baseline, often curving left. The upper
    portion looks like a 'v' or a 'u' shape. Can be confused with
    a thorn (the old English letter for "th") -- see abbreviations
    section below.
    Examples: "you" (universal), "your" (universal), "yeares" (Sedley)

z:  Very rare. When it appears, it looks like a modern 'z' or
    sometimes like a '3'.

CAPITAL LETTERS
---------------

Capitals are generally more ornate and larger than their lowercase
equivalents. Key observations:

A:  Usually recognizable but with more flourishes. The crossbar may
    be a loop rather than a straight line.

B:  Often very ornate with large loops. Can look like 'L' in some
    hands if the bottom bowl is open.

C:  Usually a large curved stroke, sometimes with an ornate top.
    Examples: "Cleane" (Brumwich), "Cordiall" (Sedley), "Cures" (Fanshawe)

D:  Often uses the secretary form even when lowercase 'd' uses italic.
    Can look like 'O' when the loop is very round. In Ayscough,
    capital D is very ornate.
    Examples: "Distill" (Ayscough), "Draught" (RCP-502)

E:  Often written as an ornate form that may look like a modern 'E'
    or sometimes like a reversed '3' with flourishes.

F:  See note under lowercase 'f' -- initial "ff" = capital F.
    A single capital 'F' also exists, usually larger and more ornate.
    Examples: "For" vs "ffor" (both appear)

G:  Large and ornate, often with a dramatic descending stroke.
    Examples: "God" (RCP-504), "Gallon" (RCP-504), "Grains" (Ayscough)

H:  Usually recognizable but large. Can have an ornate first stroke.

I/J: Essentially interchangeable as capitals. "I" is used where
    modern English would use "J" in many cases.
    Examples: "Iulip" = "Julep" (Gibson)

L:  A tall stroke, sometimes with a loop. Can look like 'T' if the
    horizontal stroke extends.
    Examples: "Lauander" (Bulkeley), "Lead" (Sedley)

M:  Large form of the lowercase. Still made of minims but taller.
    Often has an initial downstroke before the three humps.
    Examples: "Medicine" (Brumwich), "Masticke" (Fanshawe)

P:  The descender is prominent. The bowl may be large and open.
    Examples: "Plaister" (Sedley, Fanshawe), "Pitck" (Fanshawe)

R:  Usually recognizable with the right leg extending out.
    Can be very ornate in some hands (Sedley has a large looping R).
    Examples: "Runinge" (Sedley), "Rosemary" (Gibson, St. John)

S:  Often very ornate, with large loops. Can look like 'L' or 'F'
    in some hands.
    Examples: "Salve" (Sedley), "Stomache" (Fanshawe), "Spanish" (Sedley)

T:  The crossbar often extends well beyond the vertical stroke.
    In some hands (Brumwich), capital 'T' has a distinctive shape
    with a long horizontal bar and a shorter vertical.
    Examples: "Take" (universal -- this word begins nearly every recipe),
    "The" (universal)

W:  Often very large and ornate, sometimes with initial loops.
    Examples: "Water" (Ayscough), "Waxe" (Fanshawe)

PART 2: EASILY CONFUSED LETTER PAIRS
====================================================================

These are the letter pairs and groups that cause the most errors,
ranked roughly by frequency of confusion:

1. LONG 's' vs 'f'
   The number one source of confusion. Both have ascenders and
   descenders. The theoretical difference is that 'f' has a
   crossbar and long 's' does not, but in practice the crossbar
   can be very faint or formed by the natural pen movement.
   Strategy: Use context. If you see an apparent 'f' in a position
   where 's' makes sense (like "difseases" -- no, it must be
   "diseases"), read it as 's'.
   Examples: In Bulkeley "substance," "sicknes," "diseases" all
   contain long 's' that could be mistaken for 'f'.

2. MINIMS: m, n, u, i, w
   These letters are all formed from short vertical strokes. In
   hasty writing, "minimum" would be a series of identical
   downstrokes with no clear differentiation. You MUST use
   context to determine which letters are intended.
   Strategy: Count the minims, then try different letter
   combinations to find a real word.
   Examples: "manner" in Ayscough -- the "nn" section is just
   four minims that could be "nn," "mu," "um," "mi," etc.

3. 'c' vs 'e'
   Both are small curved strokes. In some hands, 'c' and 'e' are
   nearly identical. The 'e' may have a slightly more closed top
   or a small horizontal bar.
   Strategy: Context and spelling knowledge.

4. 'u' vs 'v' (as discussed above, these are the same letter
   with positional rules -- not truly "confused" but unfamiliar
   to modern readers)

5. Secretary 'd' vs 'e'
   The secretary 'd' with its left-looping ascender can look like
   an 'e' to modern eyes, especially when written small.
   Strategy: If an apparent 'e' has a taller stroke than usual,
   consider whether it might be a secretary 'd'.

6. 'y' vs thorn
   The letter thorn (representing "th") looks like 'y' in this
   period. So "ye" = "the," "yt" = "that," "yr" = "their/there."
   (See abbreviations section.)

7. 'a' vs 'o'
   In hasty writing, both are round strokes. Open 'a' can look
   like 'o' and vice versa.
   Examples: This is a frequent source of error when reading
   Brumwich's hand.

8. Capital 'I' vs 'J' vs 'T'
   All three can look similar in ornate hands. Capital I and J
   are essentially the same letter in this period.

PART 3: ABBREVIATIONS AND SYMBOLS
====================================================================

THORN AND RELATED ABBREVIATIONS
--------------------------------
The old English letter "thorn" (representing "th") survives in this
period and looks like 'y'. Combined with superscript letters:

  ye = the         (universal across MSS)
  yt = that        (seen in RCP-504, St. John)
  yr = their/there (seen in St. John: "yr first 2 qrts")
  ym = them
  yn = then

These are EXTREMELY common. When you see 'y' followed by a
superscript letter, it is almost always a thorn abbreviation.
Examples: RCP-504 has many instances of "ye" for "the":
"ye oyl of Exeter," "ye grieved place," "ye pouder"

SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS
--------------------
Superscript (raised) letters indicate that intervening letters
have been omitted:

  wth = with       (very common -- St. John: "wth the yolke")
  wch = which      (St. John: "wch keep by it selfe")
  Mrs = Mistress   (Brumwich: "Mrs Worsley")
  Mr = Mister      (Ayscough: "Mr Gilbert," Fanshawe: "Mr. Stones")
  Sr = Sir         (Fanshawe: "Sr. R. Stone")

APOTHECARY AND MEASUREMENT SYMBOLS
------------------------------------
These manuscripts are full of medical/recipe content and use
standard apothecary symbols:

  (ounce symbol -- looks like a 'z' with a line through it or
   a cursive 'z'): This represents "ounce" and is written as the
   symbol for ounce in the original, transcribed as a number +
   "ounces" or with the symbol.
   Examples: Ayscough uses these extensively: "4 oz of filed harts
   horne 2 oz"

  (dram symbol -- looks like a 'z' with a tail or a '3' shape):
   Represents "dram" (a unit of weight = 1/8 ounce).
   Examples: Ayscough: "Gallingall 1 dram"

  (scruple symbol): Represents "scruple" (1/3 dram). Less common
   but appears in Brumwich: "2 scruples"

  ana = "of each, equal amounts" -- a Latin abbreviation common
   in medical recipes.
   Examples: Gibson: "each ana 2 drams," St. John: "ana M:i:"

  ss (or with a line) = "semis" = one half
   Examples: RCP-502: "oz i-ss" = one and a half ounces

  M = "manipulus" = a handful
   Examples: St. John: "ana M:i:" = of each one handful

  R or Rx = "Recipe" = "Take" (the opening of a prescription)

ROMAN NUMERALS
--------------
Roman numerals are used for quantities, especially with apothecary
symbols:

  i, ij, iij, iiij = 1, 2, 3, 4
  j at the end of a roman numeral replaces the final i
  Examples: RCP-502: "oz ij" = 2 ounces, "oz i" = 1 ounce

LINE-END MARKS
--------------
  = (double hyphen at end of line) indicates a word is broken
    across two lines. This is NOT an equals sign.
    Examples: Sedley uses this consistently: "incor=" continuing as
    "porated" on the next line; "soverai=" continuing as "gne"

  : or :/ at the end of a passage may indicate the end of a recipe
    or section.
    Examples: Gibson: "& att bedd tyme:/"

THE AMPERSAND (&)
-----------------
The ampersand is universal and usually recognizable, though its
form varies considerably across scribes. In some hands (Bulkeley)
it is a simple curved mark; in others (Sedley) it is more ornate.
It always means "and."

THE CARET MARK (^)
-------------------
A ^ symbol between words or above the line indicates an insertion --
text that was added after the initial writing. The inserted text
usually appears above the line.
Examples: Sedley: "h^eales" where the scribe inserted letters;
St. John: "& ^ put the seeds" where text was inserted above.

PART 4: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
====================================================================

Early modern English spelling was not standardized. The same word
can be spelled differently by different scribes, or even differently
by the SAME scribe on the same page. Key patterns:

VOWEL VARIATIONS
-----------------
- 'i' and 'y' are interchangeable: "oyle" / "oil," "oyntment" /
  "ointment," "drye" / "dry," "fyre" / "fire"
  Examples: Brumwich writes "oyle," Bulkeley writes "drye"

- 'e' is often added at the end of words where modern English
  drops it: "giue" = "give," "haue" = "have," "vsed" = "used,"
  "kinde" = "kind," "herbe" = "herb"
  Examples: Bulkeley consistently uses these terminal 'e' spellings

- Double vowels where modern English uses single: "bee" = "be,"
  "seedes" = "seeds," "herbes" = "herbs"
  Examples: RCP-502: "halfe bee consumed"

THE u/v INTERCHANGE (critical -- mentioned above but bears repeating)
- 'v' at start of word, 'u' in middle, regardless of sound:
  "vnto" = "unto," "vsed" = "used," "vpon" = "upon"
  "haue" = "have," "euery" = "every," "giue" = "give"
  This is the SINGLE most common spelling difference from
  modern English and appears in virtually every line of text.

THE i/j INTERCHANGE
- 'I' is used for both I and J sounds: "Iuce" = "Juice,"
  "Iulip" = "Julep," "Iune" = "June"
  Examples: Gibson page 20: "Iuce of Sallendine," "Iulip"

CONSONANT VARIATIONS
---------------------
- Double consonants where modern English uses single, and vice
  versa: "putt" = "put," "lett" = "let," "stopt" = "stopped,"
  "gott" = "got"
  Examples: Ayscough: "putt into your water and lett it stand"

- 'ck' often written as 'ck' or 'ke': "drinke" = "drink,"
  "worke" = "work," "sticke" = "stick"

- Terminal '-tion' is often '-cion' or '-sion':
  "composition" (Bulkeley), "admonition" (Bulkeley)

- 'wh' sometimes written as 'w' alone: "wich" = "which"

SPECIFIC COMMON SPELLINGS
---------------------------
These spellings appear repeatedly across multiple manuscripts:

  physick = physic         oyle = oil
  plaister = plaster       receite/receipt = recipe
  searecloth = cerecloth   sirrup/syrrup = syrup
  pouder/powder (both)     suger = sugar
  distill/distil (both)    clister = clyster/enema
  pottell = pottle (2 qt)  handfull = handful
  spoonefull = spoonful    drame/dram (both)
  ounce/ownce (both)       gallon/galon (both)
  beries = berries         bloudy = bloody
  phisick = physic         soe = so
  thereoff = thereof       therewith = therewith (one word)
  att = at                 bee = be
  hee = he                 shee = she

PART 5: WORD SPACING AND CONNECTIONS
====================================================================

Word spacing in these manuscripts is inconsistent. Key observations:

- Words are sometimes run together, especially short words:
  "init" = "in it," "tothe" = "to the," "tobe" = "to be"
  Examples: This is most common in Brumwich and RCP-502.

- Conversely, a single word may have a gap in the middle,
  especially compound words: "eye bright" = "eyebright,"
  "sear cloth" = "searcloth," "clove gilly flowers" =
  "clovegillyflowers"

- Connecting strokes between letters within a word can extend
  to bridge between words, making separate words look like one.

- Prepositions and articles are often crowded close to the
  following word.

STRATEGIES:
- If a sequence of letters does not form a recognizable word,
  try inserting a space at different points.
- If two apparent words do not make sense, try reading them
  as one word.
- Recipes have predictable structures ("Take... of each...
  mix... boil...") that help determine word boundaries.

====================================================================
PART 6: LINE AND PAGE ORGANIZATION
====================================================================

RECIPE STRUCTURE
-----------------
These are recipe/medical manuscripts, and they follow predictable
patterns:

1. TITLE: Usually on its own line, sometimes underlined or in
   larger script. Describes the ailment or preparation.
   Examples: "A water of Exelent uirtue to helpe the weake or
   dimme sight" (Brumwich), "A Salve for sundry vses called the
   Leaden plaister" (Sedley), "Of Lauander spike, the temperature"
   (Bulkeley)

2. RECIPE NUMBER: Some manuscripts number their recipes with
   Roman numerals (Sedley uses "XXVII," "XXVIII," "XXIX").
   Others use Arabic numerals or no numbers at all.

3. BODY: Almost always begins with "Take" -- this is the most
   reliable word in the entire corpus. The word "Take" appears
   at the start of almost every recipe and is a key landmark
   for orientation.

4. INGREDIENTS listed with quantities, using apothecary symbols
   and measures.

5. INSTRUCTIONS for preparation: "boyle," "distill," "straine,"
   "mix," "bruse" (bruise), "stamp" (pound), "steep," etc.

6. ATTRIBUTION: Many recipes note their source: "commended by
   Sir James Langham" (Brumwich), "by Dr. Burlase directions"
   (RCP-502), "Mr Gilbert's Water" (Ayscough), "My Mother A Han"
   (Fanshawe).

PAGE NUMBERS
-------------
Page numbers appear at the top or in margins. They may use
Arabic or Roman numerals. Some manuscripts have folio numbers
(recto/verso) rather than page numbers.
Examples: Sedley has "20" and "21" at page tops; Ayscough has
"34" and "35"

MARGINAL NOTES
--------------
Some manuscripts have notes in the margins indicating sources,
dates, or cross-references. Fanshawe places attribution notes
in curly braces in the margin: "{Sr. R. Stone A Fan}",
"{My Mother A Han}".

PARAGRAPH BREAKS
-----------------
New recipes typically begin after a blank line or half-line.
The title is often offset or written in a slightly different
style (larger, underlined, or with more space around it).

====================================================================
PART 7: SCRIBE-SPECIFIC OBSERVATIONS
====================================================================

Understanding that each scribe has individual characteristics helps
calibrate expectations when encountering a new hand.

BRUMWICH (MS160) -- mid-17th century
- Relatively small, cramped hand
- Heavy use of secretary letterforms, especially secretary 'd'
- Consistent u/v interchange: "uirtue," "uery," "euening"
- Words can run together; spacing is tight
- Long 's' used extensively
- Informal, practical tone

BULKELEY (MS169) -- late 16th/early 17th century
- Elegant, well-formed italic mixed with secretary
- Very readable compared to others -- clear letter separation
- Secretary 'd' is prominent and consistent
- Careful spelling: "espetially" (for "especially"), "cheifely"
- Uses "vnto," "vsed" consistently
- Long lines with good horizontal flow
- This is a COPYING hand -- Bulkeley is transcribing from a
  printed source (herbal/medical text), not composing

SEDLEY (MS534) -- 1686
- Clear, relatively modern-looking hand
- Good spacing between words
- Uses "=" for line-break hyphens consistently
- Roman numeral recipe numbers (XXVII, XXVIII, XXIX)
- Mix of formal and informal: "you" alongside "your"
- Two-column layout on some pages (left page, right page)

AYSCOUGH (MS1026) -- early 18th century
- Neat, practiced hand with good spacing
- More modern-looking than earlier manuscripts
- Uses standard apothecary symbols extensively
- Clear separation between recipes
- Uses italic 'd' predominantly

RCP-502 -- mid-17th century
- Dense, somewhat hasty hand
- Many abbreviations and symbols
- Secretary letterforms prominent
- Cramped spacing
- Uses Roman numerals for quantities with apothecary symbols

RCP-504 -- mid-17th century
- Mixed hand, moderately legible
- Heavy use of "ye," "yt," "yr" (thorn abbreviations)
- Uses superscript letters: "wth," "wch"
- Some words partially illegible due to ink spread or fading

FANSHAWE (MS7113) -- mid-17th century
- Clear, well-spaced hand
- Relatively easy to read
- Marginal attributions in brackets
- Uses "==" to mark recipe titles (as underline equivalent)
- Consistent spelling within the manuscript
- Good paragraph separation between recipes

ST. JOHN (MS4338) -- mid-17th century
- Hasty, somewhat difficult hand
- Irregular letter sizes and spacing
- Abbreviations common: "yr," "wch," "wth"
- Uses a mix of secretary and italic forms
- Some passages quite cramped

GIBSON (MS311) -- late 17th century
- Moderately legible, informal hand
- Two pages studied (pp. 20 and 40) show consistent style
- Uses "Iuce" for "Juice" -- I/J interchange
- Relatively modern spelling compared to earlier MSS
- Good recipe structure with clear titles

====================================================================
PART 8: STRATEGIES FOR TRANSCRIPTION
====================================================================

Based on studying all ten manuscripts, here is a recommended
approach for transcribing a new, unseen page:

STEP 1: ORIENT YOURSELF
- Identify page numbers, titles, and recipe boundaries first
- Look for "Take" -- it appears at the start of nearly every
  recipe and is one of the most recognizable words
- Identify the scribe's general style: is it more secretary or
  more italic? How careful/hasty is the hand?

STEP 2: BUILD A LETTER KEY FOR THIS SCRIBE
- Find known words and use them to identify letter forms:
  "the," "and" (&), "of," "Take," "water," "with," "put"
- Pay special attention to: which form of 'd' does this
  scribe use? Which form of 's'? Which form of 'r'?
- Look at how the scribe writes 'e,' 'a,' 'o' -- these
  small vowels are often the key to the rest

STEP 3: READ RECIPE BY RECIPE
- Use the predictable recipe structure as a scaffold:
  Title, then "Take [ingredient], [quantity]..."
  then instructions: "boyle," "distill," "straine," "mix"
- Ingredient names recur: "water," "oyle," "wine," "sugar,"
  "powder," "ounce," "dram," "handfull," "spoonefull"

STEP 4: HANDLE DIFFICULT WORDS
- Use context: what kind of recipe is this? Eye remedy?
  Cordial? Plaster? This narrows possible ingredients.
- Count minims carefully for m/n/u/i sequences
- Check both long 's' and 'f' readings
- Remember u/v and i/j interchanges
- If a word has an unfamiliar spelling, try reading it
  aloud -- early modern spelling is often phonetic

STEP 5: VERIFY
- Read back your transcription: does each recipe make
  practical sense as instructions?
- Check that ingredient lists have quantities
- Watch for common OCR-style errors: 'c' for 'e',
  long 's' for 'f', minim confusion

PART 10: CRITICAL RULES SUMMARY
====================================================================

If you remember nothing else, remember these rules:

1. u/v ARE THE SAME LETTER. 'v' at the start of words, 'u' in
   the middle. "vnto" = unto, "haue" = have, "uery" = very.

2. LONG 's' LOOKS LIKE 'f'. It is NOT 'f'. Use context.

3. "ff" AT THE START OF A WORD = CAPITAL F.
   "ffor" = "For," "ffellons" = "Fellons"

4. "ye" = "the" (thorn abbreviation, not the word "ye").
   Similarly: "yt" = "that," "yr" = "their"

5. I AND J ARE THE SAME LETTER. "Iuce" = "Juice," "Iulip" = "Julep"

6. MINIM LETTERS (m, n, u, i) are often indistinguishable.
   Count strokes and use context.

7. SECRETARY 'd' loops LEFT -- it does not look like a modern 'd'.
   It can look like a Greek delta or a looped 'e'.

8. SPELLING IS NOT STANDARDIZED. Sound out unfamiliar words
   phonetically. "Physick" = "physic," "oyle" = "oil,"
   "receite" = "recipe."

9. "Take" STARTS NEARLY EVERY RECIPE. It is your anchor word.

10. WORD BOUNDARIES ARE UNRELIABLE. Words may run together or
    be split. Use context to determine where words begin and end.
