SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from 5 Early Modern English Manuscript Pages (c. 1580-1690)
Training Sources: Brumwich MS160, Bulkeley MS169, Sedley MS534, Ayscough MS1026, RCP MS502
================================================================================

This guide was produced by studying five manuscript pages from different scribes,
each paired with its correct transcription. No external paleography guide,
alphabet chart, or vocabulary list was used. Everything below was discovered
by comparing the handwriting to the known transcriptions across all five hands.

================================================================================
PART 1: THE ALPHABET — INDIVIDUAL LETTERFORMS
================================================================================

The following describes how each letter of the alphabet typically appears across
these five early modern hands. Where scribes differ significantly, the variants
are noted.

LOWERCASE LETTERS
-----------------

a:  Appears in two main forms:
    (1) A round form resembling a modern lowercase "a" — a closed loop with a
        short stroke on the right. This is the most common form across all five
        scribes.
    (2) An open-topped form that can look like "u" — particularly in Brumwich,
        where "water" sometimes has an "a" that does not fully close.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can resemble "u" or "o" when hastily written.
    Examples: "Take" (appears in every manuscript), "water" (Brumwich, Sedley)

b:  Formed with an upright ascender stroke and a loop at the baseline curving
    right. The ascender in Bulkeley is tall and thin; in Brumwich it is shorter.
    In RCP MS502, the "b" loop is sometimes open, making it look like an "l"
    with a following curve.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can resemble "l" if the loop does not close.
    Examples: "blood" (Bulkeley), "back" (Sedley), "beries" (RCP MS502)

c:  A simple curved stroke open to the right, similar to modern "c". In some
    hands (especially Sedley and Ayscough), it can look nearly identical to "e"
    because the scribe adds a small horizontal tick.
    CONFUSION RISK: Easily confused with "e" in hasty writing.
    Examples: "coales" (Sedley), "cordiall" (Ayscough), "clister" (RCP MS502)

d:  Appears in two major forms across these manuscripts:
    (1) Secretary "d": The ascender curves backward (to the left) in a loop,
        giving it a distinctive backward-leaning appearance. This is very common
        in Bulkeley and Brumwich.
    (2) Italic/round "d": The ascender goes straight up or slightly right,
        resembling a modern "d". More common in Sedley and Ayscough.
    CONFUSION RISK: Secretary "d" can look like the letter combination "cl" to
    modern eyes. The backward loop is diagnostic.
    Examples: "diseases" (Bulkeley — secretary d), "dipped" (Sedley — rounder d),
    "Distill" (Ayscough — mixed form)

e:  Usually a small loop or a curved stroke. In secretary hand (Bulkeley,
    Brumwich), it often looks like a reversed "3" or a small backward loop.
    In the more italic hands (Sedley, Ayscough), it is more recognizably modern.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can look like "c" or even "o" when small.
    Examples: "the" (all manuscripts), "eyes" (Brumwich), "espetially" (Bulkeley)

f:  Has a descender that drops below the baseline (unlike modern "f" which sits
    on the line). It typically has a crossbar. In all five hands, "f" looks
    somewhat like a long "s" (see below) but is distinguished by its crossbar
    or double stroke.
    CONFUSION RISK: Easily confused with long s. The crossbar is the key
    differentiator. If you see what looks like a long "s" with a horizontal
    line through it, it is "f".
    Examples: "fine" (Brumwich), "flowers" (Bulkeley), "fire" (Sedley)

g:  Formed with a loop above the baseline and a descender that curves left,
    often forming a closed or open loop below the line. In Bulkeley, the
    descender loop is quite pronounced. In Brumwich, it is more of a simple
    tail.
    CONFUSION RISK: The top loop can look like "q" in some hands, but "g"
    descenders tend to curve left while "q" descenders go straight down or right.
    Examples: "good" (Brumwich, Sedley), "giue" (Bulkeley), "glass" (Ayscough)

h:  An ascender with a curved stroke at the baseline that extends right. In
    most hands, the right stroke curves down slightly but stays above the
    baseline. In Brumwich, "h" can look like "li" because the curve is minimal.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can look like "li" or "b" depending on the hand.
    Examples: "halfe" (all manuscripts), "houre" (Sedley), "houres" (Ayscough)

i:  A simple minim stroke (a single short vertical stroke). In these
    manuscripts, "i" is rarely dotted — this is a period convention. Sometimes
    "i" is written as "j" at the end of Roman numerals (e.g., "iij" for 3).
    CONFUSION RISK: Nearly identical to the individual strokes in "m", "n",
    "u", and "w". Context is critical. This is probably the single biggest
    source of difficulty in reading these hands.
    Examples: "it" (all manuscripts), "in" (all manuscripts)

j:  Rarely used as a distinct letter. When it appears, it looks like an "i"
    with a descender curving left. Most often seen in Roman numerals where the
    final "i" is written as "j" (e.g., "iij" = 3 in RCP MS502).
    Examples: "℥ij" (RCP MS502), "℥jss" (RCP MS502)

k:  Relatively rare. Formed with an ascender and two diagonal strokes meeting
    at the midpoint. Can look angular and distinctive.
    Examples: "keep" (Ayscough, Sedley), "kinde" (Bulkeley)

l:  A simple ascender stroke, usually without a loop at the top. In Bulkeley
    and Brumwich, it sometimes has a small loop. The bottom may have a small
    rightward hook.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can look like "b" (if a loop is present) or tall "e".
    Examples: "laid" (Sedley), "Lauander" (Bulkeley), "let" (Ayscough)

m:  Three connected minim strokes. The challenge is that in secretary and
    mixed hands, these minims can be indistinguishable from "ni", "iu", "wi",
    etc. Across all five manuscripts, "m" is essentially three humps of equal
    height connected at the baseline.
    CONFUSION RISK: The most problematic letter. Three minims could be "m",
    "ni", "iu", "in" + part of another letter, etc. Count minims carefully
    and rely on word context.
    Examples: "medicine" (Brumwich), "mixed" (Bulkeley), "mingle" (Sedley)

n:  Two connected minim strokes. Same confusion issues as "m" — see above.
    CONFUSION RISK: "n" versus "u" is extremely common in these hands because
    both are two minim strokes. Often only context determines which is meant.
    Examples: "not" (Bulkeley), "neither" (Bulkeley), "next" (Ayscough)

o:  A round, closed loop. Generally one of the more recognizable letters across
    all five hands. Sometimes it is slightly open at the top.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can look like "a" when the latter is written with a full loop.
    Examples: "one" (Brumwich, Sedley), "oyle" (Sedley, Ayscough)

p:  A descender stroke with a loop or bowl to the right at the midline.
    Generally recognizable across all hands. The descender goes below the
    baseline.
    Examples: "put" (all manuscripts), "powder" (Brumwich), "plaister" (Sedley)

q:  Rare. A round bowl with a descender going down and to the right (opposite
    direction from "g"). Usually only appears in "qu-" combinations.
    Examples: "quarter" (Sedley, Ayscough), "quantety" (Brumwich), "quart" (RCP MS502)

r:  Appears in two main forms:
    (1) A short stroke with a rightward shoulder — resembles a modern "r" but
        shorter and often without a clear vertical stem. Common in Ayscough and
        Sedley.
    (2) A "2-shaped" r: looks like the number 2, used especially after round
        letters like "o". This is common in Bulkeley and Brumwich.
    CONFUSION RISK: The 2-shaped "r" can be mistaken for an actual "2" or for
    "z". Short "r" can look like "v" or "n" in some hands.
    Examples: "rose" (Brumwich — both forms visible), "refreshinge" (Bulkeley),
    "reines" (Sedley)

s:  The most complex letter in these manuscripts. Three forms are used:
    (1) Long s: Looks like "f" without a crossbar. A tall vertical stroke that
        may extend below the baseline. Used at the beginning and middle of words.
        This is the most common form across all five manuscripts.
    (2) Round/final s: Looks like a modern "s". Used at the end of words.
    (3) Sigma-shaped s: A form that looks like the Greek lowercase sigma or a
        "6". Used occasionally, particularly in Bulkeley at word endings.
    RULE: Long s is typically used at the start or middle of words; round s
    appears at the end. This convention is consistent across all five hands.
    CONFUSION RISK: Long s / f confusion is the most famous challenge in early
    modern handwriting. Remember: f has a crossbar, long s does not.
    Examples: "sicknes" (Brumwich — long s at start, round s at end),
    "diseases" (Bulkeley — long s medially, round s finally),
    "stirringe" (Sedley — long s at start)

t:  A short vertical stroke with a crossbar. In most hands it is fairly
    recognizable, though the crossbar may be faint or connected to the next
    letter. In Brumwich, "t" is sometimes very short and can look like "c".
    CONFUSION RISK: Can look like "c" or "l" if the crossbar is missing.
    Examples: "take" (all manuscripts), "them" (all manuscripts)

u/v: In early modern writing, "u" and "v" are variant forms of the same letter.
    The general convention across all five manuscripts:
    - "v" is used at the BEGINNING of words: "vnto" (= unto), "vsed" (= used),
      "vpon" (= upon), "vse" (= use), "uery" (= very in Brumwich)
    - "u" is used in the MIDDLE or END of words: "haue" (= have), "giue" (= give),
      "euery" (= every)
    However, Brumwich reverses this for some words, writing "u" initially where
    we would expect "v": "uirtue", "uery" — so the convention is not absolute.
    CONFUSION RISK: Beyond the u/v interchange, "u" is two minim strokes and
    therefore identical in form to "n" in many hands.
    Examples: "vnto" (Bulkeley), "vsed" (Bulkeley, RCP MS502), "uirtue" (Brumwich),
    "haue" (Bulkeley)

w:  Formed as a connected series of minim-like strokes, usually appearing as
    a broader "uu" or two "v" shapes joined. It is wider than other minim-based
    letters, which helps identification. In Sedley and Ayscough, "w" is
    relatively clear. In Brumwich, it can be compressed and harder to
    distinguish from "vu" or "un".
    Examples: "water" (all manuscripts), "wine" (Brumwich, Sedley),
    "wormewood" (RCP MS502)

x:  Rare. Two diagonal strokes crossing. Usually recognizable when it appears.
    Examples: "Exelent" (Brumwich), "flux" (Sedley)

y:  A letter with a descender, formed somewhat like a "v" or "u" shape above
    the baseline with a tail curving down and to the left. In Bulkeley, the
    descender is long and hooked. In Brumwich, it is shorter.
    CONFUSION RISK: Can sometimes look like "p" if the descender is straight,
    or like "g" if the curve is pronounced.
    Examples: "you" (Brumwich, Ayscough), "your" (Sedley), "yeares" (Sedley)

z:  Very rare. When it appears it has a horizontal top and bottom stroke with
    a diagonal connecting them, sometimes with a descender.
    Examples: "glazed" (Sedley)

UPPERCASE LETTERS
-----------------

Uppercase letters in these manuscripts are often elaborated versions of their
lowercase forms, with additional flourishes. Key observations:

A:  Usually formed with two diagonal strokes meeting at a peak, sometimes
    with a crossbar, sometimes without. In Ayscough, capital "A" is very
    distinctive — often written as a large triangle-like form. In Brumwich,
    it can be quite simple.
    Examples: "A water" (Brumwich), "A Salve" (Sedley), "A Clister" (Ayscough)

C:  A large curved stroke. In Sedley, capital "C" is quite ornate with a
    flourish at the top. In Brumwich, it is simpler.
    Examples: "Cleane" (Brumwich), "Cordiall" (Sedley), "Camomill" (Ayscough)

D:  Often a large version of secretary "d" with an exaggerated backward loop.
    In Ayscough, it is more upright and rounded.
    Examples: "Distill" (Ayscough), "Decoction" (Ayscough), "Draught" (RCP MS502)

F/ff: Capital "F" is sometimes written as "ff" (double lowercase f) rather
    than a true capital. This is a distinctive period convention visible in
    Sedley: "ffor A Tetter" and "ffellons". This is NOT the word starting
    with two f's — it is a way of writing capital F.
    Examples: "ffor" (Sedley = "For"), "ffellons" (Sedley = "Fellons")

L:  A tall ascender with a wide loop, often quite ornamental.
    Examples: "Lauander" (Bulkeley), "Leaden" (Sedley), "Lemon" (Sedley)

M:  An enlarged form of lowercase "m" with initial flourishes. Often has a
    leading stroke that swoops down before the three minims.
    Examples: "Medicine" (Brumwich), "Mr" (Ayscough), "Mortar" (Sedley)

S:  Capital "S" is usually a large, ornamental form distinct from both long s
    and round s. It often has a sweeping curve.
    Examples: "Salve" (Sedley), "Spanish" (Sedley), "Spoonefulls" (Brumwich)

T:  An enlarged "t" with a wide crossbar, often with flourishes. In Sedley
    and Ayscough, it can be quite decorative.
    Examples: "Take" (all manuscripts), "The" (Bulkeley, Brumwich)

W:  An enlarged "w" with wider strokes. Often begins with a downward swoop.
    Examples: "Water" (Brumwich), "Wine" (Bulkeley)

PART 2: LETTERS MOST LIKELY TO BE CONFUSED
================================================================================

Based on studying all five hands, the following confusions are the most
dangerous and frequent. A transcriber should be especially cautious with these:

1. LONG S vs. F
   Both are tall vertical strokes extending below or near the baseline.
   The ONLY reliable difference: f has a crossbar or double-stroke; long s
   does not. In hasty writing the crossbar of "f" can be faint.
   Strategy: If the tall letter has NO crossbar, it is long s. If it does,
   it is f. Also consider context: "ficknes" makes no sense; "sicknes" does.

2. MINIM LETTERS: m, n, u, i, w
   These are all built from identical short vertical strokes (minims).
   m = 3 minims, n = 2, u = 2, i = 1, w = 4 (roughly).
   In practice, the strokes often blur together. "minim" itself would be a
   nightmare to read.
   Strategy: Count minims, but rely heavily on word context. If you see 5
   minims between two clear letters, consider what combinations of m/n/u/i
   make a real word. Knowledge of common early modern spellings is essential.

3. U vs. V (interchangeable)
   These are positional variants of the same letter. "v" at word start,
   "u" in the middle. "vnto" = unto, "haue" = have, "uery" = very.
   Strategy: Apply the positional rule, but be aware that some scribes
   (Brumwich) are inconsistent.

4. C vs. E vs. T (in some hands)
   In smaller writing, these three can look very similar — all are short
   strokes with horizontal elements. The crossbar of "t" may look like
   the horizontal stroke of "c" or the loop of "e".
   Strategy: Look at letter height (t is usually taller), context, and
   whether there is a clear crossbar.

5. SECRETARY D vs. "CL"
   The backward-looping secretary "d" looks like the letters "c" and "l"
   written together to a modern reader.
   Strategy: If what looks like "cl" makes no sense but "d" does, it is
   probably a secretary d. Example: "cliseases" is not a word; "diseases" is.

6. R (2-shaped) vs. Z or the numeral 2
   The 2-shaped "r" variant looks like a "2" or a "z".
   Strategy: Context. If a "2" or "z" appears after a round letter (o, b, p)
   and a letter-form reading makes better sense, it is likely a 2-shaped r.

7. Y vs. THORN (rare)
   In early modern writing, the letter thorn (an Old English letter for "th")
   was sometimes written to look like "y". "ye" = "the", "yt" = "that".
   In these five manuscripts, this does not appear frequently, but be aware
   of it.


================================================================================
PART 3: ABBREVIATIONS, SYMBOLS, AND SHORTHAND
================================================================================

These manuscripts contain several common abbreviations and symbols:

APOTHECARY/MEASUREMENT SYMBOLS
-------------------------------
These appear especially in Ayscough and RCP MS502:

- ℥  = ounce (a symbol resembling "oz" or a stylized "z" with a line)
  Example: "4 ℥ of filed harts horne" (Ayscough) = 4 ounces
  Example: "℥ij" (RCP MS502) = 2 ounces

- ʒ  = dram (a symbol resembling a "3" with a crossbar or a stylized "z")
  Example: "Gallingall 1 ʒ" (Ayscough) = 1 dram of galangal

- ℈  = scruple (a smaller unit, looks like a small symbol with a tail)
  Example: "℈iiij" (RCP MS502) = 4 scruples

- ss = semis = one half (often follows a measurement)
  Example: "℥i-ss" (RCP MS502) = 1 and a half ounces
  Example: "℥ss" (RCP MS502) = half an ounce

ROMAN NUMERALS
--------------
Used extensively for quantities across all manuscripts:
- j = 1 (the final "i" in a Roman numeral is written as "j")
  Example: "℥i" = 1 ounce, "℥ij" = 2 ounces, "iij" = 3
- Written in lowercase, sometimes hard to distinguish from surrounding text
- The "j" ending is diagnostic of Roman numerals in these texts

WORD ABBREVIATIONS
------------------
- "wch" = "which" (Ayscough, RCP MS502)
  The scribe writes "w" then "ch" with a superscript mark or contraction.
  Example: "in wch infuse i2 hours more" (RCP MS502)

- "wth" = "with" (RCP MS502)
  Example: "mix six spoonefull of boys urine, the Electuary... wth the
  yolke of an egg" (RCP MS502)

- "yt" = "that" (when it appears — rare in these particular manuscripts)

- "ye" = "the" (when it appears — uses thorn character resembling y)

- Superscript letters: Some scribes write abbreviated words with the final
  letters raised above the line. For instance, "Mr" for "Master" with the
  "r" raised (Ayscough: "Mr Gilbert's Water", "Mr Shaw's Directions").

LINE-END HYPHENATION
--------------------
When a word is broken across lines, a "=" sign or "-" is used:
- Sedley uses "=" for word breaks: "incor=" + "porated", "soverai=" + "gne"
- Bulkeley uses "-" for word breaks: "dif-" + "ference"
- This convention is consistent within each scribe but varies between scribes.

THE AMPERSAND (&)
-----------------
Used universally across all five manuscripts for "and". The form varies:
- In Bulkeley, it is an elaborate cursive form
- In Brumwich, it is simpler, sometimes looking like a "7" or "+"
- In Sedley and Ayscough, it is a standard early modern ampersand
It always means "and" — this is completely consistent.

CARET INSERTION (^)
-------------------
Sedley uses a caret mark to insert text above the line:
- "h^eales" shows that "eales" was inserted above after "h" was written
  (Sedley p. 21, line about "breakes the ffellons...")

SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS IN ABBREVIATIONS
-------------------------------------
Common across all hands when abbreviating:
- Raised letters indicate omitted characters
- "yr" can mean "your", "Mr" means "Master", etc.

PAGE NUMBERS AND RECIPE NUMBERS
--------------------------------
- Page numbers appear at top of pages, sometimes in margins
  Example: "{6}" and "{7}" in Brumwich, "20" and "21" in Sedley,
  "34" and "35" in Ayscough
- Sedley uses Roman numerals for recipe numbers: "XXVII", "XXVIII", "XXIX"
- Ayscough uses Arabic page numbers

PART 4: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
================================================================================

Early modern English spelling was not standardized. The following patterns
appear consistently across multiple manuscripts:

VOWEL VARIATIONS
----------------
- "u" for modern "v" (interior position): "haue" = have, "giue" = give,
  "euery" = every (Bulkeley throughout)
- "v" for modern "u" (initial position): "vnto" = unto, "vsed" = used,
  "vse" = use (Bulkeley, RCP MS502)
- "i" for modern "y": rarely, but "drye" = dry (Bulkeley)
- "y" for modern "i": "drye", "airie" (Bulkeley), "oyle" (Sedley, Ayscough)
- "e" often added at the end of words: "thinne" = thin, "hotte" = hot,
  "goode" = good, "fyre" = fire

-IE/-Y ENDINGS
--------------
- Words ending in modern "-y" are often spelled "-ie" or "-ey":
  "espetially" (Bulkeley), "prettey" or "pritey" (Brumwich)
  "drye" (Bulkeley), "honey" (Brumwich)

DOUBLE LETTERS
--------------
- Double letters appear where modern English has singles:
  "distilled" (Bulkeley, Ayscough), "stirringe" (Sedley),
  "Spoonefulls" (Brumwich, Ayscough), "allmost" (Ayscough)
- And singles where modern English has doubles:
  "stoped" (Brumwich, Ayscough), "strained" etc.

-TION ENDINGS
-------------
- "tion" is sometimes written as "cion" or "sion":
  "composition" (Bulkeley), "admonition" (Bulkeley)
  These are generally spelled out, not abbreviated in these manuscripts.

-INGE ENDINGS
-------------
- The "-ing" ending is frequently written as "-inge":
  "stirringe" (Sedley), "beginninge" (Bulkeley), "refreshinge" (Bulkeley),
  "lettinge" (Bulkeley), "makinge" (Bulkeley), "dressinge" (Sedley)
  This is one of the most consistent spelling features across all five hands.

-ETH ENDINGS (verb forms)
--------------------------
- Third-person singular verbs sometimes end in "-eth":
  "provokes" and "takes" in Sedley suggest this scribe uses modern "-es"
  But "consistinge" (Bulkeley) preserves the older participial form.

SPECIFIC WORD SPELLINGS
------------------------
These recurrent words appear with non-modern spellings:

- "phisick" / "physick" = physic/medicine
- "oyle" = oil (Sedley, Ayscough)
- "plaister" = plaster (Sedley)
- "searclothes" = cerecloth (Sedley — waxed bandage)
- "sope" = soap (Sedley)
- "collour" = color (Sedley)
- "cloath" / "cloathes" = cloth/clothes (Sedley, Ayscough)
- "bloudy" = bloody (Sedley)
- "drinke" = drink (Brumwich)
- "howers" / "houres" / "houre" = hours (Brumwich, Sedley, Ayscough)
- "sponfull" / "spoonefull" = spoonful (Brumwich, Ayscough)
- "pinte" = pint (Brumwich, Ayscough)
- "receeiue" / "receiue" = receive (Brumwich)
- "sallendine" / "Sallandine" = celandine (Brumwich, Sedley)
- "fennill" / "fenill" / "fenell" = fennel (Brumwich, RCP MS502)
- "camomill" = chamomile (Ayscough, RCP MS502)
- "wormewood" = wormwood (RCP MS502)
- "terpintine" = turpentine (RCP MS502)
- "whitewyne" = white wine (RCP MS502 — sometimes written as one word)

THE SUFFIX "-LY"
----------------
Often spelled "-ley" or "-ly" interchangeably:
- "finly" (Brumwich), "grosely" (RCP MS502), "safely" (Bulkeley)

SILENT OR EXTRA LETTERS
------------------------
- Initial silent letters sometimes appear: "wh" in words where modern
  English has dropped it
- Extra "e" at word endings is very common (see above)
- "gh" is preserved in many words: "through" (Sedley), "draught" (RCP MS502)


================================================================================
PART 5: WORD SPACING, CONNECTIONS, AND LINE LAYOUT
================================================================================

WORD SPACING
------------
Word spacing is inconsistent across all five hands. Key observations:

- Words are sometimes run together, especially short function words like
  "of", "the", "in", "to", "a". In RCP MS502, "whitewyne" is written as
  one word. In Brumwich, "in corproate" is two words where modern English
  has "incorporate" as one.

- In Bulkeley, words are generally more clearly separated than in Brumwich
  or RCP MS502. Bulkeley writes in a more formal, book-like hand.

- Prepositions and articles are often crowded against the following word.
  A space that looks too small to be a word break may actually be one.

Strategy: Do not rely solely on spacing to determine word breaks. Use
knowledge of common words and phrases. Recipe texts follow predictable
patterns (see Part 7).

LETTER CONNECTIONS (LIGATURES)
------------------------------
Within words, letters are often connected by pen strokes:
- "st" is frequently a ligature — the crossbar of "t" connects to the
  top of "s" (visible in Bulkeley, Sedley)
- "ct" — connected in many hands
- "th" — very commonly connected across all manuscripts
- Final "e" is often connected to the preceding letter with a looping
  stroke

LINE AND PAGE LAYOUT
---------------------
General patterns across all five manuscripts:

- Recipe titles are written in a slightly larger or more ornamental hand,
  sometimes centered or offset from the body text.
  Examples: "A water of Exelent uirtue..." (Brumwich — larger, offset),
  "Of Lauander spike, the temperature" (Bulkeley — centered, formal),
  "A Salve for sundry vses" (Sedley — larger hand, left margin)

- Body text fills the full width of the writing area with fairly
  consistent left margins. Right margins are irregular.

- Brumwich writes in two columns (verso and recto pages visible in one
  image), with smaller, more compressed text.

- Bulkeley writes in a single column with wider line spacing — the most
  legible of the five hands.

- Sedley writes in two columns across an opening (left and right pages),
  with moderate spacing.

- Ayscough writes in two columns per opening with generous line spacing
  (every other ruled line appears to be skipped).

- RCP MS502 writes in a single column, fairly compressed, with the
  darkest ink and most angular hand.

LINE SPACING
------------
- Bulkeley: Wide line spacing, text does not crowd. Ascenders and
  descenders do not overlap between lines.
- Brumwich: Tighter spacing, some overlap of ascenders/descenders.
- Sedley: Moderate spacing with clear line separation.
- Ayscough: Wide spacing — appears to skip lines on the ruled page.
- RCP MS502: Moderate to tight spacing.

RUNNING HEADERS AND MARGINALIA
-------------------------------
- Page numbers appear at top corners (Brumwich, Sedley, Ayscough)
- Recipe numbers sometimes appear in margins (Sedley: XXVII, XXVIII, etc.)
- Occasional marginal notes or attributions appear (Brumwich: "commeded by
  Sir James Langham"; Sedley: recipe attributions in titles)


================================================================================
PART 6: CAPITALIZATION AND PUNCTUATION
================================================================================

CAPITALIZATION
--------------
Capitalization is inconsistent by modern standards:

- Recipe titles: First word and key nouns are usually capitalized.
  "A Salve for sundry vses" (Sedley), "A water for sore eyes" (Brumwich)

- Within body text: Nouns are sometimes capitalized (Germanic style),
  but not consistently. Ingredients and substances are frequently
  capitalized: "Lauander" (Bulkeley), "Spanish sope" (Sedley),
  "Saffron" (Ayscough), "Juniper beries" (RCP MS502).

- After periods: Capitalization is inconsistent. Sometimes the next word
  is capitalized, sometimes not.

- Personal names and titles are capitalized: "Sir James Langham" (Brumwich),
  "Mrs Worsley" (Brumwich), "Mr Gilbert" (Ayscough), "Dr. Burlase" (RCP MS502)

PUNCTUATION
-----------
Punctuation is sparse compared to modern practice:

- Periods (.): Used occasionally to mark sentence ends, but many sentences
  simply run on with "&" (and) connecting clauses.

- Commas (,): Used more frequently in Bulkeley (the most formal text) and
  RCP MS502. Less common in Brumwich.

- Colons (:): Occasionally used, especially in Bulkeley.

- Semicolons (;): Rare but present in Bulkeley.

- No quotation marks in any of the manuscripts.

- Ampersand (&): Functions as both "and" and sometimes as a general
  connector between clauses. It is by far the most common punctuation-like
  mark in these texts.

- The general pattern: Recipes tend to be written as long, flowing
  sentences connected by "&" with minimal internal punctuation. A recipe
  might be a single sentence from "Take..." to the final instruction.
  Example: The entire first Brumwich recipe on eye water is essentially
  one sentence.


================================================================================
PART 7: RECIPE-SPECIFIC PATTERNS AND VOCABULARY
================================================================================

Since all five manuscripts are recipe/medical books, there are strong
patterns in vocabulary and structure that aid transcription:

RECIPE STRUCTURE
----------------
Almost every recipe follows this template:
1. TITLE: "A [remedy type] for [condition]" or "[Remedy] for [condition]"
   Sometimes with attribution: "commended by [person]"
2. BODY begins with "Take" — this is nearly universal. In the 5 pages
   studied, the vast majority of recipes begin with "Take".
3. INGREDIENTS listed with quantities (ounces, drams, handfuls, etc.)
4. PREPARATION instructions (boil, distill, strain, mix, stamp, etc.)
5. APPLICATION instructions (lay to, drink, wash, apply, etc.)
6. Sometimes: duration, frequency, or additional notes.

COMMON RECIPE VERBS
--------------------
These verbs appear repeatedly and should be expected:
- Take, mix, boil/boyle, distill/still, strain/straine, stamp (= crush),
  steep, infuse, dissolve/disolved, beat/beaten, wash, lay, put, drink/drinke,
  keep, apply/applyed, pour/poure, wear, fry, bruse (= bruise/crush)

COMMON INGREDIENTS AND SUBSTANCES
----------------------------------
Recognizing these recurring terms aids transcription enormously:
- Waters: rose water, fennel water, cowslip water, cinnamon water
- Herbs: celandine (sallendine/sallandine), chamomile (camomill),
  lavender, fennel (fennill/fenell), wormwood, angelica, balm,
  eyebright, betony, tansy (tansey), scabious
- Spices: cloves, nutmeg, saffron, cinnamon, galangal (gallingall/galengall),
  mace, cumin (cummin)
- Animal products: eggs (yolk of an egg), harts horn, crabs eyes,
  crabs claws, ambergris (amber grease), boys urine, frog spawn
- Minerals/chemicals: white lead, tutty (zinc oxide), aloes, salt,
  sugar candy, Venice turpentine
- Preparations: syrup, electuary, plaster/plaister, clister (= enema),
  decoction, posset (possit)
- Measurements: ounce, dram/dramme, scruple, handful, spoonful, pint,
  pound, penny-worth, grain
- Containers: glass/glasse, mortar, earthen pot, vial (vioyll)
- Materials: cloth/cloath, feather, paper, lawn (fine linen)
- Wine types: white wine, claret (clarett), canary, malmsey (mamsey),
  posset drink

COMMON PHRASES
--------------
These formulaic phrases recur constantly:
- "Take [quantity] of [ingredient]..."
- "of each a [quantity]"
- "mix all these together"
- "put it into..."
- "strain it through..."
- "boil it till..."
- "let it stand [duration]"
- "keep it for your use"
- "for your use"
- "use this [frequency]"
- "[quantity] of the best"
- "beaten fine" / "beaten to powder"
- "finely powdered"
- "bruse the seeds"

PART 8: SCRIBE-SPECIFIC CHARACTERISTICS
================================================================================

Each of the five scribes has distinctive features. Knowing which scribe you
are reading helps calibrate expectations:

BRUMWICH (MS160)
- Hand: Small, somewhat cramped secretary-influenced hand
- Distinctive features:
  * Uses "u" where convention would suggest "v": "uirtue", "uery"
  * Very long, flowing recipes with minimal punctuation
  * Writing is small and dense — two pages visible per opening
  * Secretary "d" used frequently
  * Spelling is less consistent than other scribes
- Difficulty level: MODERATE-HIGH (small writing, dense layout)

BULKELEY (MS169)
- Hand: Clear, well-spaced italic-influenced hand
- Distinctive features:
  * Most legible of the five hands
  * Formal layout with clear line spacing
  * Consistent use of v/u convention ("vnto", "vsed", "haue")
  * "-inge" endings very consistent
  * Careful punctuation including commas and semicolons
  * Text is from an herbal/medical treatise, more literary than recipes
- Difficulty level: LOW-MODERATE (clearest hand)

SEDLEY (MS534)
- Hand: Mixed secretary/italic hand, medium size
- Distinctive features:
  * Uses "ff" for capital "F": "ffor", "ffellons"
  * Uses "=" for line-end word breaks
  * Roman numeral recipe numbers (XXVII, XXVIII, etc.)
  * Caret insertion marks for corrections (^)
  * Uses "t'is" as contraction for "it is"
  * Relatively clear hand with good spacing
  * "&c" used for "et cetera"
- Difficulty level: MODERATE (clear but with some archaic forms)

AYSCOUGH (MS1026)
- Hand: Fairly clear italic hand with wide spacing
- Distinctive features:
  * Generous line spacing (appears to skip ruled lines)
  * Uses apothecary symbols (℥, ʒ) frequently
  * Uses fractions (1/2) written out
  * Superscript abbreviations ("Mr")
  * Two-page openings with different recipes per page
  * Attributions to named individuals ("Mr Gilbert", "Mr Shaw")
- Difficulty level: LOW-MODERATE (clear hand, good spacing)

RCP MS502
- Hand: Angular, somewhat compressed hand with dark ink
- Distinctive features:
  * Heavy use of apothecary symbols and Roman numerals
  * Abbreviations like "wch" (which), "wth" (with)
  * Uses "i" in place of "1" in Roman numerals within measurements
  * Dense text with moderate spacing
  * "ss" for "semis" (half) in measurements
  * Darker, heavier pen strokes than other scribes
  * Uses "itt" for "it" sometimes
- Difficulty level: MODERATE-HIGH (abbreviations, dense layout)

PART 9: TRANSCRIPTION STRATEGY — STEP BY STEP
================================================================================

Based on studying these five manuscripts, here is a recommended strategy for
transcribing a new, unseen page in a similar hand:

STEP 1: ORIENT YOURSELF
- Look at the overall page layout. Is it one column or two? How dense is
  the text? Are there recipe titles standing out?
- Identify the page number if visible (usually top corner).
- Note whether this appears to be a recipe book, herbal, or medical text.

STEP 2: START WITH WHAT YOU CAN READ
- Recipe titles are often the most legible text on the page because they
  are written larger or more carefully.
- The word "Take" at the start of recipe bodies is nearly universal and
  easy to recognize — use it as an anchor.
- Ingredient names and measurements often stand out.

STEP 3: BUILD AN ALPHABET FROM THE PAGE
- Once you identify a few certain words, use them to learn the scribe's
  specific letterforms. If you can read "Take", you now know this scribe's
  T, a, k, and e. If you can read "water", you know w, a, t, e, r.
- Build up your alphabet letter by letter from confirmed words.
- Pay special attention to: how this scribe writes long s vs. f, how they
  write d (secretary or italic), and how they handle minims.

STEP 4: USE RECIPE PATTERNS
- Expect "Take [quantity] of [ingredient]" patterns.
- Common measurements: ounce(s), dram(s), handful(s), spoonful(s), pint(s).
- Common instructions: boil, strain, distill, mix, put, keep, use, apply.
- If a word is unclear, ask: "What ingredient, measurement, or instruction
  would make sense here?"

STEP 5: HANDLE DIFFICULT PASSAGES
- Count minims carefully when dealing with m/n/u/i combinations.
- Check for long s vs. f (look for crossbar).
- Remember u/v interchange (positional).
- Remember "ff" can mean capital "F".
- Look for abbreviation marks (superscript letters, tildes, etc.).
- Apothecary symbols: ℥ = ounce, ʒ = dram, ℈ = scruple.
- "wch" = which, "wth" = with, "yt" = that, "ye" = the.

STEP 6: PRESERVE ORIGINAL SPELLING
- Do NOT modernize spelling. Transcribe exactly what the scribe wrote.
- Keep "u" for "v" and vice versa as written: "vnto", "haue", "uirtue".
- Keep "-inge" endings, double letters, etc.
- Keep "ff" for capital F: "ffor", "ffellons".
- Keep ampersands as "&".
- Expand only the most standard abbreviations (like apothecary symbols)
  and mark expansions if your transcription convention requires it.

STEP 7: VERIFY
- Read your transcription back. Does every word make sense in context?
- For recipe texts, does the recipe make practical sense? (Ingredients,
  then preparation, then application.)
- Are there any impossible letter combinations that suggest a misreading?
- Check especially: Did you confuse long s/f? Did you get the minims right?
  Did you mistake secretary d for "cl"?

WRONG READING    →  CORRECT READING    EXPLANATION
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
"ficknes"        →  "sicknes"          Long s misread as f
"difeases"       →  "diseases"         Secretary d misread as "di"
"clistilled"     →  "distilled"        Secretary d misread as "cl"
"haue"           →  (correct as-is)    Not "have" — preserve original
"vnto"           →  (correct as-is)    Not "unto" — preserve original
"ffor"           →  (correct as-is)    Capital F written as ff
"iiij"           →  (correct as-is)    Roman numeral 4 (not letters)
"wch"            →  (correct as-is)    Abbreviation for "which"
"t'is"           →  (correct as-is)    Contraction for "it is"
"oyle"           →  (correct as-is)    Period spelling of "oil"
"plaister"       →  (correct as-is)    Period spelling of "plaster"
"bruse"          →  (correct as-is)    Period spelling of "bruise"
"sirrup"/"syrrup"→  (correct as-is)    Period spelling of "syrup"
"℥ij"            →  (correct as-is)    2 ounces in apothecary notation
"℥i-ss"          →  (correct as-is)    1.5 ounces in apothecary notation
