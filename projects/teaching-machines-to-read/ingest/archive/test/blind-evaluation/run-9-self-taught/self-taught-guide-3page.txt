SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from study of three early modern English manuscript pages
Training materials: Brumwich MS160, Bulkeley MS169, Sedley MS534
=================================================================

This guide was created by studying three manuscript images alongside their
correct transcriptions, comparing letterforms, spellings, abbreviations, and
layout conventions. It is designed to enable transcription of similar
early modern English recipe/medical manuscripts (roughly 1600-1700).

=================================================================
PART 1: THE ALPHABET — LETTERFORMS IN EARLY MODERN HANDS
=================================================================

These manuscripts are written in English secretary hand transitioning toward
italic hand, typical of the 17th century. Some letters are dramatically
different from modern forms.

LOWERCASE LETTERS
-----------------

a — Usually looks like a modern lowercase "a" but can be more angular,
    sometimes resembling a small "u" with a connecting stroke. In Brumwich,
    the "a" in "water" and "Take" is fairly round. In Bulkeley, it is
    more carefully formed and closer to modern italic.

b — Has a tall ascender, generally recognizable. The bowl may be small.
    Example: "blood" in Bulkeley, "belly" in Sedley.

c — Small, open curve, similar to modern "c" but sometimes nearly
    indistinguishable from "e" when written quickly. In Brumwich,
    "Cleane" shows a clear "c". Compare carefully with "e" in context.

d — CRITICAL LETTER. Appears in TWO forms:
    1. Secretary "d" — looks like a backwards-leaning loop, almost like
       a modern cursive Greek delta. The ascender curves LEFT and back.
       This is the most common form in Brumwich and appears in words like
       "dimme," "decayed," "drames."
    2. Italic "d" — more like modern "d" with a straight or right-leaning
       ascender. Sedley uses this form more often.
    Both forms can appear in the same manuscript. In Bulkeley, "distilled"
    begins with an italic-style "d."

e — Small, often looks like a tiny backwards "3" or a simple curved stroke.
    Can be confused with "c" or "i" in rapid hands. In Brumwich, the "e"
    at the end of "take" is a small upward flick. In Bulkeley, "e" is
    more carefully formed.

f — Has a descender that drops below the baseline (unlike modern "f").
    The crossbar may extend to the left. Looks somewhat like a modern "f"
    but taller and with the descender. Example: "fine" in Brumwich,
    "flowers" in Bulkeley. IMPORTANT: at the start of words, "ff" (double f)
    is used where we would write a capital "F." See Sedley: "ffellons,"
    "ffor A Tetter." This is a standard convention, not a spelling error.

g — Has a distinctive descending loop. The secretary form can look like
    a figure "8" or a "y" with a closed loop. In Bulkeley, "giuen" shows
    a clear secretary "g." In Brumwich, "glase" has a recognizable "g."

h — Recognizable ascender with a curved stroke at the baseline. Can
    sometimes look like "li" when written hastily. Example: "helpe" in
    Brumwich, "haue" in Bulkeley, "heales" in Sedley.

i — A simple minim (single downstroke) usually without a dot in these
    hands, or with a very faint dot. Can be confused with the first
    stroke of "m," "n," "u," or "w." Context is essential.
    Example: "it" in all three manuscripts.

j — Rarely appears as a distinct letter. The letter "i" is used where
    modern English would use "j" in many cases. When "j" does appear,
    it looks like an "i" with a descender.

k — Has an ascender and two diagonal strokes at the baseline, somewhat
    like modern "k" but the lower strokes may be more angular.
    Example: "Take" in all three manuscripts (though "T" is capital here,
    the "k" is visible). Also "back" in Sedley.

l — A simple ascender stroke, taller than "i" but otherwise similar.
    Can be confused with "i" in cramped writing. "ll" combinations
    (as in "all," "still") help identify the height difference.

m — Three minims (downstrokes) connected at the top. CRITICAL: "m"
    looks like "iii" in many hands. You must count minims and use
    context. In "them" (Bulkeley), the three connected downstrokes
    are visible. In some hands "m" at the end of a word has a
    finishing stroke that curves right.

n — Two minims connected at the top. Same challenge as "m" — looks
    like "ii." Must be read in context. "fine" vs "fme" — context
    tells you it is "n" not "m."

o — Generally round and recognizable, similar to modern "o."
    Example: "of" in all three manuscripts.

p — Has a descender. Generally recognizable. The bowl sits at the
    baseline. Example: "powder" in Brumwich, "partes" in Bulkeley,
    "plaister" in Sedley.

q — Rare. Has a descender going right. Example: "quantety" in Brumwich,
    "qualitie" in Bulkeley, "quarter" in Sedley.

r — CRITICAL LETTER with multiple forms:
    1. Long "r" — looks like a "2" or a "z" shape. This appears after
       rounded letters (o, b, p, d) especially. In Brumwich, "corproate"
       and "drames" show this form.
    2. Short "r" — more like a modern "r" with a short shoulder. Used
       at the start of words or after straight-stroke letters.
    Both forms appear throughout all three manuscripts. The "2-shaped r"
    is one of the most confusing features for modern readers.

s — CRITICAL LETTER with TWO major forms:
    1. Long "s" — looks like an "f" WITHOUT a crossbar (or with only a
       half-crossbar extending left). Used at the beginning and middle
       of words. Example: in Bulkeley, "substance," "diseases," and
       "sicknes" all begin with long-s. In Brumwich, "sight," "sune,"
       and "steepe" use long-s initially.
    2. Short/round "s" — looks like modern "s." Used at the END of words.
       Example: "eyes" in Brumwich ends with round-s. "thinges" in
       Bulkeley ends with round-s.
    CONFUSION: Long "s" vs "f" — the KEY difference is the crossbar.
    "f" has a full crossbar; long-s has none or only a half-stroke to
    the left. This distinction is subtle and critical.

t — Has a crossbar but the vertical stroke does not usually ascend as
    high as "l" or "d." Can look like "c" when the crossbar is faint.
    Example: "the" in all manuscripts, "Take" in recipe headings.

u/v — In this period, "u" and "v" are variant forms of the SAME letter.
    Convention: "v" is used at the START of words, "u" in the MIDDLE.
    This is the OPPOSITE of modern usage in many cases.
    Examples from Brumwich: "uirtue" = "virtue," "uery" = "very,"
    "euening" = "evening." From Bulkeley: "vnto" = "unto,"
    "vsed" = "used," "haue" = "have." From Sedley: "vses" = "uses."
    THIS IS ONE OF THE MOST IMPORTANT CONVENTIONS TO KNOW.

w — Looks like two "v"s joined together, often with a distinctive
    shape. Generally recognizable but can be confused with "vv."
    Example: "water" in Brumwich, "Wine" in Bulkeley, "wood" in Sedley.

x — Rare. Looks like modern "x." Example: "Exelent" in Brumwich.

y — Has a descender, similar to modern "y" but the tail may curve
    differently. Secretary "y" can look somewhat like "p" or "g" in
    hasty writing. Example: "you" in Brumwich, "by" in Bulkeley,
    "yeares" in Sedley.

z — Very rare. When it appears, resembles a "3" or an angular "z."

UPPERCASE / CAPITAL LETTERS
---------------------------

A — Often a larger, more elaborate version of lowercase "a" with
    an initial approach stroke. In Brumwich, "A water..." headings
    show a clear capital A.

B — Recognizable with large loops. "But" in Bulkeley shows a clear B.

C — Large curved stroke. "Cleane" in Brumwich, "Catalepsie" in
    Bulkeley, "Childbed" in Sedley.

D — Can be an enlarged secretary "d" (with leftward loop) or an
    italic form.

E — Can look like a modern "E" or may have extra curves.
    "Exelent" in Brumwich, "Eye" in Brumwich.

F — See note about "ff" convention above. When a true capital F
    appears, it has prominent crossbars. But "ff" (double lowercase f)
    is the more common convention for capital F.
    Sedley: "ffor A Tetter" and "ffellons."

G — Large, elaborate form with a descending loop. "Good" in Sedley.

H — Recognizable tall letter. "Henslow" would show this clearly.

I — Often just a taller "i" stroke, sometimes with serifs.

L — Large version with a loop at top. "Lauander" in Bulkeley,
    "Leaden" in Sedley.

M — Large version with clear three-stroke pattern. "Medicine" in
    Brumwich, "Migram" in Bulkeley, "Mortar" in Sedley.

N — Similar to enlarged lowercase but more angular.

O — Large round letter. "Of" in Bulkeley headings.

P — Large with prominent descender. "Pills" in Sedley.

R — Can have an elaborate form with flourishes. "Runinge" in Sedley.

S — Capital S is usually a round/modern form (never the long-s form).
    "Salve" in Sedley, "Spanish" in Sedley.

T — "Take" appears frequently in recipes. The capital T in all three
    manuscripts is generally recognizable with a prominent crossbar.
    In Brumwich, the T in "Take" has a curved horizontal stroke.

V — See notes on u/v above. Capital V may appear at the start of
    words we would spell with U: "Vertues" in Bulkeley heading.

W — Elaborate, wide letter. "Wine" in Bulkeley.

X — "XXVII," "XXVIII," "XXIX" in Sedley show Roman numerals using X.

PART 2: EASILY CONFUSED LETTER PAIRS
=================================================================

These are the letter pairs most likely to cause transcription errors:

1. Long-s vs f
   Long-s has NO crossbar (or half-crossbar left only).
   f has a FULL crossbar crossing the stem.
   Example: In Bulkeley, "safely" — the first letter is long-s,
   not f. In Brumwich, "fine" starts with f (has crossbar),
   while "sight" starts with long-s (no crossbar).

2. u vs n vs m vs i (the minim problem)
   All are composed of similar downstrokes (minims).
   u = two minims joined at bottom
   n = two minims joined at top
   m = three minims joined at top
   i = single minim
   In a word like "minim" itself, you would see a sequence of
   downstrokes that only context can parse. Use word context
   and spelling knowledge to resolve.

3. c vs e vs t
   In rapid hands, these can all look similar — small curved
   or crossed strokes at x-height. "t" should have a visible
   crossbar; "c" is open to the right; "e" has an internal stroke.

4. Secretary d vs a or o
   The leftward-looping secretary "d" can resemble "a" or "cl."
   If you see what looks like "cl" but the word doesn't make sense,
   try reading it as "d."

5. y vs p vs g (descenders)
   All have descenders. "y" descends and curves right; "p" descends
   straight; "g" has a closed loop. But in quick writing these blur.

6. r (2-shaped) vs terminal s or z
   The "2-shaped r" can be mistaken for other letters. It appears
   after round letters. When you see a "2"-like shape after o, b,
   p, or d, it is likely "r."

7. w vs double-v (vv)
   "w" may look like "uu" or "vv." Context determines.

PART 3: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
=================================================================

Early modern English spelling was not standardized. Expect:

U/V INTERCHANGE (most important rule):
- "v" used at the start of words where modern English uses "u":
  "vnto" = unto, "vsed" = used, "vpon" = upon
- "u" used in the middle of words where modern English uses "v":
  "uirtue" = virtue, "uery" = very, "haue" = have,
  "euening" = evening, "ouer" = over, "giuen" = given
  Examples from all three manuscripts confirm this universally.

I/J INTERCHANGE:
- "i" often used where modern English uses "j":
  "iuice" = juice (if it appears), "iust" = just

DOUBLE LETTERS:
- "ff" for capital F: "ffellons" = Fellons, "ffor" = For (Sedley)
- Double letters may appear where modern English uses single:
  "doung" = dung, "soe" = so

-IE/-Y INTERCHANGE:
- Words may end in "-ie" where modern English uses "-y":
  "qualitie" = quality, "espetially" = especially (Bulkeley)
  "quantety" = quantity (Brumwich)

-TION ENDINGS:
- May be spelled "-tion" or "-sion" or with abbreviations

TERMINAL -E:
- Many words have a final -e that modern English drops:
  "helpe" = help, "sune" = sun, "Cleane" = clean,
  "steepe" = steep, "sicknes" = sickness (Brumwich)
  "giue" = give, "kinde" = kind (Bulkeley)

VOWEL VARIATIONS:
- "ee" where modern uses single "e": "steepe," "steeped"
- "ey" for modern "y": possibly in some words
- "oa" variations: "coales" = coals (Sedley)

CONSONANT VARIATIONS:
- "ck" for "k": "sicknes" (Brumwich), "Collicke" (Sedley)
- "ll" where modern uses "l": "halfe" = half, "smalle" = small
- "th" for "t" or vice versa: "therwith" = therewith (Bulkeley)
- "ch" for various sounds

OTHER SPELLING PATTERNS:
- "Lauander" = Lavender (u/v rule + vowel shift) (Bulkeley)
- "espetially" = especially (Bulkeley) — note "t" for "c"
- "cheifely" = chiefly (Bulkeley)
- "fenill" = fennel (Brumwich)
- "sallendine/Sallandine" = celandine (Brumwich and Sedley)
- "yeallow" = yellow (Brumwich)
- "Cristane" = Christian? (Brumwich)

WORD BOUNDARY VARIATIONS:
- Some words run together: "therwith" = therewith (Bulkeley)
- Some compound words split: "eye bright" = eyebright (Brumwich)
- "a bundance" = abundance (Bulkeley) — split into two words
- "in corproate" = incorporate (Brumwich) — split into two words

=================================================================
PART 4: ABBREVIATIONS AND SYMBOLS
=================================================================

AMPERSAND (&):
- Used very frequently for "and." Appears in all three manuscripts.
  In Brumwich: "weake or dimme" vs "&" for connecting items.
  In Bulkeley: extensive use of "&" throughout.
  In Sedley: "&" and "&c" (= etc.) at the end of the Tetter recipe.

SUPERSCRIPT LETTERS:
- Abbreviations using raised/superscript letters are common.
  The most typical are:
  - "y^e" = "the" (the superscript "e" over "y" which represents thorn)
  - "y^t" = "that"
  - "w^th" = "with"
  - "w^ch" = "which"
  These may not appear in all three training manuscripts but are
  standard in the period.

LINE-END WORD BREAKS:
- Words broken across lines use "=" or "-" as a hyphen.
  Bulkeley: "dif-" / "ference" (line break with hyphen)
  Sedley: "incor=" / "porated" and "soverai=" / "gne"
  Note: Sedley uses "=" while Bulkeley uses "-" for the same purpose.

CARET INSERTIONS:
- Sedley: "h^eales" — a caret mark (^) indicates an insertion
  above the line. The "e" was added above between "h" and "ales."
  This means the scribe corrected themselves.

TILDE/MACRON OVER LETTERS:
- A tilde or line over a vowel can indicate a missing "m" or "n."
  For example, a line over "o" might mean "on" or "om."

MARGINALIA AND NUMBERS:
- Page numbers appear at top of pages: {6}, {7} in Brumwich;
  "7" in Bulkeley; "20," "21" in Sedley.
- Roman numerals used for recipe numbering in Sedley: XXVII, XXVIII, XXIX.

=================================================================
PART 5: PAGE LAYOUT AND STRUCTURE
=================================================================

RECIPE FORMAT (all three manuscripts):
- Recipes follow a consistent structure:
  1. Title/heading (often underlined or in larger script)
  2. Body text beginning with "Take..." (very common opening)
  3. Ingredients listed with quantities
  4. Instructions for preparation
  5. Instructions for application/use

HEADINGS:
- Often centered or written more carefully than body text
- May use different letter sizes or styles
- Brumwich: "A water of Exelent uirtue..." as heading, then body
- Bulkeley: "Of Lauander spike, the temperature." and "The Vertues."
  as headings, with periods at end
- Sedley: "A Salve for sundry vses called / the Leaden plaister"
  followed by Roman numeral "XXVII"

LINE SPACING:
- Text is written continuously with relatively consistent line spacing
- New recipes start after a gap or blank line
- Headings may have space above and below

MARGINS:
- Left margins are generally respected
- Right margins are less consistent — words may be broken or squeezed

TWO-PAGE SPREADS:
- Brumwich MS160 shows a two-page spread (pages 6-7 side by side)
- Sedley MS534 also shows a two-page spread (pages 20-21)
- Bulkeley MS169 shows a single page (page 7)
- When transcribing two-page spreads, read left page first, then right

ATTRIBUTION:
- Recipes may be attributed to specific people:
  Brumwich: "commeded by Sir James Langham" and "commended by Mrs Worsley"
- These attributions appear at the end of headings

=================================================================
PART 6: DIFFERENCES BETWEEN THE THREE SCRIBES
=================================================================

BRUMWICH (MS160):
- Most informal/hasty of the three hands
- Smaller, more cramped writing
- More spelling irregularities ("Exelent," "uirtue," "sune," "ons")
- Uses u/v convention consistently
- Two-page spread format with many recipes per page
- Recipes separated by headings but closely packed
- The hand on page 7 (right side) may be a different scribe — slightly
  different letterforms, which is consistent with the "(& others)" in
  the manuscript title
- Secretary hand features are most prominent here

BULKELEY (MS169):
- Most formal and careful of the three hands
- Larger, clearer letterforms
- More consistent spacing
- Literary/medical text (herbal description) rather than recipe
- Text flows as continuous prose with clear paragraph breaks
- Headings are distinct: "Of Lauander spike, the temperature."
  and "The Vertues."
- Uses long-s consistently; u/v convention consistent
- Secretary hand with strong italic influences
- Most readable of the three for modern eyes

SEDLEY (MS534):
- Medium formality — neat but practical
- Clear distinction between heading styles and body text
- Uses "ff" for capital F: "ffellons," "ffor"
- Uses "=" for line-break hyphens (Bulkeley uses "-")
- Roman numeral recipe numbering (XXVII, XXVIII, XXIX)
- Two-page spread, reasonably well-spaced
- Shows corrections: "h^eales" with caret insertion
- "t'is" appears for "it is" — contraction with apostrophe
- Mix of secretary and italic features

COMMON TO ALL THREE:
- u/v convention (v initial, u medial) is universal
- Long-s used at beginning/middle of words, round-s at end
- "&" for "and" throughout
- "Take" as common recipe opening
- Final -e on many words where modern English omits it
- Measurements: "drames" (drams), "ounces," "pinte," "handfull"
- Medical/recipe vocabulary: "plaister," "cordiall," "distilled"

PART 7: PRACTICAL TRANSCRIPTION STRATEGY
=================================================================

When transcribing an unseen manuscript page in a similar hand:

1. ORIENT YOURSELF FIRST
   - Look at the whole page before transcribing
   - Identify headings, paragraph breaks, page numbers
   - Note whether it is one page or a two-page spread
   - Identify the general hand style (more secretary or more italic?)

2. START WITH WHAT YOU CAN READ
   - Recipe headings are often clearer than body text
   - Common words like "Take," "of," "the," "and/&" appear frequently
   - Use these anchor words to calibrate your eye to the hand

3. USE CONTEXT FOR DIFFICULT WORDS
   - Recipe manuscripts follow predictable patterns:
     ingredients, quantities, preparation methods, application
   - Common ingredients: rose water, sugar, fennel, celandine, aloes,
     cloves, cinnamon, wine, honey, etc.
   - Common quantities: ounce(s), dram(me)(s), pound, pint(e),
     handful(l), spoonful(l), pennyworth
   - Common verbs: take, put, mix, boil, strain, distil(l), beat,
     stamp, wash, lay, drink, apply
   - Common body parts: eyes, head, stomach, back, belly

4. APPLY THE U/V RULE
   - Every "v" at the start of a word is likely pronounced "u"
   - Every "u" in the middle of a word might be pronounced "v"
   - "haue" = have, "giue" = give, "vnto" = unto, "vsed" = used

5. WATCH FOR LONG-S
   - If you see what looks like "f" but the word doesn't make sense
     with "f," try reading it as long-s
   - "ficknes" doesn't exist; "sicknes" does — that's a long-s
   - Check for the crossbar: full crossbar = f; no/half crossbar = long-s

6. COUNT MINIMS CAREFULLY
   - For sequences of downstrokes (i, m, n, u), consider all possible
     parsings and pick the one that makes a real word
   - "ininim" is not a word; "minim" is — that tells you where the
     m's, n's, i's, and u's fall

7. PRESERVE ORIGINAL SPELLING
   - Do NOT modernize spelling
   - Transcribe "uirtue" not "virtue," "vnto" not "unto"
   - Keep original capitalization patterns
   - Keep "&" as "&" (not "and")
   - Keep "ff" as "ff" (not "F")

8. HANDLE UNCERTAIN READINGS
   - Use [brackets] for uncertain readings
   - Use [...] for illegible passages
   - Note insertions with ^ (as in "h^eales")
   - Preserve line-break hyphens (= or -)

9. COMMON WORDS YOU WILL SEE REPEATEDLY
   (with their likely spellings)
   the, of, and/&, to, in, a, for, it, is, with, that, be, or,
   Take, put, make, one, halfe, water, good, ounce(s), pound,
   pint(e), dram(me), sugar, wine, them, this, all, your, well,
   may, must, let, being, after, then

10. NUMBERS AND MEASUREMENTS
    - Numbers may be written as words or numerals
    - "3" and "2" as numerals are common
    - Roman numerals for recipe numbering
    - "a quarter of a pint," "half a pound," "one ounce"
    - Apothecary measures: drams (drames), scruples, grains

PART 8: SPECIFIC WORD EXAMPLES FROM TRAINING MATERIALS
=================================================================

These examples show how specific words appear across manuscripts,
helping calibrate expectations for similar words in unseen pages:

"Take" — All three MSS. Capital T + "ake." Very common recipe opening.
"water" — Brumwich (frequent). Long-s not present; "w" + "ater."
"ounces" — Brumwich and Sedley. Note the round-s at end.
"powder/puder/powdred" — Various spellings across MSS.
"plaister" — Sedley. Note "ai" where modern English has "a."
"distilled" — Bulkeley. Note long-s after "di"; double "l."
"cordiall" — Sedley. Double "l" at end; "i" for modern "ia."
"together" — All three MSS. Very common connecting word.
"halfe" — Brumwich, Sedley. Final -e on "half."
"pinte" — Brumwich, Sedley. Final -e on "pint."
"handfull" — Brumwich, Sedley. Double "l" variation.
"enough" — Sedley. Standard spelling.
"remedy" — Sedley. Standard spelling.
"disease(s)" — Bulkeley, Sedley. May see long-s in middle.
"stomach" — Sedley. Note "ach" ending.
"soveraigne" — Sedley. Broken across lines as "soverai=/gne."

=================================================================
PART 9: FINAL TIPS
=================================================================

- When in doubt, read aloud. Many "misspellings" are phonetic and
  make sense when spoken: "cheifely" sounds like "chiefly," "espetially"
  sounds like "especially."

- The scribe's hand may change within a single manuscript. Brumwich
  MS160 has contributions from multiple people ("& others"). If the
  hand suddenly looks different, you may be reading a different scribe.

- Ink blots, stains, and bleedthrough from the other side of the page
  are common. Do not try to transcribe marks that are obviously damage
  rather than text.

- Insertions above the line (like Sedley's "h^eales") indicate the
  scribe caught an error. Transcribe the corrected reading with a
  caret or note.

- Common contractions of the period:
  "t'is" = it is (Sedley uses this)
  "&c" = et cetera (Sedley uses this)
  "y^e" = the, "y^t" = that, "w^th" = with (standard period forms)

- Recipe manuscripts are FORMULAIC. Once you can read "Take one
  handfull of [ingredient] & [quantity] of [ingredient]..." the
  pattern will carry you through much of the text.
