SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Based on Henslow MS688 (1601), Early Modern English Secretary Hand
==================================================================

This guide was developed by studying five pages of a single scribe's hand
from a 1601 English recipe book. All observations are grounded in specific
examples from the training materials.

==================================================================
PART 1: GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THE HAND
==================================================================

This is a late Elizabethan secretary hand with some italic influences. The
scribe writes in a fairly consistent, moderately formal hand. The text is
a collection of medical and household recipes. Key general features:

- The hand is reasonably legible but uses many characteristic secretary
  letter forms that differ significantly from modern handwriting.
- Recipe titles are written in a larger, more decorative script, sometimes
  with decorative dots/dashes before and after.
- The word "Take" at the start of recipes is written very large and
  ornately, functioning almost as a paragraph marker.
- Lines are generally well-spaced with clear separation between words,
  though some words can run together.

==================================================================
PART 2: ALPHABET — LOWERCASE LETTER FORMS
==================================================================

a - Two forms appear:
    1. A single-compartment "a" resembling a modern lowercase "a" but
       sometimes open at the top.
    2. In some positions, "a" can look like a "u" — context is essential.
    Examples: "watter" (p.8), "water" (p.10), "Take" (throughout)

b - Has a tall ascender looping to the right, with a round bowl at bottom.
    Can be confused with "l" when the bowl is small.
    Examples: "bee" (p.8), "bloude" (p.8), "barlye" (p.16)

c - Small, open crescent shape, similar to modern "c" but sometimes very
    small and hard to distinguish from "e".
    Examples: "colde" (p.10), "cann" (p.14), "coprose" (p.20)

d - THIS IS A KEY LETTER. Uses the secretary "d" form: the ascender loops
    backward (to the left) rather than forward. This creates a shape that
    can look like a modern "cl" combination. The backward loop is the most
    distinctive feature of secretary hand.
    Examples: "sodde" (p.8), "drinke" (p.10), "diseased" (p.14), "doges" (p.20)

e - Small, often resembling a reversed "3" or a small loop. Can look very
    similar to "o" in some positions. Final "e" is sometimes just a small
    upward flick.
    Examples: "teethe" (p.8), "euelle" (p.8), "eache" (p.10)

f - Tall letter descending below the baseline, with a crossbar. The scribe
    often doubles "ff" at the start of words where modern English uses a
    single "f". The "f" can look like a long "s" but has a crossbar.
    Examples: "ffasten" (p.8), "freshe" (p.8), "fier" (p.20)

g - Has a distinctive descending tail that loops back. The bowl sits on the
    baseline. Can sometimes be confused with "y" at a glance.
    Examples: "greace" (p.8), "gargolle" (p.14), "grease" (p.16)

h - Has a tall ascender and a descending tail that curves right. The tail
    often drops below the baseline, which is a distinctive secretary feature.
    This can make "h" look different from modern expectations.
    Examples: "the" (throughout), "halfe" (p.8), "handefull" (p.10)

i - Simple minim stroke (a single downstroke). Usually NOT dotted in this
    hand, which makes it easy to confuse with parts of m, n, u.
    Examples: "in" (throughout), "itt" (throughout)

k - Relatively rare. Has a tall ascender with two diagonal strokes forming
    the k-shape. Fairly recognizable.
    Examples: "keepe" (p.8), "killed" (p.20)

l - Tall ascender, simple stroke. Can be confused with "b" (which has a
    bowl) or with the ascending part of "h" (which has a descending tail).
    Examples: "liquore" (p.8), "lett" (p.14), "longe" (p.20)

m - Three minims (downstrokes) joined together. Confusion with "ni", "iu",
    "in" is very common since individual minims look alike. COUNT MINIMS
    CAREFULLY. Context is crucial.
    Examples: "moreninge" (p.10), "muche" (p.14), "matter" (p.20)

n - Two minims joined together. Same confusion risks as "m" — the
    difference is just one fewer minim.
    Examples: "nine" (p.8), "narrowe" (p.14), "necke" (p.14)

o - Round, similar to modern "o" but can be quite small and confused with
    "e" or "a" in hasty writing.
    Examples: "ouer" (p.14), "ounce" (p.10), "owte" (p.14)

p - Has a descending tail below the baseline with the bowl above. Fairly
    recognizable and consistent.
    Examples: "putt" (throughout), "potte" (p.14), "powder" (p.20)

q - Rare. Has a descending tail. Seen in "quantitye" (p.14), "quarte" (p.8),
    "Quinsie" (p.14).

r - TWO FORMS are used:
    1. A long "r" that looks like a "2" or a zigzag — used after rounded
       letters (o, b, p, d). This is very common in secretary hand.
    2. A short "r" more like the modern letter, used in other positions.
    Examples: "water" (p.8), "rootes" (p.10), "throte" (p.14)

s - THREE FORMS:
    1. Long "s" (looks like "f" without the crossbar) — used at the start
       and middle of words. This is the most common form.
    2. Short/round "s" (looks like modern "s") — used at the end of words.
    3. Capital "S" — large, decorative, at start of sentences.
    Examples: "seethe" (p.8), "spoonefulls" (p.10), "straine" (p.8)
    CRITICAL: Long "s" vs "f" — look for the crossbar. "f" has one, long "s"
    does not (or has only a half-crossbar on the left side).

t - Has a short ascender with a crossbar. The crossbar sometimes extends
    to connect with the next letter. Can be confused with "c" when small.
    Examples: "teethe" (p.8), "togeather" (p.8), "tente" (p.20)

u/v - THESE ARE INTERCHANGEABLE in this period:
    - "v" is typically used at the start of a word: "vnto", "verie" (p.20),
      "vntill" (p.14)
    - "u" is used in the middle of a word: "putt", "untille" (p.8)
    The scribe is not fully consistent with this rule.
    Examples: "verie" (p.8), "untille" (p.8), "vnnto" vs "unto"

w - Distinctive: looks like two "v"s joined together or a double-u shape.
    Fairly easy to recognize in this hand.
    Examples: "watter" (p.8), "water" (p.10), "withe" (throughout)

x - Rare. Standard cross shape. Seen in "waxeth" (p.14).

y - Has a descending tail that curves left or straight down. The top can
    look like a "v" or a dotted "i" with a tail. Often used where modern
    English uses "i" — especially in "ytt" (= "it") and "yow" (= "you").
    Examples: "ytt" (throughout), "yow" (p.8), "youre" (p.20)

z - Not observed in the training materials.

==================================================================
PART 3: ALPHABET — UPPERCASE/CAPITAL LETTER FORMS
==================================================================

A - Large, decorative. The crossbar may be a separate stroke. Seen in
    "A watter" (p.8), "A plaister" (p.14), "Applye" (p.16), "Archangell" (p.10).

B - Large round letter with decorative loops. "Butt" (p.20), "Boyle" (p.20),
    "Bowlarmene" (p.8).

C - Large crescent shape. "Cinkfoud" (p.14).

D - Large with a backward-looping ascender (like lowercase but bigger).
    "Decayed" (p.8), "Docke" (p.10).

F - Large, tall form descending below baseline. "For" (throughout),
    "Firste" (p.14), "Fistula" (p.20).

G - Ornate, with a descending tail. "Gargisine" (p.16).

H - Large, with the characteristic descending tail of h enlarged.
    "Halle" (p.20).

L - Tall, with a decorative loop at top. "Liver" (p.10), "Longes" (p.10),
    "Laye" (p.20), "Langdebeefe" (p.16).

M - Large version of the three-minim form. "Make" (p.20), "Marye" (p.10).

Q - Very ornate, with a large flourished descending tail. "Quinsie" (p.14),
    "Queene" (p.10).

R - "Redd" (p.20), "Reasons" (p.10).

S - Large, ornate, curving form. "Sunne" (p.10).

T - Large with prominent crossbar. "Take" (throughout — always very large
    and decorative), "This" (p.20), "The" (p.14), "To" (p.10, p.20).

Y - "Yow" (p.20).

==================================================================
PART 4: COMMON ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS
==================================================================

This scribe uses relatively few abbreviations, preferring to spell words
out fully. Observed abbreviations:

1. Superscript letters: Occasionally letters are written above the line
   to indicate abbreviation. Watch for small letters floating above.

2. Truncation with context: Some words may be cut short at line ends.
   Example: "folow[eth]" at end of line (p.14) — the "eth" ending is
   partially lost at the page edge.

3. The word "ounces" appears to be abbreviated in p.16 line 6 as a symbol
   resembling a "z" with a line through it or a special mark.

==================================================================
PART 5: SPELLING CONVENTIONS
==================================================================

Early modern English spelling is NOT standardized. Key patterns in this
scribe's usage:

1. DOUBLE LETTERS where modern English has singles:
   - "tt" for "t": "putt" (put), "itt" (it), "ytt" (it), "att" (at),
     "hott" (hot), "nott" (not), "lett" (let), "butt" (but)
   - "ee" for long "e": "teethe" (teeth), "bee" (be), "keepe" (keep),
     "seethe" (seethe), "breake" (break)
   - "ff" for initial "f": "ffasten" (fasten) — but not always
   - "ll" in many words: "wille" (will), "vntill" (until), "untille"

2. FINAL "E" added to many words:
   - "throte" (throat), "bloude" (blood), "mouthe" (mouth), "clothe",
     "withe" (with — very common), "inne" (in), "owte" (out)
   - "greace" (grease), "warme" (warm), "colde" (cold), "stiffe" (stiff)

3. "Y" used where modern English has "i":
   - "ytt" (it), "yow" (you), "youre" (your), "fyer" (fire),
     "daye" (day), "laye" (lay)
   - "itt" and "ytt" both appear to mean "it" — "ytt" more common

4. "W" spellings:
   - "withe" (with), "withein" (within), "withowt" (without)
   - "owte" (out), "owt" (out), "howe" (how)

5. "-INGE" ending (modern "-ing"):
   - "runninge" (running), "moreninge" (morning), "stoppinge" (stopping),
     "swellinge" (swelling), "eveninge" (evening)

6. "-ETHE/-ETH" ending:
   - "waxeth" (waxes), "achethe" (aches)

7. Other characteristic spellings:
   - "togeather" (together)
   - "annoynte" (anoint)
   - "verie" (very)
   - "muche" (much)
   - "eache" (each)
   - "ofte/often" (often)
   - "soe/so" (so)
   - "doe/dothe" (do/does)
   - "heare" (hair, in p.20)
   - "awaye" (away)
   - "allreadye" (already)

PART 6: WORD-LEVEL PATTERNS AND COMMON WORDS
==================================================================

These words recur frequently in the recipe format:

- "Take" — always at the start of a recipe, written very large
- "and" — extremely common conjunction
- "of" — very common preposition
- "the" — standard article
- "itt" / "ytt" — "it" (both forms used)
- "putt" — "put"
- "withe" — "with"
- "in" / "inne" — "in"
- "to" / "too" / "unto" — "to"
- "for" — "for"
- "or" — "or"
- "a" — article
- "an" — article (sometimes "a" before vowels too)
- "halfe" — "half"
- "then" — "then"
- "them" — "them"
- "that" — "that"
- "this" — "this"
- "as" — "as"
- "so" — "so"
- "bee" — "be"
- "his" — "his" (used where we might say "its")
- "lett" — "let"
- "all" — "all"

Recipe-specific vocabulary:
- "seethe" / "boyle" — to boil
- "straine" — strain
- "annoynte" — anoint

- "spoonefull/spoonefulls" — spoonful(s)

- "moreninge" — morning
- "quantitye" — quantity
- "plaister/plaisteer" — plaster (medical dressing)

==================================================================
PART 7: CONFUSABLE LETTERS — CRITICAL PAIRS
==================================================================

These are the most common sources of misreading:

1. LONG "S" vs "F":
   Both are tall letters descending below the baseline. The KEY difference:
   "f" has a full crossbar extending both sides; long "s" has at most a
   half-crossbar on the left or no crossbar. This is the single most
   important distinction to master.

2. MINIMS: m / n / i / u:
   All are composed of downstrokes (minims). "m" = 3, "n" = 2, "u" = 2
   (but with a connecting curve at bottom), "i" = 1. When adjacent, count
   carefully. Example: "runninge" has many minims in sequence.

3. SECRETARY "D" vs "CL":
   The backward-looping "d" can look like "cl" to modern eyes. If you
   think you see "cl" in a word where it doesn't make sense, try reading
   it as "d". Example: "and" might look like "ancl".

4. "C" vs "T" vs "E":
   These small letters can resemble each other, especially when written
   quickly. "c" is an open curve, "t" has a crossbar, "e" is a small loop.

5. "Y" vs "I" vs "J":
   "y" descends below the line; "i" does not (but may lack a dot); "j"
   is rare in this period (usually "i" is used instead).

6. "V" vs "U":
   In initial position, expect "v" (even for the vowel sound). In medial
   position, expect "u". This is a convention, not a different letter.

7. "W" vs "VV":
   "w" looks like a double-v, but is written as a single connected letter.

8. "TH" combination:
   Very common. The "t" and "h" often ligate (connect). Learn to recognize
   "th" as a unit — it appears in "the", "that", "them", "then", "this",
   "throte", "withe", "togeather", etc.

==================================================================
PART 8: PAGE LAYOUT AND FORMATTING
==================================================================

1. RECIPE TITLES: Written in a slightly larger hand, often centered or
   near-center. May be preceded/followed by decorative dots, dashes, or
   flourishes (like "~~~~" or "....").

2. "TAKE" AS OPENER: The word "Take" beginning each recipe is written
   very large and ornately — essentially a decorated initial. It stands
   out visually from the rest of the text.

3. LINE BREAKS: The scribe writes continuously, breaking lines wherever
   space runs out. Line breaks do NOT necessarily correspond to sentence
   or clause boundaries. Words are sometimes split across lines.

4. PUNCTUATION: Very sparse. Colons ":" and periods "." appear occasionally
   but the scribe does not use commas systematically. Recipe titles may
   have periods or colons after them.

5. SPACING: Generally clear word spacing, but some words can run together,
   especially short function words like "to", "in", "of", "and".

6. PARAGRAPH BREAKS: Blank lines separate recipes. Each new recipe gets
   its own title.

==================================================================
PART 9: STRATEGY FOR TRANSCRIBING AN UNSEEN PAGE
==================================================================

1. FIRST PASS — Survey the page:
   - Identify recipe titles (larger text, possibly centered)
   - Identify the large "Take" openers
   - Get a sense of overall layout and number of recipes

2. SECOND PASS — Read line by line:
   - Start with words you can confidently read
   - Use context to fill in harder words (recipe vocabulary is repetitive)
   - Pay special attention to the confusable pairs listed above
   - Count minims carefully for m/n/i/u combinations

3. THIRD PASS — Check and verify:
   - Re-read uncertain words in context of the full sentence
   - Check that recipe instructions make logical sense (ingredients,
     quantities, preparation steps)
   - Mark genuinely unclear readings with [word?] or [...]

4. COMMON RECIPE STRUCTURE:
   - Title describing the ailment or purpose
   - "Take" followed by ingredients with quantities
   - Preparation instructions (boyle, seethe, straine, beate, etc.)
   - Application instructions (drinke, annoynte, laye, washe, etc.)
   - Sometimes ends with "probatum" (proved) or dosage instructions

5. EXPECT THESE FEATURES:
   - Unstandardized spelling throughout
   - "withe" for "with", "ytt/itt" for "it", "yow" for "you"
   - Double consonants and extra final "e"s
   - Latin or French-derived medical/botanical terms
   - Quantities: "handefull", "spoonefull", "ounce", "quarte", "pinte"
   - Time references: "moreninge", "eveninge", "daye", "houre"

PART 10: ADDITIONAL OBSERVATIONS FROM SPECIFIC PAGES
==================================================================

Page 8:
- "Bowlarmene" and "dreggans bloude" are specialized apothecary terms
- "gommasticke" = gum mastic (a resin)
- "probatum" appears as a recipe endorsement
- "tauesaker" — an unusual term, possibly a plant name

Page 10:
- "polippes" = polypody (a fern)
- "Reasons of the Sunne" = raisins of the sun (sun-dried raisins)
- "sugare candye" = sugar candy
- "clary" = clary sage (an herb)
- "perwinkell" = periwinkle (a plant)
- "Archangell" = archangel (a plant, dead-nettle)
- "smithes water" = water from a blacksmith's forge (used to quench iron)

Page 14:
- "Cinkfoud" = cinquefoil (a plant)
- "liuer vaine" = liver vein (a blood vessel in the arm)
- "methridatum" = mithridate (an antidote compound)
- "swallowes neste" = swallow's nest (literal ingredient)
- "mallowes" = mallows (plant)

Page 16:
- "Langdebeefe" = lang-de-boeuf / ox-tongue (a plant, bugloss)
- "coorrance" = currants
- "pulltis" = poultice
- "barrowes grease" = barrow's grease (pig fat)

Page 20:
- "coprose" = copperas (iron sulfate)
- "valerion" = valerian (a plant)
- "floppe" = possibly hyssop or another herb
- "malmesye" = malmsey (a sweet wine)
- "levened breade" = leavened bread
- "Halle" = a person's name (a practitioner)

These botanical and medical terms recur across early modern recipe
manuscripts, so expect similar vocabulary in other manuscripts of
this type.
