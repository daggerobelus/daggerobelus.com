SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Based on study of Henslow MS688 (1601), five pages of an early modern English recipe book
==========================================================================================

This guide is derived from careful comparison of five manuscript images with their
verified transcriptions. It covers letter forms, confusable characters, spelling
conventions, abbreviations, formatting, and other features needed to transcribe
similar early modern secretary/italic hybrid hands.

=== 1. GENERAL SCRIPT CHARACTERISTICS ===

The hand is a fairly clear mixed secretary/italic hand from c. 1601. It is generally
legible with consistent letter forms. The scribe writes in a flowing style with
moderate use of ligatures. Ink varies in darkness across pages. The writing is
predominantly in a single hand throughout.

=== 2. LOWERCASE LETTER FORMS ===

a - Has a round, open form. Sometimes resembles a modern 'a' with an open top.
    Can look like 'u' when written quickly. Examples: "and", "take", "water"

b - Tall ascender with a rounded bowl at bottom. Generally clear.
    Examples: "bee", "bloude", "boyle"

c - Small, rounded, open to the right. Can resemble 'e' or 'r' in some contexts.
    Examples: "colde", "come", "candye"

d - Two forms appear:
    (1) Secretary 'd' with a looped ascender curving back to the left — this is the
        dominant form. The loop curves leftward over the bowl.
    (2) Occasionally a more italic 'd' with a straight or slightly curved ascender.
    Examples: "doges", "dothe", "drie"

e - Small, rounded form. Sometimes has a slight hook at top. When final, can trail off.
    The scribe often doubles 'e' in words like "teethe", "seethe", "bee".
    Examples: "euelle", "enter", "eache"

f - Has a descender below the baseline and often a crossbar through the middle.
    Initial 'ff' (double f) is used for capital F — see section on capitals.
    The descender distinguishes it from long-s in many cases.
    Examples: "fine", "fier", "for"

g - Has a characteristic descending tail that loops back. The bowl sits on the line.
    Examples: "gargole", "goode", "greace"

h - Tall ascender, generally clear. The bottom curves or kicks to the right.
    Examples: "heade", "heale", "handefull"

i - A simple minim (short vertical stroke) with a dot above, though the dot is
    sometimes faint or absent. Easily confused with the first stroke of 'n', 'm', 'u'.
    Examples: "in", "itt", "is"

k - Has a tall ascender with two strokes forming the lower portion.
    Examples: "keepe", "killed", "kinges"

l - Tall ascender, simple vertical stroke. Can look like 'h' if a slight curve appears
    at bottom. Distinguished from 'h' by lacking the pronounced rightward kick.
    Examples: "lett", "longes", "like"

Long-s (ſ) - This is the secretary/long form of 's'. It looks like an 'f' but WITHOUT
    a full crossbar extending to the right. It appears:
    - At the beginning of words: "seethe" "straine" "spoonefulls"
    - In the middle of words: "diseased" "togeather"
    It does NOT appear at the end of words — final 's' uses the round form.
    CRITICAL: Long-s vs f is a key distinction. Long-s may have a slight leftward
    tick but f has a clear crossbar extending both directions.

m - Three minims (short strokes). Easily confused with sequences of 'in', 'ni', 'un'.
    Context is essential. Examples: "make", "muche", "mouthe"

n - Two minims. Context needed to distinguish from parts of 'm' or 'u'+minim combos.
    Examples: "narrowe", "nine", "neste"

o - Round, generally clear. Examples: "one", "ounce", "oyle"

p - Descender below baseline with a round bowl. Generally clear.
    Examples: "putt", "powder", "pinte"

q - Descender below baseline, small bowl. Always followed by 'u'.
    Examples: "quarte", "quantitye", "Quinsie"

r - Two forms:
    (1) A short stroke with a shoulder — the common modern form
    (2) A 'z'-shaped or '2'-shaped form (secretary r), especially after 'o'
    Examples: "runninge", "rootes", "rose"

Round-s - Used at the end of words. Modern 's' shape.
    Examples: "eyes", "longes", "rootes"

t - Short ascender with a crossbar. The crossbar may connect to the next letter.
    Can resemble 'c' if the ascender is short. Examples: "the", "teethe", "them"

u/v - The scribe follows the period convention:
    'v' is used at the beginning of words for the /u/ or /v/ sound: "verie", "untill"
    'u' is used medially: "putt", "runninge", "muche"
    Note: 'v' initial for vowel sound is normal here — "vntill" = "until"

w - A distinctive letter formed by two 'v' shapes joined. Quite wide.
    Examples: "water", "withe", "waxeth"

x - Rare. Appears in "waxeth" (page 14). Formed as a cross.

y - Has a descender. The tail curves below and to the left. Used frequently in
    word-final position: "ytt", "yow". The descender distinguishes it from a
    thorn (though thorn is not used in this hand — "the" is spelled out).
    Examples: "yow", "ytt", "youre"

z - Very rare in this hand. Not clearly attested in the training materials.

=== 3. UPPERCASE / CAPITAL LETTER FORMS ===

The scribe uses decorated capitals, especially at the start of recipe titles and
at the beginning of recipe instructions (the word "Take" is almost always capitalized
with a large ornamental T).

A - Large, with a pointed top and often ornamental serifs. Appears in "A watter",
    "A Gargisine", "A plaister", "A swallowes", "A greene"

B - Large rounded form with clear double bowls. "Butt", "Boyle", "Butter",
    "Bowlarmene", "Broome"

D - Large with a curved back. "Decayed", "Docke"

F - Written as 'ff' (double-f) at word beginnings where we would expect a capital F.
    This is a standard secretary-hand convention. Examples: "ffasten" (= Fasten),
    "Fistula" — though "Fistula" and "Firste" appear to use a single ornamental F.
    The double-ff convention is most visible in "ffasten" on page 08.

G - Large, rounded. "Gargisine"

H - Tall with clear form. "Halle"

L - Tall, sometimes with decorative flourish. "Laye", "Liver", "Longes",
    "Langdebeefe"

M - Wide, with clear humps. "Make", "Marye", "Mallowes"

Q - Large with distinctive descending tail. "Quinsie", "Queene"

R - Large form with a clear leg. "Redd", "Reasons"

S - Round capital form (not long-s). "Sunne"

T - Very ornamental, especially in "Take" which begins most recipes. The T has
    a large crossbar and often decorative strokes. "Take", "To", "The", "This"

Y - Large with descender. "Yow"

=== 4. COMMONLY CONFUSED LETTER PAIRS ===

These are the most dangerous confusions in this hand:

f vs long-s (ſ): Both have ascenders and descenders. Key difference: f has a
    crossbar extending to the right side; long-s has only a leftward tick or no bar.
    Look at the word in context — if 'f' makes no sense, it is probably long-s.

c vs e: Both small and rounded. 'e' tends to have a more closed loop at top.

c vs t: If the ascender on 't' is very short, it can look like 'c'.

n vs u: Both are formed by two minims. 'n' has the connecting stroke at top,
    'u' at bottom. In quick writing, nearly identical. Use word context.

m vs 'in' or 'ni': The three minims of 'm' can look like 'in' or 'ni'. Context is key.

i vs l: Both are single vertical strokes. 'l' is taller. Dot over 'i' helps but
    is not always present.

=== 5. SPELLING CONVENTIONS ===

This scribe has highly consistent spelling habits:

Double letters — The scribe frequently doubles vowels and consonants:
    "teethe", "seethe", "bee", "keepe" (double ee)
    "putt", "ytt", "itt", "att", "lett" (double tt)
    "wille", "stille", "untille" (double ll + final e)
    "ffasten" (double ff for capital F)
    "spoonefulls", "handefull" (double ll at end)

Final -e — Very common. Added to many words: "withe" (with), "muche" (much),
    "bloude" (blood), "colde" (cold), "warme" (warm), "fyer" (fire→fier),
    "verie" (very), "owte" (out), "throte" (throat), "mouthe" (mouth)

-inge ending — Present participles and gerunds end in "-inge" not "-ing":
    "runninge", "stoppinge", "swellinge", "moreninge" (morning)

ytt/itt — "it" is spelled "ytt" or "itt" throughout. Both forms are used,
    sometimes interchangeably on the same page.

yow — "you" is consistently "yow"

withe — "with" is consistently "withe"

togeather — "together" is consistently "togeather"

thereto/thereof/therein — compound forms with "there-" appear as single words

eache — "each" is "eache"

owt/owte — "out" is "owt" or "owte"

=== 6. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS ===

This scribe uses relatively few abbreviations compared to some early modern hands:

[ounces] — The word "ounces" may be abbreviated with a symbol (see page 16,
    line 6: "two [ounces]"). Watch for abbreviated measurement terms.

Superscript letters — Some words may have superscript letters indicating
    abbreviation. Not heavily used in this hand.

The ampersand (&) — Does not appear to be used. "and" is written out in full.

=== 7. PUNCTUATION AND FORMATTING ===

Colons — Used occasionally as pauses or sentence divisions: "colde: washe"

Commas — Used after "Take" at the beginning of recipes and within ingredient lists.

Periods — Appear at the end of some recipes. Not consistently used.

Decorative dots/marks — Rows of dots or small marks appear between recipe titles
    and before/after headings as decorative separators. These are NOT transcribed
    as text but may appear as decorative elements.

Line breaks — The scribe writes continuously, breaking lines at the right margin.
    Words may be split across lines.

Indentation — Recipe titles are often centered or slightly indented. The word "Take"
    at the start of recipe instructions is often written large and set apart.

=== 8. RECIPE STRUCTURE AND FORMATTING ===

Each recipe follows a consistent pattern:
    1. Title line (often centered, sometimes with decorative marks)
       Example: "For the kinges euelle in the throte"
    2. Body begins with "Take" (large, ornamental T)
    3. Ingredients and instructions follow in running text
    4. Some recipes end with "probatum" (= "proved/tested") as validation

Recipe titles commonly begin with:
    "For..." (describing the ailment)
    "To..." (describing the action)
    "A..." (describing the product)

=== 9. VOCABULARY AND SUBJECT MATTER ===

This is a medical/herbal recipe book. Common vocabulary includes:

Medical terms:
    - Quinsie (quinsy — throat abscess)
    - Fistula (deep ulcer/wound)
    - euelle (evil — as in "king's evil", scrofula)
    - plaister (plaster — poultice)
    - tente (tent — a roll of lint for wound dressing)
    - gargole/gargolle (gargle)
    - probatum (Latin: tested/proved)
    - methridatum (mithridate — an antidote)

Ingredients:
    - Herbs: rosemarie, sage, rewe (rue), wormeworde (wormwood), fennelle,
      cinkfoud (cinquefoil), mallowes (mallows), valerion, archangell,
      cammamell (chamomile), polippes, neepp, perwinkell (periwinkle),
      clary, violetts
    - Foods: barlye, figges, reassons/Reasons (raisins), dates, suger/sugare candye,
      hunnye (honey), butter, waxe, breade, egge
    - Minerals: copperis (copperas), allome (alum), gommasticke (gum mastic),
      Bowlarmene (bole armeniac), coprose (copperas)
    - Other: bloude (blood), greace/grease, oyle, milke, wine, vinneger

Measurements:
    - handefull, spoonefull, quarte, pinte, pottell (pottle = half gallon),
      quartene, ounce/ounces, pounde

Cooking/preparation verbs:
    - seethe (boil), boyle, straine, stille (distill), beate, stampe (stamp/pound),
      burne, drie, pounde, bruse (bruise/crush), washe, annoynte

=== 10. WORD BOUNDARIES AND SPACING ===

The scribe generally maintains clear word spacing, but some common issues:

- "ytt" and "itt" can look connected to preceding words
- Prepositions may run into following words when written quickly
- Compound words may appear as one or two words: "togeather", "withein",
  "withowt", "moreninge"

=== 11. NUMBERS ===

Numbers are generally written as words:
    "three", "fower" (four), "sixe", "nine", "two"

=== 12. TIPS FOR TRANSCRIPTION ===

1. Read the recipe title first to understand the context — knowing the ailment
   helps predict ingredients and instructions.

2. Look for the word "Take" — it almost always begins the recipe body and is
   highly recognizable with its large ornamental T.

3. When stuck on a word, consider:
   - Is it an herb, ingredient, or medical term from the vocabulary above?
   - Does it follow the spelling conventions (final -e, double letters, -inge)?
   - Could confused minims (n/u/m/i) resolve to a known word?

4. Remember that long-s (looks like f) is common at word beginnings and middles.

5. Expect "ytt", "itt", "yow", "withe", "muche", "eache", "togeather" throughout.

6. Line breaks are arbitrary — a word may be split across lines.

7. If a word is truly illegible, mark it [...] rather than guess.

8. Pay attention to whether a hand is the same scribe or different — letter forms
   may vary between scribes even in the same manuscript.
