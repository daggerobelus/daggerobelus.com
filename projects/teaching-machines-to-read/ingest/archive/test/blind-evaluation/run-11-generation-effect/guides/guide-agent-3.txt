SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from Henslow MS688 (1601), 5 training pages
=======================================================

This guide documents the handwriting characteristics of a single scribe
writing an early modern English recipe/medical remedy book. All observations
are drawn from comparing manuscript images with verified transcriptions.

================================================================
1. GENERAL HAND CHARACTERISTICS
================================================================

This is a secretary hand with some italic influences, written in a fairly
consistent, moderately practiced style. The hand is generally legible but
has several systematic features that can trip up a modern reader.

Key overall features:
- The script leans slightly to the right
- Letters are generally well-formed but can be cramped within words
- The scribe uses a mix of secretary and italic letter forms
- Ink darkness varies — some passages are darker/thicker than others
- Line spacing is fairly generous, with clear separation between lines
- Words are generally separated, though spacing can be tight

================================================================
2. LETTER FORMS: LOWERCASE
================================================================

a - Secretary 'a' with a rounded bowl, sometimes open at the top.
    Can look like 'u' or 'o' when poorly formed. Example: "Take"
    appears at the start of nearly every recipe.

b - Has an ascender loop that rises above the line. The bowl sits on
    the baseline. Example: "bee" (page 08), "barlye" (page 16).

c - Small rounded letter, open on the right. Can be confused with 'e'
    when written quickly. Example: "colde" (page 10), "cresse" (page 16).

d - Secretary 'd' with a looped ascender that curves back to the LEFT
    (over the letter). This is a key secretary-hand feature. The loop
    often extends well above the line. Example: "drinke" (page 08, 10).
    IMPORTANT: Do not confuse this with 'cl' — the leftward loop is
    distinctive.

e - Small, often looks like a reversed '3' or a small curved stroke.
    Very common letter. Can be confused with 'c' or 'i'.
    Example: "euelle" (page 08), "eache" (page 10).

f - Has a descender that drops below the baseline, with a crossbar.
    The descender curves to the left. Can look like a long 's' but has
    a clear crossbar. Example: "for" (appears in every recipe title),
    "fier" (page 20).

g - Has a distinctive descending tail that loops back. The bowl sits
    on the baseline. Example: "gargolle" (page 14), "grease" (page 16).

h - Has an ascender and a curved stroke descending from the top.
    The right stroke curves down and can look like a second letter.
    Example: "handefull" (pages 10, 14, 16), "his" (page 14).

i - A simple minim (short vertical stroke) with no dot in this hand.
    VERY easily confused with other minims (m, n, u). Context is
    essential. Example: "in" (throughout), "itt" (throughout).

k - Has an ascender with two strokes meeting at midline. The lower
    strokes can look angular. Example: "keepe" (page 08), "killed"
    (page 20).

l - A simple ascender stroke, slightly taller than minims. Can be
    confused with the start of other letters. Example: "lett" (page 14),
    "longes" (page 10).

m - Three minims joined together. VERY difficult to distinguish from
    'in', 'ni', 'mi', 'iu', etc. Count minims carefully and use context.
    Example: "mallowes" (page 14), "milke" (page 14).

n - Two minims joined. See note about minims under 'm'.
    Example: "narrowe" (page 14), "neste" (page 14).

o - Rounded letter sitting on the baseline. Generally clear.
    Example: "ounce" (page 10, 16), "oyle" (page 16).

p - Has a descender dropping below the baseline and a bowl at the x-height.
    Example: "putt" (throughout), "powder" (page 20).

q - Rare. Has a descender. Example: "quantitye" (page 14),
    "quarte" (pages 08, 10), "Quinsie" (page 14).

r - Secretary 'r' can take two forms: (1) a short stroke descending
    from the preceding letter, looking like a '2' shape; (2) a more
    modern form after certain letters. Example: "runninge" (pages 08,
    10, 16), "rootes" (page 10).

s - TWO FORMS:
    (1) Long 's' — looks like 'f' but WITHOUT a crossbar. Used at the
    beginning and middle of words. Example: "seethe" (page 08),
    "straine" (pages 08, 10).
    (2) Short/round 's' — modern-looking 's', used at the END of words.
    Example: "eyes" (page 08), "rootes" (page 10).
    CRITICAL: Distinguishing long 's' from 'f' is one of the most
    important skills for this hand.

t - Has a short ascender with a crossbar. The crossbar may connect
    to the next letter. Example: "take" (throughout), "teethe" (page 08).

u/v - In this period, 'u' and 'v' are variants of the same letter:
    'v' is used at the START of words: "vntill" (page 14), "verie"
    (page 08)
    'u' is used in the MIDDLE of words: "putt" (throughout), "untill"
    (page 16 — note inconsistency, sometimes 'u' starts a word too)

w - A broad letter made of multiple strokes, often looking like 'vv'
    connected. Sits on the baseline and is wider than most letters.
    Example: "water" (throughout), "withe" (throughout), "waxe" (page 14).

x - Rare. Two crossing strokes. Example: "waxeth" (page 14).

y - Has a descender that curves or drops below the baseline. The top
    looks like a dotless 'i' or 'v' shape. Example: "yow" (throughout),
    "ytt" (throughout). NOTE: "ytt" = "it" in modern English.

z - Very rare in this text.

================================================================
3. LETTER FORMS: UPPERCASE / CAPITALS
================================================================

Capitals are used at the start of recipes (titles) and sometimes at the
start of sentences within recipes. They are often larger and more ornate
than lowercase letters.

A - Large, ornate form with decorative strokes. Example: "A watter"
    (page 08), "A plaister" (page 14), "A greene salve" (page 20).

B - Large rounded letter with two bumps. Example: "Bowlarmene" (page 08),
    "Butt" (page 20), "Boyle" (page 20).

C - Large curved letter. Example: "Cinkfoud" (page 14).

D - Large letter with curved back. Example: "Decayed" (page 08),
    "Docke" (page 10).

F - Large ornate form, clearly different from lowercase.
    Example: "For" (many recipe titles), "Firste" (page 14).

H - Large letter with two vertical strokes. Example: "Halle" (page 20).

L - Large ascending stroke. Example: "Liver" (page 10), "Longes" (page 10).

M - Broad letter. Example: "Marye" (page 10), "Make" (page 20).

Q - Large ornate letter with a long tail. Example: "Quinsie" (page 14),
    "Queene" (page 10).

R - Large letter. Example: "Redd" (page 20), "Reasons" (page 10).

S - Large ornate capital, different from both long 's' and round 's'.
    Example: "Sunne" (page 10).

T - Large form with prominent crossbar. Example: "Take" (throughout —
    appears at the start of nearly every recipe instruction),
    "The" (page 14), "This" (page 20).

Y - Large form. Example: "Yow" (page 20).

================================================================
4. CONFUSABLE LETTER PAIRS
================================================================

These are the most common sources of misreading:

f / long s — Both have descenders. 'f' has a crossbar; long 's' does not.
    Look carefully at the middle of the stroke for a horizontal line.

c / e — Both are small rounded letters. 'e' typically has a more angular
    or reversed-3 appearance; 'c' is more open.

u / n / m / i — All are minims. Count strokes carefully:
    i = 1 minim, n = 2 minims, m = 3 minims, u = 2 minims (like 'n'
    but curves at bottom instead of top). Context is crucial.

d / cl — Secretary 'd' loops left; 'cl' are two separate letters.

t / c — 't' has a clear crossbar and is slightly taller.

y / thorn (th) — In some hands, 'y' can represent the old letter thorn
    (= 'th'). In this manuscript, "ytt" appears to simply be a spelling
    variant of "it" using 'y', and "yow" = "you".

================================================================
5. SPELLING CONVENTIONS
================================================================

This scribe uses characteristic early modern English spelling:

Double letters are very common:
- "tt" for 't': "putt", "ytt", "itt", "lett", "butt", "att"
- "ff" for 'f': "ffasten" (page 08), "ofte" vs "offt"
- "ee" for long 'e': "teethe", "seethe", "keepe", "bee", "sweete"
- "ll" in many words: "wille", "halfe", "all", "untill"

-e endings are common on words that lack them in modern English:
- "withe" (with), "owte" (out), "inne" (in is usually "in" though)
- "mouthe" (mouth), "bloude" (blood), "softe" (soft)

"y" for "i" at the start of words:
- "ytt" = it, "yow" = you

"u" and "v" usage:
- "v" at start of words: "vntill", "verie", "vinneger"
- "u" in middle of words: "euelle" (evil), "untill" (inconsistent)

"-inge" endings: "runninge", "stoppinge", "swellinge" — present
participles consistently use "-inge"

"th" variations: "withe" (with), "withowt" (without), "togeather"
(together)

"ow" for modern "ou": "owte" (out), "withowt" (without), "fowre" (four)

================================================================
6. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS
================================================================

This scribe uses relatively few abbreviations compared to some
contemporary hands:

- Superscript letters occasionally appear to mark contractions
- "[eth]" ending: "folow[eth]" on page 14 — a word ending is abbreviated,
  with letters raised or compressed
- "probatum" appears at the end of some recipes as a nota bene meaning
  "proven/tested"
- Tildes or macrons over vowels can indicate a missing 'n' or 'm'
  (common in period hands, though infrequent here)
- "[ounces]" on page 16 — abbreviated with a symbol

================================================================
7. FORMATTING AND LAYOUT
================================================================

Recipe structure:
- Each recipe begins with a TITLE in slightly larger or more ornate script
- Titles typically follow the pattern: "For [ailment]" or "To [do something]"
- The word "Take" (with ornate capital T) begins the instructions of
  almost every recipe — this is the most common first word
- Some titles are preceded by decorative dots or dashes as separators
- "probatum" (meaning "proven") sometimes appears at the end of a recipe

Line breaks:
- Text runs continuously within a recipe, wrapping at the right margin
- Line breaks in the transcription correspond to physical line breaks
  on the page
- There is NO hyphenation when words break across lines — the word
  simply continues on the next line

Punctuation:
- Colons (:) are used fairly frequently as pauses within sentences
- Periods (.) appear occasionally
- Commas appear but are sometimes hard to distinguish from other marks
- There is very little systematic punctuation by modern standards

Paragraph structure:
- Blank lines separate recipes
- Within a recipe, text is continuous (no paragraph breaks)

================================================================
10. STRATEGIES FOR TRANSCRIBING AN UNSEEN PAGE
================================================================

1. Read through the entire page first to get a sense of content and context
2. Identify recipe titles first — they are larger and more ornate
3. Look for "Take" at the start of instructions — it is almost always there
4. Use the recipe context to disambiguate unclear words (ingredients,
   body parts, actions like boiling/straining)
5. Count minims carefully for m/n/u/i distinctions
6. Check for long 's' vs 'f' by looking for crossbars
7. Remember 'v' at word beginnings and 'u' in word middles
8. Preserve original spelling — do NOT modernize
9. Maintain line breaks as they appear on the page
10. Use [...] for truly illegible passages and [word?] for uncertain readings
11. Watch for "-inge" endings on present participles
12. Double consonants and silent '-e' endings are normal
13. "ytt" means "it" and "yow" means "you" — these appear constantly
14. Look for "probatum" at recipe endings
15. When stuck on a word, try sounding it out phonetically — early modern
    spelling is often phonetic
