SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from Henslow MS688 (1601), Five Training Pages
======================================================

This guide was built by comparing five manuscript images with their verified
transcriptions. It covers letter forms, confusable characters, abbreviations,
spelling conventions, and formatting patterns for early modern English
secretary/italic mixed hand, circa 1601.

========================================================================
1. GENERAL SCRIPT CHARACTERISTICS
========================================================================

The hand is a relatively clear English secretary hand with some italic
influences. It is written with a fairly consistent pen angle. The scribe
writes in a flowing style with moderate ligatures (connections between
letters). The overall impression is of a practiced but not highly formal
hand — this is a domestic recipe book, not a legal document.

The text is generally legible but certain letter forms are highly
confusable with each other. The key to accurate transcription is learning
which letters look alike and using context to disambiguate.

========================================================================
2. LOWERCASE LETTER FORMS
========================================================================

a - Secretary 'a' has two forms: (1) a single-compartment form that looks
    like a backward 'e' or a small loop, and (2) a more recognizable
    round 'a'. The single-compartment form is more common. It can look
    like 'o' or 'e' in some contexts.
    Examples: "and" (very frequent), "water", "take"

b - Has a tall ascender with a loop at the top, and a rounded bowl at
    the baseline. Fairly recognizable.
    Examples: "boyle", "bloude", "butter", "breade"

c - Small rounded letter, open on the right side. Can resemble 'e' or 'r'
    in some formations. Often has a slight hook at the top.
    Examples: "come", "colde", "cures"

d - Secretary 'd' has a distinctive looped ascender that curves to the LEFT
    (backward), not to the right like modern 'd'. This is one of the most
    distinctive secretary hand features. The loop often extends well above
    the line.
    Examples: "dothe", "daye", "dates", "dried"

e - Small, often looks like a reversed '3' or a simple curved stroke.
    Can be confused with 'c' or 'a'. When final, it often has a
    flourish or tail.
    Examples: "the" (very frequent), "eache", "these"

f - Has a long descender below the baseline AND an ascender above.
    Distinguished from long-s by having a crossbar that extends to the
    left. Double-f ("ff") is used at the start of some words where
    modern English uses capital F.
    Examples: "for", "fine", "ffasten" (double-f for capital)

g - Has a distinctive descending loop. The top portion is usually a
    small oval or open circle, with a descending stroke that loops back.
    Examples: "goode", "gargolle", "greace"

h - Has a tall ascender and the lower portion curves or loops below the
    baseline slightly. The form is relatively standard.
    Examples: "his", "halfe", "handefull", "heale"

i - A simple minim (short vertical stroke) with or without a dot.
    Can be confused with the first stroke of 'u', 'n', or 'm'.
    The dot is not always present.
    Examples: "in", "itt", "is"

k - Has an ascender with a looped or angular junction where two strokes
    meet at the midline. Relatively rare but distinctive.
    Examples: "keepe", "killed", "kinges"

l - A tall ascender, usually with a small loop at the top. Can
    resemble 'b' if the loop is prominent, but 'l' typically has no
    bowl at the baseline.
    Examples: "lett", "longe", "liquore"

m - Consists of three minims (short vertical strokes). The key
    challenge is distinguishing 'm' from 'in', 'ni', 'ui', etc.
    Context is essential.
    Examples: "make", "muche", "mouthe", "moreninge"

n - Two minims. Easily confused with 'u' since both are formed by
    two short vertical strokes. Context determines which is which.
    Examples: "and", "nine", "narrowe"

o - A round closed loop at the midline. Usually fairly clear but
    can be confused with 'a' in some formations.
    Examples: "of", "or", "one", "ounce"

p - Has a descender below the baseline and a bowl or curved stroke
    at the midline. Fairly recognizable.
    Examples: "putt", "powder", "potte", "paciente"

q - Rare. Has a descender with a rightward tail. Seen in words
    like "quarte", "quantite", "Quinsie".

r - Has TWO common forms: (1) a short stroke with a shoulder, looking
    somewhat like modern 'r', and (2) a '2'-shaped form especially
    after 'o' and rounded letters. The '2'-shaped r can be confused
    with 'z' or 'e'.
    Examples: "runninge", "rootes", "rose"

s - THREE forms: (1) long-s at the beginning and middle of words,
    which looks like 'f' but WITHOUT a leftward crossbar; (2) short/
    round 's' at the end of words, which looks like modern 's';
    (3) occasionally a sigma-like form. Long-s vs. f is one of the
    most critical distinctions.
    Examples: "some" (long-s initial), "roses" (round-s final),
    "straine" (long-s initial)

t - A short letter (does not reach full ascender height). Has a
    crossbar. Can be confused with 'c' when written quickly.
    The crossbar is sometimes very faint or connected to the next letter.
    Examples: "the", "to", "togeather", "teethe"

u/v - In this period, 'u' and 'v' are not yet fully distinguished.
    'v' tends to be used at the start of words, 'u' in the middle,
    regardless of whether the sound is a vowel or consonant.
    Examples: "verie" (= very), "untill" (= until), "vnder" (= under),
    "euelle" (= evil), "vntill"

w - A distinctive wide letter formed by two 'v' shapes joined together,
    or by a series of connected curves. Fairly recognizable.
    Examples: "water", "withe", "when", "waxe"

x - Rare. A simple cross shape.
    Examples: "sixe", "waxe"

y - Has a descender. The top looks like a modern 'y' but the descending
    stroke often has a distinctive leftward curve or loop.
    Examples: "yow", "ytt", "youre"

z - Very rare. Used in words like "razons" (raisins).

========================================================================
3. CAPITAL LETTER FORMS
========================================================================

A - Large, often with an angular top or a sweeping curved form.
    Distinguished from lowercase by size and often by a more elaborate
    opening stroke.
    Examples: "A watter", "A plaister", "Applye", "Archangell", "And"

B - Large with a prominent double-looped bowl. Often has a decorative
    initial stroke.
    Examples: "Boyle", "Butt", "Bowlarmene"

C - Large sweeping curved stroke, open on the right.
    Examples: "Cinkfoud"

D - Large with the characteristic leftward-looping ascender of secretary 'd'
    but scaled up.
    Examples: "Decayed", "Docke"

F - Large form, or rendered as "ff" (double-f) in some words.
    Examples: "For" (very common as recipe opener), "Firste", "Fistula"

G - Large with a descending loop.
    Examples: "Gargisine"

H - Large form with prominent ascender.
    Examples: "Halle"

L - Large, sometimes with an elaborate loop.
    Examples: "Liver", "Longes", "Laye", "Langdebeefe"

M - Large form starting with a downstroke.
    Examples: "Make", "Marye"

Q - Large with prominent descending tail. Often very decorative.
    Examples: "Quinsie", "Queene"

R - Large, can have an elaborate form with an extended leg.
    Examples: "Redd", "Reasons"

S - Large sigma-like or modern-S form. At the start of words the
    capital form is typically used rather than long-s.
    Examples: "Sunne"

T - Large with prominent crossbar, often with decorative serif.
    Examples: "Take" (very frequent as recipe opener), "The", "This", "To"

Y - Large with descender.
    Examples: "Yow"

The word "Take" appears as the opening of nearly every recipe and is
written with a large, decorative capital 'T'. Learning to recognize
this word is essential — it appears dozens of times.

========================================================================
4. HIGHLY CONFUSABLE LETTER PAIRS
========================================================================

This is the most critical section for accurate transcription.

f / long-s:
  Both have ascenders and descenders. The key distinction is that 'f'
  has a crossbar extending to the LEFT, while long-s does not (or has
  only a very faint one on the right). In practice, this is very hard
  to see. Use context: 'f' appears in words like "for", "fine", "fire",
  "fower"; long-s appears in "seethe", "some", "straine", "sweete".
  Examples: "softe" uses long-s then f then t
            "freshe" uses f at start then long-s+h in the middle

c / e / t:
  These three letters can look very similar when written quickly.
  'c' is open on the right, 'e' has a closed or nearly-closed loop,
  and 't' has a crossbar. Context is essential.

u / n / m / i:
  These minim-based letters are notoriously confusable. 'm' = 3 minims,
  'n' = 2 minims, 'u' = 2 minims (but with connecting curve at bottom),
  'i' = 1 minim. Groups like "min", "mun", "inn" etc. require careful
  counting and contextual reading.

a / o:
  In some formations, single-compartment 'a' closely resembles 'o'.
  Check context and word patterns.

========================================================================
5. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS
========================================================================

This scribe uses relatively few abbreviations, but some occur:

- Superscript letters: occasionally letters are written above the line
  to indicate abbreviation. E.g., a superscript 'e' after 'th' may
  indicate "the".

- Truncation with marks: words may be cut short with a horizontal
  stroke or tilde over the last letter to indicate missing letters.

- "ytt" = "it" — this is not an abbreviation but an archaic spelling.
  The 'y' here represents the thorn character (þ) which was written
  as 'y' in this period. Similarly "yow" = you.

- [ounces] — the scribe sometimes uses the ounce symbol (℥) which
  appears as a 'z'-like character with a horizontal line.

- "probatum" — Latin tag meaning "proven/tested", appears at the end
  of some recipes. Written in a slightly different style.

- "fo-" or "folow[eth]" — words broken across lines may have a hyphen
  or simply continue on the next line.

========================================================================
6. SPELLING CONVENTIONS
========================================================================

Early modern English spelling is not standardized. Key patterns in
this manuscript:

Double letters where modern English has single:
  - "tt" for "t": "itt" (it), "putt" (put), "ytt" (it), "att" (at),
    "butt" (but), "hott" (hot), "nott" (not), "lett" (let)
  - "ee" for "e": "teethe" (teeth), "seethe" (seethe), "keepe" (keep),
    "sweete" (sweet), "breede" (breed)
  - "ll" for "l": "untill" (until), "wille" (will), "spille" (spill)
  - "ff" for initial capital "F": "ffasten" (fasten)
  - "dd" for "d": less common but occurs

Final '-e' added to many words:
  - "colde" (cold), "warme" (warm), "longe" (long), "fyne" (fine),
    "mouthe" (mouth), "bloude" (blood), "eache" (each), "muche" (much),
    "withe" (with), "halfe" (half), "youre" (your)

'-inge' ending for '-ing':
  - "runninge", "moreninge", "stoppinge", "swellinge"

'y' for 'i' in many words:
  - "ytt" (it), "yow" (you), "youre" (your), "daye" (day),
    "fyer" (fire), "drye" (dry), "laye" (lay)

'-the' or '-te' endings:
  - "dothe" (doth), "mouthe" (mouth), "throte" (throat)

'w' where modern English has silent or different letters:
  - "owte" (out), "withowt" (without), "withein" (within)

Other notable spellings:
  - "verie" = very
  - "togeather" = together
  - "annoynte" = anoint
  - "quantite" / "quantitye" = quantity
  - "euelle" = evil
  - "coorrance" = currants
  - "Reasons" / "reassons" = raisins
  - "malmesye" = malmsey
  - "levened" = leavened
  - "spoonefulls" = spoonfuls
  - "handefull" = handful
  - "moreninge" = morning
  - "eveninge" = evening

========================================================================
7. FORMATTING AND LAYOUT
========================================================================

Recipe Structure:
  Each recipe follows a consistent pattern:
  1. Title line (often centered or prominent), usually starting with
     "For..." or "To..." or "A..."
  2. The word "Take" begins the instructions (sometimes on the same
     line as the title, sometimes on the next line)
  3. Ingredients and instructions follow in continuous prose
  4. Some recipes end with "probatum" (proven/tested)

Title formatting:
  - Titles are often written in a slightly larger hand
  - Sometimes decorated with rows of dots, dashes, or small ornaments
     before and after (e.g., "....." or "-----" or "~~~~~")
  - Titles may be centered on the page

Line breaks:
  - Lines break wherever the scribe reaches the right margin
  - Words are NOT hyphenated at line breaks — they simply continue on
     the next line (with occasional exceptions)
  - There is no indentation for continuation lines within a recipe

Punctuation:
  - Colons (:) are used occasionally as pauses or separators
  - Periods (.) appear but not consistently at sentence ends
  - Commas (,) appear after "Take" and between ingredients
  - Semicolons are rare or absent
  - The scribe does not use consistent modern punctuation

Spacing between recipes:
  - A blank line or small gap usually separates recipes
  - The new recipe title may have decorative elements

8. COMMON WORDS AND PHRASES
========================================================================

These words appear repeatedly and should be memorized for quick
recognition:

  "Take" - opens nearly every recipe (large decorative T)
  "and" - extremely frequent conjunction
  "the" - article
  "of" - preposition
  "in" - preposition
  "to" - preposition / infinitive marker
  "ytt" - it (with thorn-y)
  "itt" - it (alternate spelling)
  "putt" - put
  "withe" - with
  "for" - preposition (also opens recipe titles: "For the...")
  "yow" - you
  "youre" - your
  "lett" - let
  "them" - pronoun
  "then" - temporal adverb
  "halfe" - half
  "water" - water (key ingredient)
  "a" - article
  "or" - conjunction
  "bee" - be
  "is" - is
  "all" - all
  "as" - as

Medical/recipe vocabulary:
  "seethe/boyle" - to boil
  "straine" - strain
  "annoynte" - anoint
  "plaister" - plaster (poultice)
  "rootes" - roots
  "handefull" - handful
  "spoonefull" - spoonful
  "moreninge" - morning
  "eveninge" - evening
  "daye/dayes" - day/days
  "ounce/ounces" - ounce/ounces
  "quarte" - quart
  "pinte" - pint
  "probatum" - proven (Latin)
  "freshe" - fresh
  "warme" - warm
  "colde" - cold

========================================================================
9. WORD BOUNDARY CHALLENGES
========================================================================

The scribe's spacing is not always consistent. Some words may run
together, especially:
  - Short prepositions + articles: "of the", "in the", "to the"
  - Compound phrases: "togeather" (together), "withowt" (without),
    "withein" (within), "undernethe" (underneath)
  - "there to" vs "thereto" — sometimes written as one word

When in doubt about word boundaries, consider whether the result
makes sense as a single word or two separate words.

========================================================================
10. NUMBERS AND MEASUREMENTS
========================================================================

Numbers are typically written out as words, not as numerals:
  "two", "three", "fower" (four), "five", "sixe" (six), "nine"
  "halfe" (half), "a quarter"

Measurement terms:
  "quarte" (quart), "pinte" (pint), "pottell" (two quarts),
  "ounce", "handefull", "spoonefull"
  The ounce symbol (℥) may appear as a special character.

========================================================================
11. STRATEGIES FOR DIFFICULT PASSAGES
========================================================================

1. Read the WHOLE recipe first to understand context before focusing
   on individual words.
2. For illegible words, consider what ingredient, action, or quantity
   would make sense in context.
3. Cross-reference letter forms with clearly readable instances of the
   same letter elsewhere on the page.
4. Remember that spelling is variable — the same word may be spelled
   differently in different places (e.g., "quantite" / "quantitye").
5. Look for the characteristic secretary-hand 'd' (leftward loop) to
   orient yourself — it's one of the most distinctive features.
6. Count minims carefully for m/n/u/i disambiguation.
7. For f vs. long-s, look for the leftward crossbar on 'f'.
8. When a word seems to start with 'y', consider whether it might
   represent thorn (þ), making "yt" = "it", "ye" = "the".

========================================================================
12. NOTES ON DIFFERENT MANUSCRIPTS
========================================================================

While this guide was derived from a single manuscript (Henslow MS688),
many of these conventions apply broadly to English manuscripts of the
late 16th and early 17th centuries:
  - Secretary hand letter forms (especially d, e, r, s)
  - u/v interchange based on word position
  - Unstandardized spelling with characteristic patterns
  - Recipe book formatting conventions
  - Medical/herbal vocabulary

However, each scribe has individual habits. When encountering a new
hand, look first for clearly readable words to calibrate your eye,
then apply the general principles above while watching for the new
scribe's specific variations.
