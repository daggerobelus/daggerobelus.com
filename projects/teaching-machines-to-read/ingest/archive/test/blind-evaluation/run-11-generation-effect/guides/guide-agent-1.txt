SELF-TAUGHT PALEOGRAPHY GUIDE
Derived from Henslow MS688 (1601), Five Training Pages
========================================================

This guide was built by comparing five manuscript page images against their
verified transcriptions. Every observation is grounded in specific examples
from the training materials.

========================================================
1. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THE HAND
========================================================

This is an English secretary hand from circa 1601. The script is relatively
neat and consistent, with a slight right-leaning slant. The scribe writes
in a flowing, connected style — letters within words are often joined by
ligatures. Ink is brown/dark brown.

Recipe titles are written in a larger, more decorative script with
elaborate capitals. Body text is in a smaller, more cursive hand.

The text is a medical/household recipe book. Recipes follow a formula:
title (often beginning "For..." or "To..."), then "Take" with a large
decorated initial 'T', then ingredients and instructions.

========================================================
2. LOWERCASE LETTER FORMS
========================================================

a — Single-compartment secretary 'a'. A rounded bowl, sometimes open at
    top, with a curved stroke over it. Can resemble 'o' or 'u' when
    hastily formed.
    Examples: "Take" (every recipe), "and" (passim), "awaye" (p.08)

b — Tall ascender with a loop, bowl formed at baseline going right and
    curving back. Example: "bee" (p.08), "bloude" (p.08), "barlye" (p.16)

c — Small curved stroke open to the right. Can resemble 'e'.
    Examples: "colde" (p.10, p.14), "corralle" (p.08), "copperis" (p.08)

d — Secretary 'd': THE most distinctive letter. The ascender loops back
    to the LEFT, unlike modern 'd'. The leftward loop often extends over
    the preceding letter. Critical to recognize this form.
    Examples: "and" (passim), "drinke" (p.08), "dayes" (p.08)

e — Small curved letter, sometimes like a backwards '3' or small loop.
    Final 'e' is often just a small upward flick.
    Examples: "the" (passim), "eache" (p.10), "euelle" (p.08)

f — Descender goes well below baseline, with a crossbar extending through
    or to the right of the stem. The crossbar is the KEY distinction from
    long 's'. Double 'ff' used at word starts for /f/ sound: "ffasten".
    Examples: "for" (passim), "ffasten" (p.08), "fower" (p.14)

g — Bowl at baseline, descending tail loops below and often curves left.
    Examples: "gargolle" (p.14), "greace" (p.08), "goode" (p.16, p.20)

h — Tall ascender with loop. The second stroke curves down and right,
    sometimes dipping slightly below baseline.
    Examples: "the" (passim), "handefull" (p.10, p.16), "halfe" (p.08)

i — Single minim stroke, sometimes with a dot above (dots inconsistent).
    VERY easy to confuse with parts of m, n, u. Context essential.
    Examples: "in" (passim), "itt" (passim)

k — Tall ascender with two angled strokes forming right side. Can
    resemble 'lk' run together.
    Examples: "keepe" (p.08), "killed" (p.20)

l — Tall single ascending stroke with loop at top, like 'b' without bowl.
    Can be confused with 'i' if ascender is short.
    Examples: "lett" (p.14), "longes" (p.10), "liquore" (p.08)

m — Three connected minims (short vertical strokes). Easily confused with
    'ni', 'in', 'iu', 'nn'. Must count strokes and use context.
    Examples: "moreninge" (p.08, p.10), "mouthe" (p.14), "muche" (p.14)

n — Two connected minims. Must be distinguished from 'm' by stroke count.
    Examples: "narrowe" (p.14), "nine" (p.08), "neste" (p.14)

o — Rounded, closed. Similar to modern 'o'.
    Examples: "ouer" (p.14), "one" (p.16), "ounce" (p.10, p.16)

p — Descender below baseline with bowl to right at baseline.
    Examples: "putt" (passim), "potte" (p.14), "powder" (p.20)

q — Descender with bowl, rare. Usually followed by 'u'.
    Examples: "quarte" (p.08), "quantitye" (p.14), "Quinsie" (p.14)

r — Two forms:
    (1) Standard 'r': short stroke with shoulder, used after most letters.
    (2) Round 'r' (2-shaped): used after 'o' and round letters.
    Examples: "runninge" (p.08), "rootes" (p.10), "roses" (p.16)

s — THREE forms:
    (1) Long 's': tall stroke resembling 'f' but WITHOUT crossbar. Used
        at beginning and middle of words. Most common confusion source.
    (2) Short/round 's': like modern 's'. Used ONLY at end of words.
    (3) Capital 'S': large ornamental form at word/sentence start.
    Examples: "seethe" (p.08 — long s), "rootes" (p.10 — round s at end)
    CRITICAL: f vs long s is the #1 confusion. Check for crossbar.

t — Short vertical stroke with crossbar, which often connects to next
    letter. Double 'tt' very common: "itt", "putt", "att", "lett".
    Examples: "teethe" (p.08), "the" (passim), "togeather" (p.08)

u/v — Positional variants (period convention, not separate letters):
    'v' at START of words: "verie" (p.08), "vntill" (p.14)
    'u' in MIDDLE of words: "runninge" (p.08), "putt" (passim)

w — Broad letter formed from two connected 'v' shapes. Distinctive.
    Examples: "water" (p.08), "white" (p.08), "withe" (passim)

x — Rare. Two crossing strokes. Example: "waxeth" (p.14)

y — Descending tail, sometimes with dot above. Used in "ytt" (=it),
    "yow" (=you), "youre" (=your). The 'y' stands in for older thorn.
    Examples: "ytt" (passim), "yow" (p.08, p.20), "youre" (p.08, p.20)

========================================================
3. CAPITAL LETTER FORMS
========================================================

A — Large sweeping first stroke, decorative. "A watter" (p.08),
    "A plaister" (p.14), "A Gargisine" (p.16)

B — Large with decorative loops. "Bowlarmene" (p.08), "Butt" (p.20),
    "Boyle" (p.16, p.20)

C — Large curved stroke. "Cinkfoud" (p.14)

D — Rounded with tall looped ascender. "Decayed" (p.08), "Docke" (p.10)

F — Large ornamental with sweeping descender. "For" (passim, titles),
    "Firste" (p.14), "Fistula" (p.20)

G — Large rounded. "Gargisine" (p.16)

H — Tall vertical stroke with curved right side. "Halle" (p.20)

L — Tall with loop. "Liver" (p.10), "Longes" (p.10), "Laye" (p.20)

M — Broad, three-stroke structure. "Marye" (p.10), "Make" (p.20)

Q — Very ornamental with long sweeping tail. "Quinsie" (p.14),
    "Queene" (p.10)

R — Extended leg. "Redd" (p.20), "Reasons" (p.10)

S — Large swash form. "Sunne" (p.10), "stoppinge" (p.10)

T — Tall vertical with wide crossbar, often decorated. "Take" (passim),
    "To" (p.10, p.20), "The" (p.14), "This" (p.20)

Y — Large with descending tail. "Yow" (p.20)

========================================================
4. CRITICAL CONFUSABLE PAIRS
========================================================

f vs. long s: MOST IMPORTANT. Both are tall with descenders. 'f' has a
    crossbar extending through/across the stem; long 's' does NOT (or
    has only a slight leftward mark). When uncertain, ask: does the word
    make sense with 's' or 'f'?

c vs. e: Both small curved forms. 'e' has a more closed loop with tail;
    'c' is more open to the right.

u vs. n: Both are two minims. 'u' curves at bottom; 'n' arches at top.
    Nearly identical in practice. Use word context.

m vs. ni/in: 'm' = 3 minims; 'in'/'ni' = minim + dot + minim. Check
    for dots above 'i'. Also count strokes carefully.

i vs. l: 'l' has taller ascender. In hasty writing can look similar.

d vs. cl: Secretary 'd' with its leftward loop can look like 'cl' to
    an unfamiliar eye. Recognize the distinctive secretary 'd' form.

========================================================
5. ABBREVIATIONS AND CONTRACTIONS
========================================================

This scribe writes words out fairly fully. Observed abbreviations:

- Tildes/macrons over vowels: a line over a vowel may indicate an
  omitted nasal consonant (n or m).

- Terminal flourishes: decorative end-of-line strokes that do NOT
  represent additional letters. Do not transcribe them.

- Truncation at page/line edge: "folow[eth]" (p.14) where word is
  cut off — transcribe what is visible, bracket the rest.

- Abbreviated symbols: "[ounces]" (p.16, line 6) where the scribe
  uses an abbreviation symbol for the word.

- "probatum" (Latin: "tested/proven") appears at recipe ends.

========================================================
6. SPELLING CONVENTIONS
========================================================

Early modern spelling was NOT standardized. Key patterns:

DOUBLE LETTERS where modern English uses singles:
  "teethe" (teeth), "seethe", "bloude" (blood), "togeather" (together),
  "bee" (be), "putt" (put), "itt" (it), "lett" (let), "hott" (hot)

TERMINAL 'e' added to many words:
  "mouthe" (mouth), "throte" (throat), "bloude" (blood), "warme" (warm),
  "stiffe" (stiff), "owte" (out), "withe" (with)

'y' FOR 'i':
  "ytt" (it), "yow" (you), "youre" (your), "fyer" (fire), "daye" (day)

'-inge' ENDING (present participle):
  "runninge", "moreninge", "stoppinge", "swellinge", "eveninge"

'-eth' ENDING (third person singular):
  "waxeth", "achethe", "dothe"

'v' AT WORD START, 'u' IN MIDDLE:
  "verie" (very), "vntill" (until), "euelle" (evil)

'ff' FOR CAPITAL F:
  "ffasten" (p.08) — lowercase convention for initial /f/ sound

COMMON ARCHAIC/VARIANT SPELLINGS:
  "withe" (with), "witheall" (withal), "withowt" (without),
  "withein" (within), "allreadye" (already), "togeather" (together),
  "thereto", "thereof" (written as single words), "tymes" (times),
  "annoynte" (anoint), "cammamell" (chamomile), "perwinkell" (periwinkle),
  "malmesye" (malmsey), "Archangell" (archangel), "copperis" (copperas),
  "Bowlarmene" (bole armeniac), "gommasticke" (gum mastic)

========================================================
7. PUNCTUATION AND FORMATTING
========================================================

PUNCTUATION (minimal):
- Colons (:) as primary internal pause marker
- Commas after "Take" and between ingredients
- Periods at end of some recipes, sometimes with flourish
- Ampersand (&) used for "and": "& if the Quinsie" (p.14)
- No quotation marks or semicolons observed

RECIPE TITLES:
- Centered or near-centered on page
- Larger, more ornamental script
- Often preceded/followed by decorative dots or dashes ("......")
- Begin with "For...", "To...", or "A..."

RECIPE BODY:
- Opens with "Take" (large ornamental T) in nearly every recipe
- Continuous prose, not numbered steps
- Recipes separated by blank space

CAPITALIZATION:
- Recipe titles: capitals on major words
- "Take" always capitalized at recipe start
- Proper nouns capitalized: "Queene Marye"
- General ingredients lowercase

LINE BREAKS:
- Transcription follows manuscript line breaks exactly
- Words may break across lines without hyphenation

========================================================
8. WORD SPACING AND LIGATURES
========================================================

Word spacing is generally clear but can be inconsistent. Short
prepositions may crowd into following words.

Common ligatures (connected letter pairs):
- 'st': long 's' connects directly to 't'
- 'th': 't' crossbar extends into 'h'
- 'tt': double 't' connected
- 'ct': 'c' connects to 't'
- 'sh': long 's' connects to 'h'

9. RECIPE-SPECIFIC VOCABULARY
========================================================

INGREDIENTS: rosemarie, sage, rewe (rue), allome (alum), hunnye (honey),
  copperis (copperas), waxe, butter, greace (grease), figges (figs),
  dates, reasons/reassons (raisins), sugarr candye, vinneger (vinegar),
  malmesye (malmsey wine), oyle of roses

PLANTS: Broome (broom), Cinkfoud (cinquefoil), mallowes (mallows),
  valerion (valerian), fennelle (fennel), parsely (parsley), smallage,
  polippes (polypody), wormeworde (wormwood), Archangell, perwinkell,
  clary, neepp, hartes tounge

MEDICAL TERMS: Quinsie (quinsy), Fistula, kinges euelle (king's evil),
  plaister (plaster/poultice), pulltis (poultice), tente (wound probe
  lint), annoynte (anoint), gargolle (gargle), methridatum (mithridate)

BODY PARTS: teethe, throte, eyes, mouthe, longes (lungs), liuer (liver),
  splene (spleen), chinne, necke, arme, bloude (blood)

MEASUREMENTS: handefull, spoonefull, quarte, pinte, pottell (half gallon),
  ounce, quartene

ACTIONS: seethe (boil), straine (strain), boyle, beate, stille (distill),
  stampe (pound), bruse (bruise/crush), gargolle (gargle), annoynte

NUMBERS (written as words): two, three, fower (four), sixe (six),
  eighte (eight), nine

========================================================
10. TRANSCRIPTION METHOD FOR AN UNSEEN PAGE
========================================================

1. Read the WHOLE page first for overall context before transcribing.
   Knowing it is a recipe book helps predict vocabulary and structure.

2. Identify recipe boundaries: find titles (larger script, often with
   "For"/"To") and "Take" openings.

3. Start with words you can read confidently: "the", "and", "Take",
   "of", "in", "to", "for", "withe", "ytt", "putt".

4. Use recipe context to decode unclear words. If it says "Take a
   [???] of water", the blank is likely a measurement word.

5. For each uncertain letter:
   - Check f vs long s (crossbar?)
   - Count minims for m/n/u/i
   - Check for secretary d (leftward loop)
   - Check positional u/v

6. Do NOT modernize spelling. Write what the scribe wrote.

7. Preserve original line breaks exactly.

8. Mark genuinely illegible passages [...], uncertain readings [word?].

9. A different scribe will form letters differently, but secretary hand
   conventions (long s, secretary d, positional u/v, minim letters) are
   period-wide and should still apply.

10. Look for consistent patterns in the new scribe's hand. Once you
    identify how THEY form a particular letter, apply that consistently.
