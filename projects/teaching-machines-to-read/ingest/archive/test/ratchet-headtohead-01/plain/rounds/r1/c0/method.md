## Long-s, f, and ſ disambiguation (read this before transcribing any word with a tall vertical stroke)

This is an English secretary-hand recipe book. The single most common letterform error is confusing the long s (ſ) with the letter f. They look nearly identical: both are tall strokes that rise above the x-height and may descend below the baseline. You MUST decide between them by the rules below, not by visual impression alone. Resolve EVERY tall stroke as either s or f deliberately, letter by letter, before writing the word.

THE DECIDING TEST — the crossbar:
- The letter **f** has a horizontal crossbar that cuts fully THROUGH the stem, crossing to both the left and the right of the vertical stroke (it makes a small "+" on the stem).
- The long **s (ſ)** has at most a short nib or tick on the LEFT side of the stem only; it never has a stroke crossing to the RIGHT of the stem. If there is no bar crossing to the right of the stem, it is a long s, not an f.
- When the crossbar is faint, smudged, or ambiguous, do NOT guess from the ink. Fall back on the position and spelling rules below — they are more reliable than a faint bar.

POSITION AND SPELLING RULES (use these to confirm or override the visual reading):
1. **Final position:** the long s (ſ) is NEVER used at the end of a word. A tall stroke at the very end of a word is either a round terminal **s** or an **f** — it is never ſ. Words like "this," "was," "us," "his," plurals, and possessives all take round terminal s.
2. **Initial and medial position:** a tall stroke at the START of a word or BETWEEN two letters is far more often long s than f. Default to s in these positions unless the crossbar clearly crosses to the right of the stem.
3. **Before another consonant within a word** (st, sh, sp, sl, sc, sk), a tall stroke is long s: "moſt," "firſt," "diſtill," "muſt," "ſhall," "ſpoonefull," "ſlice."
4. **Doubled s:** early modern spelling writes double-s as **ſs** (long-s followed by round-s), e.g. "bleſsing," "preſserve," "poſsett." A tall stroke immediately followed by a round s is ſ, not f.
5. **ff:** a genuine double-f ("ffaire," "ſtuffe," "off") is two crossed stems side by side — both have bars crossing to the right. ſſ does not occur; the s-sound is always written ſs. So two adjacent tall strokes are "ff" ONLY if BOTH show a right-crossing bar; otherwise read "ſs" or "ſt" etc.
6. **Recipe-vocabulary cross-check (strong signal):** reading a tall stroke as f frequently produces a non-word; reading it as s usually yields a real recipe word. If your reading gives "uſe→ufe," "moſt→moft," "ſo→fo," "ſugar→fugar," "ſeeth→feeth," "ſafe→fafe," "fhall," "afe," that is a misread long s — change the f to s. Likewise keep f where it yields a real word ("for," "from," "of," "if," "after," "flower/flour," "fine," "halfe," "leafe," "fingers").

NORMALIZATION OF s-FORMS:
- For a semi-diplomatic transcription, transcribe BOTH the long s (ſ) and the round terminal s as a plain modern **s**. Do NOT carry the ſ glyph into the output, and do NOT silently turn an s into an f or an f into an s. The rules above exist so that each tall stroke is identified as the correct LETTER (s vs f); both forms of s are then written "s."
- Render the doubled long-s spelling (ſs) as "ss."
- Otherwise preserve the original period spelling of every word; the s/f rules change letter IDENTIFICATION only, not the rest of the spelling.

## General transcription guidance
- Transcribe line by line, in reading order, preserving original spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and word order.
- Expand nothing silently; if a letter is genuinely illegible, mark it [?].
- FINAL PASS (do this before submitting): re-scan every word and check each "f" you wrote. For each one, ask: does the bar cross to the RIGHT of the stem, AND does "f" give a real word? If either answer is no — especially if the stroke is word-initial, word-medial, before t/h/p/c, or doubled with a round s — rewrite it as "s." Then re-check the crossbar test on any remaining tall strokes.
