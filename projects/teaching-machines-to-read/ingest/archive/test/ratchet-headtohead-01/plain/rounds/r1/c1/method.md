## Minim discipline (i / m / n / u / w)

This is secretary hand. Many letters are built from "minims" — short vertical down-strokes that all look alike: i, m, n, u, and w are nothing but runs of these strokes. The single most common reading mistake is miscounting the strokes in such a run, so slow down at every cluster of vertical strokes and resolve it deliberately:

1. **Count the down-strokes first, then assign letters.** A run of minims has a fixed stroke count regardless of how you read it. Two strokes = one of {n, u, ii, w-start}; three strokes = one of {m, ni, in, iu, ui, w}; four strokes = {mi, in+u, nm, ...}, etc. Decide the count before guessing the word.

2. **Use the dots and the word to break ties.** An i normally carries a dot or short tick above its stroke (the dot may drift right). If a stroke in the run is dotted, it is an i; assign the remaining strokes to n/m/u. Then check the candidate against a plausible English word — a real word almost always disambiguates m vs in vs ni and u vs n.

3. **Distinguish n / u by neighbors, not shape.** n and u are often the same two strokes; tell them apart by how they join the surrounding letters and by the word: e.g., "un", "nu", "mun", "min". When unsure between n and u, pick the reading that yields a real word.

4. **Do not silently drop or add a stroke.** Resist "smoothing" a long minim run into a shorter familiar word. If you read a word and your letters use fewer or more strokes than the ink shows, recount.

5. **Watch the high-frequency minim traps:** final -ing / -inge (three to four strokes after the body), -ion / -ioun, "him/hir/them", "minute/mince/comin", and double letters (nn, mm, uu) which add a stroke each.

If after this you still cannot resolve a minim run, transcribe your single best letter-by-letter reading rather than substituting an unrelated word. Preserve original spelling; do not modernize.
