## Reading minim runs (i, m, n, u, and adjacent letters)

In English secretary hand, the letters i, m, n, u (and parts of w) are written as
near-identical vertical down-strokes called minims. A short word can become an
ambiguous "picket fence" of strokes (e.g. "minim" itself is ten minims in a row).
Miscounting or misgrouping these strokes is the single most common transcription
error. Work through every minim run deliberately:

1. Do not guess from overall word shape. Count the individual minims in the run,
   then decide how to group them into letters.

2. Use these stroke counts when grouping: i = 1 minim (with a dot or tittle above,
   often drifting right or absent); n = 2 minims; u = 2 minims; m = 3 minims;
   w = 4 minims (often with a connecting hairline). The letters r and the second
   stroke of h/b/p can also contribute stroke-like forms — account for them too.

3. Resolve the grouping by the actual word, not by a plausible-looking word. Read
   the surrounding letters first, then test which grouping yields a real word that
   fits the recipe context. "iu" vs "u", "ni" vs "in" vs "m", "un" vs "nu" vs "uin"
   are classic confusions — verify against the lexical sense of the line.

4. Watch the diacritic dots/ticks: a tick over a run signals an i or j and tells
   you where a single minim sits among its neighbors. Use dot placement to anchor
   the count even when it is shifted to one side.

5. Distinguish u and v by position, not by stroke: medieval/early-modern usage
   often writes v initially and u medially (e.g. "vpon", "loue"). Transcribe the
   letter as written rather than modernizing.

6. After transcribing a word containing a minim run, re-read it once and confirm
   the letter count matches the stroke count you observed. If a reading requires
   inventing or dropping a stroke, prefer the grouping that respects the strokes
   actually on the page.

Preserve original spelling, abbreviations, and word forms; do not modernize.
