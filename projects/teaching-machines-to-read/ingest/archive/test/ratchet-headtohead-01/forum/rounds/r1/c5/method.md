# Method: Transcribe by First Building This Hand's Capital & Punctuation Chart

You are transcribing a page of an English secretary-hand recipe book. Do NOT
start transcribing line 1 immediately. First build a small chart of how THIS
scribe forms letters and marks, then transcribe against your own chart. The goal
is a semi-diplomatic transcription that is faithful to the page.

## Step 1 — Survey the page (do not transcribe yet)

Read across the whole page once before writing anything. You are gathering
evidence about this specific hand. Pay special attention to the two things that
single-character error counts punish hardest and that scribes handle
unpredictably: which letters are capitalized, and which marks are punctuation
vs. flourishes.

## Step 2 — Build your own intermediate chart for THIS hand

Write out (for your own use) a short chart in two parts. Derive every entry from
shapes you actually see on this page — not from a generic alphabet. The chart is
your reasoning instrument: decide this scribe's habits ONCE, then apply them
uniformly, instead of re-guessing case and punctuation letter by letter.

### Part A — Capital vs. minuscule

Secretary hand often makes a capital look like an enlarged or differently-angled
version of its minuscule, and several letters are routinely written large
mid-word with no capital meaning. For each high-confusion letter, find a clear
minuscule example AND (if present) a clear capital example on this page, and note
exactly what separates them — size relative to x-height, an added entry stroke,
extra looping, a swash:

- C / c, S / s, W / w, A / a, T / t, R / r, M / m, N / n, I / i, J, V / v, U / u,
  P / p, B / b, L / l, E / e, G / g, H / h, K / k, O / o, F / f

For each, write a one-line rule of thumb you will apply consistently, e.g. "S is
capital only when its bowl is clearly larger than neighbouring x-height letters."
Note this hand's chronic over-capitalizers (ornamental initial **S, A, T, W, R,
C** and enlarged ingredient words like "Take"): an enlarged initial at the start
of a line or recipe is decoration, NOT evidence of a capital, unless the word is
one you would capitalize anyway.

Record a default for letters whose majuscule and minuscule are genuinely
indistinguishable in this hand (commonly **c, k, m, o, s, u, v, w**): default to
**lowercase** unless the word is the first word of a sentence, a proper name, or
the pronoun "I". State this default in your chart and obey it everywhere.

### Part B — Marks and punctuation

List the marks this scribe actually uses and what each one is:

- Sentence/clause separators: is the mid-line point a period, a comma, or a
  raised point (·)? Transcribe periods as `.` and commas as `,`. Record a raised
  point only if the scribe clearly uses one as punctuation.
- Virgule `/`: note whether the scribe uses a slash as a comma/period
  equivalent and transcribe it as `/` exactly where it appears. Do NOT silently
  convert a virgule to a comma or drop it.
- Terminal punctuation is often ABSENT: many lines and recipes simply end with
  no mark. Do not invent a period the page does not show.
- Brevigraphs / abbreviation marks — IDENTIFY them so you do not misread them as
  punctuation or as extra letters, but transcribe them as written (this is a
  semi-diplomatic transcription; do not silently expand): a bar/macron over a
  letter marking an omitted m/n, the `er`/`ar` loop, the terminal curl, the
  `p`-with-stroke, and superscript forms (`wch`, `wt`, `yt`, `ye`, `yͤ`, `&`,
  `&c`). Keep the abbreviation in the form the scribe wrote.
- Distinguish a real punctuation mark from a pen flourish or a descender:
  ascending/descending loops, paraphs, and end-of-line fillers are NOT
  punctuation and must not be transcribed as marks.

## Step 3 — Fix capitalization and punctuation policy before writing

Commit to these rules and apply them uniformly:

1. **Capitalize** only what the letterform in your Part-A chart shows as a
   majuscule. As a guide to where majuscules legitimately fall, expect them at:
   the first word of a sentence; proper names (people, places); and "I" as the
   pronoun. Do NOT capitalize common nouns, ingredient names, or the first word
   of a recipe line merely because its initial letter is enlarged.
2. When a letter's case is genuinely ambiguous after Step 2, follow your Part-A
   default (lowercase mid-sentence).
3. **Punctuation:** transcribe only marks the scribe actually made. Add no modern
   commas, periods, or apostrophes that are not on the page; drop no mark that is
   present. Preserve the scribe's `/` virgules as `/`.
4. Keep original spelling exactly: `u`/`v`, `i`/`j`, doubled letters, archaic
   forms, and original word division all stay as written. Read long-s as `s`. Do
   not modernize spelling.

## Step 4 — Transcribe against your chart

Now transcribe line by line, top to bottom. For every capital you write and
every punctuation mark you write, check it against the chart and policy from
Steps 2–3. When a letterform is unclear, read bottom-up (what strokes are on the
page?) and prefer the reading your chart says is most frequent for this scribe;
do not guess the likeliest word top-down. Mark a single uncertain word `[?]` and
a longer illegible stretch `[...]` rather than inventing text.

## Step 5 — Capitalization & punctuation pass

Re-read your transcription once, ignoring word meaning, and check only these:

- Is each capital you typed matched by a majuscule form in your Part-A chart?
  Lowercase any that are not.
- Does each punctuation mark correspond to a mark on the page, and is no page
  mark missing? Add or remove to match the page; confirm every virgule is `/`.
- Is long-s rendered as `s` and are `u`/`v` and `i`/`j` preserved as written?

## Output

Output only the transcription text, line by line in reading order. Preserve line
breaks. Do not include your Step 2 chart, notes, or commentary in the final
output.
