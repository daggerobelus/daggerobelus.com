# Paleography Guide for Early Modern Recipe Book Transcription

This guide encodes the Folger Shakespeare Library's paleography training materials for use in semi-diplomatic transcription of early modern English manuscripts. It is based on the Folger's handouts on secretary hand, common abbreviations, and editorial conventions.

Source images of the original Folger guide are in `folger-paleography-guide/`.

---

## Part 1: Transcription vs. Translation

Transcription and translation are two fundamentally different tasks. This project treats them as **separate steps** that must never be conflated.

### Transcription (Primary Task)

Transcription is the faithful reproduction of what is written on the manuscript page. The goal is to convert handwritten text into typed text **as the scribe wrote it**, preserving the original language, spelling, grammar, and structure.

A transcription:
- Preserves original spelling exactly ("physicke" not "physic", "chirurgery" not "surgery")
- Preserves original punctuation and capitalization
- Preserves original line breaks and indentation
- Expands abbreviations with supplied letters marked in italics
- Records deletions, insertions, and marginal notes
- Reproduces errors in the original — transcribe what is *there*, not what was *meant*
- Flags illegible or uncertain readings rather than guessing

Transcription requires **paleographic skill** (the ability to read historical handwriting) but does **not** require interpretation of meaning.

### Translation (Separate, Optional Step)

Translation converts the transcribed text into modern, readable English. It is an act of **interpretation** and should only be performed after a transcription is complete.

A translation may:
- Modernize spelling ("physicke" → "physic")
- Expand and modernize abbreviations
- Clarify obsolete terminology
- Regularize grammar and punctuation
- Paraphrase for clarity

**This project focuses on transcription. Translation is not currently in scope.**

## Part 2: The Secretary Hand

Early modern English manuscripts (roughly 1500–1700) are typically written in **secretary hand**, a script that looks very different from modern handwriting. Many letters have forms that bear little resemblance to their modern equivalents.

### Key Differences from Modern Handwriting

Letters that are especially confusing to modern readers:

| Letter | What it looks like in secretary hand |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| **e** | Often a reversed or backward-facing loop, easily confused with o |
| **c** | Can look like r or t |
| **r** | Multiple forms — sometimes looks like a modern v or a 2-shaped mark |
| **s** | Two forms: the **long-s** (ſ) looks like an f without a full crossbar; round s used at word-end |
| **h** | The descender often loops far below the line, can be confused with b |
| **d** | Often has a looped ascender that curves back to the left |
| **f** | Similar to long-s but with a crossbar; can be confused with ſ |
| **k** | Often very elaborate with loops |
| **p** | Has a special form (see abbreviations) that can mean pre-, pro-, par-, per- |
| **th** | Often written with a **thorn** (þ) that looks like a y — this is why "ye olde" was actually "the olde" |
| **v/u** | Used interchangeably; v typically at the start of a word, u in the middle ("vpon" = upon, "haue" = have) |
| **i/j** | Used interchangeably; j often used as the final digit in Roman numerals (e.g., "iij" = 3) |
| **ff** | Double-f at the start of a word represents a capital F |

### Reading Tips (from the Folger)

1. Paleography is NOT an exact science
2. Forget what modern letters look like, and forget how to spell
3. The OED (Oxford English Dictionary) is essential — it includes archaic spellings and obsolete words
4. Attention to detail, including punctuation, is paramount
5. Every individual has an individual, inconsistent hand, and when hurried or incapacitated, the hand degenerates
6. Think of how the letters are formed — work out the direction of pen strokes (duct) and lifts. The pliancy of the quill pen means lighter, thinner strokes are usually upstrokes and thicker strokes are usually downstrokes
7. Compile your own alphabet from letters in the manuscript, focusing especially on letters with unique ascenders, descenders, loops, bowls, spurs, etc.
8. Minuscules (lowercase) and majuscules (uppercase) are often used inconsistently
9. Watch out for interference from letters in the lines above and below the line you are working on
10. If you are not sure of a word, write the letters you know, and put dots or x's to indicate the illegible bits (and return to them later)

## Part 3: Common Abbreviations

Early modern scribes routinely abbreviated words to save time and space. The following are standard abbreviations found across manuscripts of this period.

### Abbreviated Words

| Abbreviation | Expansion | Notes |
|-------------|-----------|-------|
| A° | Anno | |
| Esq. | Esquire | |
| Ex', Exañte | Exalte, Examinant, Examinante | |
| Ho', hono^ble | Honorable | |
| Imp, inp | Imprimis, in primis (in the first place) | |
| Itm, it | Item | |
| La', La^p | Lady, Ladyship | |
| lre, lres | lettre, lettres | |
| L', Lo, L^p | Lord or Lordship | |
| Ls, L^ps, Lo^pps | Lords or Lordships | |
| Ma^ty, Ma^tie | Majesty, Majestie | |
| M^r | Master or Mister | |
| m^d, mem. | memorandum | |
| M^ris, M^is | Mistress | |
| o^r | our | |
| p^d | paid | |
| S^r | Sir | |
| wor', wo' | Worshipful, worship | |
| w^ch | which | |
| w^th | with | |
| th^m | them | |
| th^t | that | |
| yo', y^r | your | |

### Abbreviated Word Endings

| Mark | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| -m^t | -ment | procurem^te = procuremente |
| -cõn, -siõn, -tĩon | -cion, -sion, -tion | affecciõn = affeccion |
| ~ above m, n, or vowel | add an m or n | cõmendacõns = commendacions; mã = man |

### Special Graphs (Shorthand Marks)

| Graph | Meaning | What it looks like | Examples |
|-------|---------|-------------------|----------|
| **-es graph** | -es | A looped downstroke at the end of a word | poundes, hazardes, buttes |
| **-er graph** | -er (but could also be -ar, -or, -re) | A hook-shaped upstroke | neuer (never), recouer (recover), generall |
| **-ur graph** | superscript r, often at end of word | A small r written above the line | Reuercõns (Reverencions), appurtenonces |
| **"special" p** | pre-, pro-, par-, per- | The descender of p is modified | persuadio, persons, parish, premisses, prosperitie |
| **&c.** | et cetera, or etc. | | |
| **xp** | Christ | From Greek chi (χ) and rho (ρ), the first two letters of the Greek word for Christ | Christofer |
| **pnt-es** | presentes | Often found in bargain and sale documents | |
| **"special" s** | ser- or sir | | your seruaunt |
| **"the" elisions** | the (with e dropped) | | thonor = the honor |

### Important Notes on Abbreviations

- Sometimes something looks like an abbreviation but actually is not — it may contain a superfluous tittle or a letter that has been superscripted for no apparent reason
- Sometimes a word is abbreviated but not according to any of the standard rules above (e.g., "accordinge" for according, "you" written in shorthand, "granted" or "bargained" written with unusual contractions)
- When in doubt, transcribe what you see and flag for review

## Part 4: Editorial Conventions for Semi-Diplomatic Transcription

These conventions are from the Folger Shakespeare Library and define the standard for this project.

### Header

Each transcription should begin with:
- Repository name
- Manuscript shelfmark
- Page or folio number
- A brief statement of editorial conventions (if diverging from the standard below)

Make the statement as concise and clear as possible.

### Spelling and Orthography

- **Original spelling is maintained** — including u/v and i/j usage, and ff for capital F
- **Original punctuation is maintained**
- **Original capitalization is maintained**
- **Original lineation (line breaks) and indentation are maintained**

### Superscript Letters

- Superscript letters are **silently lowered** (brought down to the baseline)
- Example: w^th → wth; y^r → yr

### Abbreviations

- Abbreviations are **expanded**, with the supplied letters in *italics*
- Exception: forms of address (Mr, Mrs) and symbols for money (l, s, d) are kept as-is
- **The orthography of the scribe is observed** when supplying letters — expand according to how the scribe spells the word elsewhere in the manuscript, not according to modern spelling

### Thorn and Special Graphs

- **Thorn** (þ, which looks like y) is replaced with italicized *th*
- **Terminal -es graphs** are replaced with -es (in italics if the graph is an abbreviation)

### Brevigraphs and Symbols

- **Ampersands** (& and &c.) are **preserved** as-is

### Deletions and Insertions

- **Cancelled (struck-through) words** are shown with ~~strikethrough~~
- **Interlineal insertions** (text added above the line) remain superscript and are marked by a caret symbol (^) if a caret is present in the original

### Illegible and Missing Text

- **Indecipherable or obliterated words** appear in square brackets with dots representing the estimated number of missing letters: [b....es]
- When uncertain about a reading, provide your best guess in square brackets: [word?]

### Things Not Represented

- Accidental obliterations, flourishes, and line fillers are **not represented** in the transcription
- They can be noted in a general description or textual note if significant

### Special Features

- Any other notable feature — such as a **change in hand**, change in **ink**, change in **size of script** — can be mentioned in a textual note

## Part 5: Confidence Flagging

When transcribing, every word or passage should be assigned a confidence level:

### High Confidence
The reading is clear and unambiguous. No flag needed.

### Medium Confidence
The reading is probable but not certain — perhaps the letterforms are ambiguous, the ink is faded, or the word is unfamiliar. Flag with a question mark in brackets:
```
Take the [iuice?] of roses
```

### Low Confidence
The reading is a guess at best, or the text is largely illegible. Flag with square brackets and dots for estimated missing letters:
```
Take [......] and mix with [.......]
```

### Contextual Notes
When a reading depends on context (e.g., you recognize a word because of surrounding recipe vocabulary rather than being able to read every letter), note this:
```
Take the iuice of roses {context: common recipe ingredient; letterforms partially obscured}
```

## Part 6: Recipe-Specific Vocabulary

Early modern recipe books use specialized vocabulary. Familiarity with these terms aids transcription.

### Common Recipe Terms

- **Receipt/recipe** — used interchangeably in this period
- **Take** — the standard opening word for most recipes
- **Dram, scruple, grain** — apothecary weights
- **Pennyworth** — a measure by cost
- **Pottle, quart, pint, gill** — liquid measures
- **Seeth/seethe** — to boil
- **Decoction** — liquid produced by boiling an ingredient
- **Poultice/cataplasm** — a soft, moist mass applied to the body
- **Salve/unguent** — ointments
- **Cordial** — a stimulating medicine or drink
- **Electuary** — a medicinal paste mixed with honey or syrup
- **Simples** — individual herbal ingredients
- **Still/distill** — to extract by vaporization
- **Anoint** — to apply oil or ointment
- **Posset** — a hot drink of curdled milk with ale or wine
- **Caudle** — a warm drink of thin gruel mixed with wine or ale
- **Pipkin** — a small earthenware pot
- **Mortar** — a bowl for grinding ingredients
- **Bolus** — a large pill
- **Plague water** — a compound distilled water thought to protect against plague
- **Aqua vitae** — distilled spirits

### Common Ingredients

- **Venice turpentine** — a resin from larch trees
- **Dragon water** — distilled water of tarragon
- **Rosewater** — distilled from rose petals
- **Harts tongue** — a fern (Asplenium scolopendrium)
- **Buglosse** — a plant (Anchusa)
- **Sack** — a dry white wine from Spain
- **Claret** — red Bordeaux wine
- **Ale/beer** — brewing was a domestic activity
- **Suet** — hard fat around kidneys (often deer or mutton)
- **Mace** — the outer covering of nutmeg
- **Grains of paradise** — a spice (Aframomum melegueta)
- **Sallet oil** — salad oil, typically olive oil
- **Bole armeniac** — an astringent clay
- **White lead/ceruse** — a cosmetic (toxic)
- **Quicksilver** — mercury
