HAND-SPECIFIC ALPHABET: Henslow MS 688, Page 12
=================================================

Source: Folger Shakespeare Library, MS V.b.366 (Henslow MS 688), page 12
Date of analysis: 2026-02-27
Method: Bottom-up letterform identification from manuscript image


GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THIS HAND
======================================

Size: Medium-to-large hand. The scribe writes with a fairly generous size,
making individual letters generally readable. Recipe headings are slightly
larger and more deliberately formed than body text.

Slant: Slight rightward slant, fairly consistent throughout. The hand does
not lean dramatically in either direction.

Spacing: Word spacing is generally clear and consistent. Letter spacing
within words is moderate -- letters are neither crowded together nor widely
spaced. Lines are well separated with adequate interlinear space.

Regularity: This is a moderately regular hand. The scribe is clearly
literate and practiced but not a professional scrivener. There is some
variation in letter formation, especially between the more carefully written
headings and the body text. The hand becomes slightly less regular in the
middle of the page (the "pyles" recipe body text) where there may have been
faster writing.

Pen pressure: Moderate and fairly consistent. Downstrokes are visibly thicker
than upstrokes, as expected with a quill pen. The scribe maintains a
reasonable ink flow throughout -- no major blotting or dramatic thinning.

Baseline: Generally steady. The text follows a consistent horizontal
baseline, suggesting the scribe may have used a ruling or guide.

Ink: Dark brown/black ink. Appears consistent throughout the page, suggesting
the recipes were likely written in a single session or with the same ink.


LETTERFORM INVENTORY
=====================

--- a ---

Letterform description: The scribe forms 'a' as a secretary-style letter
with a somewhat rounded body. It consists of a small curved stroke on the
left (like a backwards c) joined to a vertical or slightly curved stroke on
the right that comes back down. The letter sits on the baseline and is a
minim-height letter. In some instances the 'a' is quite open at the top;
in others it closes into a more compact form.

Example words:
  - "a" (standalone preposition), line 2: very clear isolated example
  - "Take" (appears multiple times as recipe opener): the 'a' is clear in
    each instance, e.g., line 3 word 1, line 8 word 1, line 12 word 1
  - "handfull", line 3: the 'a' is clearly formed after the 'h'
  - "Caunteberries", line 1: the 'a' after the 'C'

Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'o' when the top is left open, or
with 'u' when hastily formed. In this hand, 'a' tends to have a more angular
top closure than the round 'o'.

Variants: Mostly one form used consistently. Slightly more open in body text
versus headings.


--- b ---

Letterform description: The 'b' has a tall ascender that rises well above
minim height, with a rounded bowl at the baseline. The ascender is a
straight or very slightly curved upstroke, and the bowl is formed with a
clockwise curve that returns to the stem. This is a fairly standard form,
closer to modern 'b' than many secretary hands.

Example words:
  - "Brothe", line 2: clear 'b' at the start (though capitalized, the
    minuscule form is similar)
  - "before", line 3: clear 'b' at word start
  - "Boorrage", line 4: initial 'b' (may be capitalized)
  - "butter", line 13: clear 'b'

Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'h' (both have ascenders), but in
this hand, 'h' has a more pronounced rightward hook or arch at the top of
the descending stroke, while 'b' has a closed bowl. Also potentially
confused with 'l' if the bowl is small, but the bowl distinguishes it.

Variants: One primary form. Capitalized B has a larger, more elaborate bowl.


--- c ---

Letterform description: The 'c' is formed as a simple leftward-opening
curve, sitting at minim height. It is relatively small and compact. In
this hand it looks fairly close to a modern 'c' -- a simple open curve to
the left.

Example words:
  - "Consumpshone", line 2: the 'c' (though capitalized here)
  - "leaues", multiple occurrences lines 3-4: the context helps identify
    the 'c' is not present here, but in "Colewort" (line 9) the 'c' is clear
  - "Colewort", line 9 (approximate): clear initial 'c'

Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'r' in secretary hand, as noted in
the paleography guide. In this hand, 'c' tends to be more smoothly curved,
while 'r' (in its secretary form) has a more angular or pointed top.
Also sometimes hard to distinguish from 't' when the crossbar of 't' is
faint.

Variants: One primary form. The capital C is much larger and more
elaborately curved.


--- d ---

Letterform description: The scribe uses a secretary-style 'd' with a
looped ascender that curves back to the LEFT. This is the classic secretary
'd' -- the bowl is formed at the baseline and the ascender loops up and
back over the letter, arching to the left. This is one of the most
distinctive secretary-hand features in this manuscript.

Example words:
  - "handfull", line 3: the 'd' is visible mid-word
  - "and" (appears frequently): every "and" on the page shows this looped-
    back ascender form, e.g., line 4 "and Strawberrie", line 9 "and grinde"
  - "good", line 10: clear 'd' at end

Confusion risks: The leftward-looping ascender can cause confusion with
other letters if the loop is exaggerated. In this hand, 'd' is fairly
distinctive because of that backward-curving ascender. Could potentially
be confused with 'cl' combination if the loop is read as two strokes.

Variants: Consistently uses the secretary looped-d throughout. No modern
'd' form observed.


--- e ---

Letterform description: The 'e' in this hand uses the secretary form -- a
reversed or backward-facing loop. It looks like a small loop that opens to
the left, quite different from a modern 'e'. Some instances are more like
a small backwards 'c' with a connecting stroke. The letter sits at minim
height.

Example words:
  - "the" (appears many times): the 'e' at the end is clearly visible in
    the heading "for the pyles" (line 7)
  - "leaues" (lines 3-4): the initial 'e' is visible after the 'l'
  - "before", line 3: the final 'e' is clearly a reversed loop
  - "Brothe", line 2: final 'e' clearly visible

Confusion risks: The secretary 'e' is the HIGHEST confusion risk in this
hand. It can look very similar to 'o' when the loop is round, or like a
stray mark. In this particular hand, the 'e' tends to be slightly smaller
and more angular than the 'o', and it often has a slight tail or connecting
stroke going to the right toward the next letter.

Variants: Mostly consistent secretary 'e' form. Occasionally a slightly
more open form appears.


--- f ---

Letterform description: The 'f' has an ascender that rises above minim
height and a descender that drops below the baseline. It has a crossbar
or horizontal stroke through the middle. This is the key difference from
long-s (which lacks the full crossbar). The scribe forms 'f' with a
fairly straight vertical stroke and a clear crossbar.

Example words:
  - "for" (appears in headings): "for the pyles" (line 7), "for the
    flixe" (line 11) -- the 'f' is clearly formed with its crossbar
  - "of" (appears frequently): lines 3, 4, 8, etc. -- the 'f' is
    consistent
  - "handfull", line 3 and line 8: the 'f' in the middle is clear
  - "flixe", line 11: the initial 'f' shows both the descender and
    crossbar clearly

Confusion risks: PRIMARY confusion with long-s. In this hand, the 'f'
has a more definite crossbar that extends to both sides of the vertical
stroke, while long-s either lacks a crossbar entirely or has only a
slight tick to one side. This distinction is sometimes subtle.

Variants: One form. Capital F appears as "ff" (double-f) per convention,
visible in "ffor" headings -- actually in this manuscript the scribe
uses a decorated capital F rather than the ff convention in the headings
("For" in headings).


--- g ---

Letterform description: The 'g' has a rounded upper bowl at minim height
and a descender that drops below the baseline, curving back to the left
in a loop. This is a fairly recognizable form, not drastically different
from modern 'g' but with a more elaborate descender loop.

Example words:
  - "Boorrage", line 4: the 'g' is visible mid-word (if present)
  - "grinde", line 9 (approximate): initial 'g' clearly formed
  - "goinge" or similar words in the body text

Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'q' (which also has a descender),
but 'q' is nearly always followed by 'u' and its tail typically goes
right rather than left. In this hand, 'g' is fairly distinctive.

Variants: One primary form observed.


--- h ---

Letterform description: The 'h' has a tall ascender and then an arching
stroke that comes down to the right, ending at or near the baseline. In
this hand, the arch is moderately pronounced. The descending right stroke
sometimes curves slightly below the baseline but does not loop as
dramatically as in some secretary hands.

Example words:
  - "the" (frequent): every "the" on the page shows a clear 'h'
  - "handfull", line 3 and line 8: initial 'h' is tall and clear
  - "them", line 9: the 'h' precedes 'e' and 'm'
  - "horsedounge", line 12: initial 'h'

Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'b' (both have ascenders), but in
this hand 'b' closes into a bowl while 'h' has an open arch. Could also
be confused with 'k' if the arch is exaggerated, but 'k' has a more
angular or pointed junction.

Variants: One primary form. The ascender height is fairly consistent.


--- i ---

Letterform description: A simple minim (short vertical stroke) sitting on
the baseline. In this hand, 'i' usually has a small dot or tittle above
it, though not always clearly visible. It is the same height as other
minims (m, n, u).

Example words:
  - "in", line 9: simple and clear
  - "it", appears in body text: clear minim with dot
  - "violet", line 3: the 'i' after 'v' is visible

Confusion risks: VERY HIGH confusion risk with minim letters. In this
hand, the sequence of minims can make 'i', 'u', 'n', 'm' very hard to
distinguish. An 'i' is a single minim; 'u' is two connected minims; 'n'
is two minims with an arch; 'm' is three minims with arches. When the
tittles (dots) over 'i' are missing, it becomes very difficult. Context
is often needed.

Variants: Occasionally the 'i' may appear with a slightly longer stroke,
but generally consistent.


--- j ---

Letterform description: Not clearly distinguishable from 'i' in this hand.
In early modern usage, 'j' and 'i' are used interchangeably. No clear
example of a distinct 'j' form appears on this page.

Example words: No clear standalone examples found on this page.

Confusion risks: Identical to 'i' in most contexts.

Variants: N/A


--- k ---

Letterform description: The 'k' has an ascender and two angular strokes
branching from the middle of the stem -- an upper stroke going up-right
and a lower stroke going down-right, meeting at the stem. In this hand,
the 'k' is fairly clearly formed with distinct angular branches.

Example words:
  - "Take" (appears at the start of each recipe): the 'k' is very
    clearly formed in every instance -- line 3, line 8, line 12, line 16.
    This is the best letter to study 'k' from, as it appears in large,
    carefully written form at the start of recipes.

Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'h' if the upper and lower
branches blur together into a single arch. In this hand, 'k' is
distinguished by having two separate angular strokes rather than a
single smooth arch.

Variants: The 'k' in "Take" is consistently formed. It appears to be
one of the scribe's more stable letterforms.


--- l ---

Letterform description: The 'l' is a simple ascender -- a tall vertical
stroke rising above minim height. In this hand it is straight or very
slightly curved, with no pronounced loop at the top (unlike some secretary
hands where 'l' has a looped ascender).

Example words:
  - "leaues", lines 3-4: the initial 'l' is clear
  - "laye", line 10 (approximate): clear initial 'l'
  - "handfull", line 3 and line 8: the double 'l' at the end

Confusion risks: Could be confused with the ascender of 'h', 'b', or 'k'
if the rest of the letter is unclear. In this hand, 'l' is notably
simpler than these -- just a straight upstroke with no arch, bowl, or
branches.

Variants: One form, consistent throughout.


--- m ---

Letterform description: Three minims connected by arches at the top. In
this hand, the 'm' is formed as a continuous series of three up-down
strokes joined at the top. It sits at minim height on the baseline.

Example words:
  - "My", line 1: the initial 'M' (capitalized, so larger and more
    elaborate)
  - "them", body text: the final 'm' shows three clear minims
  - "morninge", line 10 (approximate): initial 'm' is clear
  - "made", line 12: initial 'm'

Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'in', 'ni', 'ui', or 'iu' when
minims are not clearly arched. This is a universal problem in secretary
hand. In this hand, the arches connecting the minims of 'm' are usually
visible but can be subtle.

Variants: One form. Capital M (as in "My lord") is much larger and more
ornate, with broad sweeping strokes.


--- n ---

Letterform description: Two minims connected by an arch at the top. Very
similar to 'm' but with only two strokes. Sits at minim height.

Example words:
  - "and" (frequent): the 'n' between 'a' and 'd' is clear
  - "one", line 8: the 'n' is mid-word
  - "new", line 12: initial 'n' is visible
  - "in" (frequent): the 'n' after 'i' shows the two-minim form

Confusion risks: Same minim confusion as 'm' and 'i'. Two connected
minims could be read as 'n', 'u', or 'ii'. In this hand, 'n' has the
arch at the top connecting the two minims, while 'u' connects at the
bottom.

Variants: One form, consistent.


--- o ---

Letterform description: A round or slightly oval closed loop sitting at
minim height. In this hand, 'o' is fairly round and well-closed, without
the angular top that 'a' sometimes has.

Example words:
  - "of" (frequent): the 'o' is clear and round
  - "one", line 8: initial 'o' clearly formed
  - "Consumpshone", line 2: the 'o' is visible
  - "Colewort", line 9: the 'o' after 'C' is round and clear

Confusion risks: Can be confused with secretary 'e' (the reversed-loop
form). In this hand, 'o' tends to be rounder and more fully closed than
'e', which is a smaller reversed loop. This is one of the trickiest
distinctions in this hand and likely the most frequent source of misreading.

Variants: One form, generally consistent. Sometimes slightly more oval
than round.


--- p ---

Letterform description: The 'p' has a vertical stroke that descends below
the baseline (descender) and a bowl or rounded stroke on the right side
at minim height. The descender is fairly straight.

Example words:
  - "pyles", line 7: the initial 'p' in the heading is clearly formed
  - "put" (appears multiple times in body text): the 'p' is clear
  - "pyll" or similar, if present

Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'q' (both have descenders), but
'p' has its bowl on the right while 'q' has its bowl on the left.
Watch for the "special p" abbreviation forms (per-, pre-, pro-, par-)
where the descender is modified.

Variants: One primary form. The scribe may also use the "special p" for
abbreviations, but no clear example of the special form was identified on
this page with certainty.


--- q ---

Letterform description: Similar to 'p' but with the bowl on the left
side and the descender curving to the right. In this hand, 'q' is almost
always followed by 'u'.

Example words:
  - "quantitye", line 5: the 'q' is visible at the start, followed by 'u'

Confusion risks: Could be confused with 'p', but the bowl orientation and
the nearly universal 'qu' pairing help distinguish it.

Variants: One form observed.


--- r ---

Letterform description: The scribe uses at least two forms of 'r'. The
PRIMARY form is a secretary 'r' that looks somewhat like a modern 'v' or
a '2'-shaped mark -- it has a short upward stroke followed by a curved
downward stroke. The SECONDARY form, appearing occasionally, is closer to
a modern 'r' with a short vertical stroke and a rightward shoulder.

Example words:
  - "Brothe", line 2: the 'r' after 'B' shows the secretary form
  - "for" (in headings): the 'r' at the end shows the secretary form
  - "grinde", body text: the 'r' after 'g' is visible
  - "rested", body text line 9-10: initial 'r'

Confusion risks: The secretary 'r' (v-shaped or 2-shaped) can be confused
with 'v' or 'u'. In this hand, 'r' tends to have a sharper angular top
and a shorter overall form than 'v'. The 'r' can also be confused with 'c'
as noted in the paleography guide. Careful attention to context and
adjacent letters is needed.

Variants: Two forms as described above. The secretary form is dominant.
The more modern form appears occasionally, especially in certain positions
within words.


--- s ---

Letterform description: TWO distinct forms are used:

LONG-S: Used at the beginning and middle of words. Looks like a tall
stroke descending below the baseline (similar to 'f' but WITHOUT a full
crossbar or with only a slight tick on one side). The ascender rises
above minim height.

ROUND-S: Used at the END of words. Looks like a modern lowercase 's' --
a small sinuous curve sitting at minim height.

Example words:
  - "Strawberrie", line 4: the initial long-s is clear (tall vertical
    stroke)
  - "leaues", lines 3-4: the final round-s is visible
  - "pyles", line 7: the final round-s in the heading
  - "roses" or similar: long-s in the middle, round-s at the end

Confusion risks: Long-s is the SECOND HIGHEST confusion risk in this hand
(after e/o confusion). It can be confused with 'f' -- the key distinction
is that 'f' has a crossbar extending to both sides, while long-s either
lacks a crossbar or has only a faint tick on one side. In this hand, the
distinction is moderately clear but requires attention. Round-s poses no
particular confusion risk.

Variants: Two forms as described (long-s and round-s), used in their
conventional positions.


--- t ---

Letterform description: The 't' is slightly taller than minim height but
shorter than full ascender letters (b, d, h, k, l). It consists of a
vertical stroke with a crossbar. In this hand, the crossbar is usually
a short horizontal stroke crossing the vertical. The letter is fairly
simple and close to modern form.

Example words:
  - "the" (frequent): the initial 't' is clear in every instance
  - "Take" (recipe openers): the initial 'T' is capitalized and large
  - "together", line 4-5: the 't' is clearly formed
  - "them" (frequent): the initial 't' is clear

Confusion risks: Can be confused with 'c' when the crossbar is faint or
when the vertical stroke is short. In this hand, 't' tends to be slightly
taller than 'c' and the crossbar, though sometimes faint, is usually
visible.

Variants: One primary minuscule form. The capital T in "Take" is large and
elaborate, with a broad horizontal stroke and a sweeping vertical.


--- u ---

Letterform description: Two minims connected at the BOTTOM by a curve --
this is the opposite of 'n', which connects at the top. Sits at minim
height. In early modern usage, 'u' and 'v' are often interchangeable,
with 'v' typically at word-start and 'u' in word-interior.

Example words:
  - "put" (body text): the 'u' between 'p' and 't' is clear
  - "vntill" or "untill" (body text line 9): if 'u' appears mid-word
  - "leaues" (lines 3-4): the 'u' before 'e' is visible

Confusion risks: Very high confusion with 'n' (arch at top vs. curve at
bottom), and with minim sequences generally. Also potentially confused
with 'v' at word-start (though by convention, 'v' appears word-initially
and 'u' appears mid-word in this period).

Variants: One form. See 'v' entry for the word-initial variant.


--- v ---

Letterform description: In this hand, 'v' at word-start looks like a
modern 'v' or possibly like the secretary 'r'. It has two strokes meeting
at a point at the baseline, with the strokes going up-left and up-right.

Example words:
  - "violet", line 3: initial 'v' is clearly formed
  - "vpon" or "vppon", line 14 (approximate): initial 'v'
  - "vse", line 10 (approximate): initial 'v' if present

Confusion risks: Can be confused with secretary 'r' (both are angular).
In this hand, 'v' tends to be wider and more symmetrical than the
secretary 'r'. When appearing word-initially before a vowel, it is almost
certainly 'v' by convention.

Variants: Used primarily in word-initial position. Mid-word, 'u' is used
instead (standard early modern practice).


--- w ---

Letterform description: The 'w' looks like two 'v' shapes joined together,
or sometimes like a 'uu' ligature. In this hand, it is formed with a
series of angular strokes creating a wide letter at minim height. It is
one of the wider letters in this hand.

Example words:
  - "wrappe", line 8 (approximate): initial 'w' if present
  - "well", body text line 9: initial 'w' is visible
  - "with" or "withe" (appears in body text): initial 'w' is clear
  - "water" (if present in body text)

Confusion risks: Generally distinctive due to its width. Could potentially
be confused with 'vv' but context usually resolves this. In this hand,
'w' is fairly clear.

Variants: One form observed.


--- x ---

Letterform description: Two diagonal strokes crossing in the middle,
similar to modern 'x'. In this hand, the letter is small and compact.

Example words:
  - "flixe", line 11: the 'x' is visible near the end of the word

Confusion risks: Fairly distinctive in this hand. No major confusion risk.

Variants: One form observed. Limited examples on this page.


--- y ---

Letterform description: The 'y' has two strokes meeting above the baseline
and a descender dropping below. In this hand, it looks somewhat like a
modern 'y' but the descender often curves to the left. Be aware that what
looks like 'y' at word-start may actually be a thorn (representing 'th').

Example words:
  - "pyles", line 7: the 'y' is clearly formed
  - "you", body text line 14 (approximate): the 'y' if present
  - "yee" (line 9, approximate): if present, showing initial 'y'
  - "quantitye", line 5: the final 'y' before 'e'

Confusion risks: Can be confused with THORN (which also looks like 'y'
but represents 'th'). In this period, "ye" could mean "the" if the 'y'
is actually a thorn. In this hand, it is difficult to distinguish thorn
from 'y' by form alone -- context is needed. Also, the descender of 'y'
could be confused with 'g' or 'p' descenders in cramped writing.

Variants: One primary form. Watch for thorn vs. genuine 'y'.


--- z ---

Letterform description: No clear example of 'z' was identified on this
page. The letter is rare in early modern English recipe texts.

Example words: None found.

Confusion risks: N/A

Variants: N/A


COMMON COMBINATIONS AND LIGATURES
===================================

--- th ---

Description: Very common combination. The scribe writes 't' followed
closely by 'h', often nearly joined. The 't' crossbar sometimes extends
into the 'h' arch, creating a near-ligature. Also watch for THORN --
a single letter that looks like 'y' but represents 'th'.

Example words:
  - "the" (appears many times throughout the page): very clear in
    headings ("for the pyles", "for the flixe")
  - "them", "then", "there" in body text
  - "Brothe", line 2: final 'th' before 'e'

Notes: In this hand, the 'th' combination is one of the most reliably
readable digraphs. The thorn form, if used, would appear as 'y' but
should be expanded to 'th' in transcription per Folger conventions.


--- sh ---

Description: The long-s followed by 'h'. Because long-s has a vertical
stroke similar to 'h', this combination can look like a doubled tall
letter.

Example words:
  - "Consumpshone", line 2: the 'sh' in the middle
  - "sheepe" or "sheese" (line 17, approximate): initial 'sh' if present

Notes: The key to reading 'sh' is noting that the first tall stroke
(long-s) lacks the arch that 'h' has on its right side.


--- ch ---

Description: A small 'c' curve followed by the taller 'h'. In this hand,
the 'c' is clearly shorter than the 'h'.

Example words:
  - "blacke", line 16: no 'ch' here, but...
  - "whiche", line 16 (approximate): 'ch' in the middle

Notes: Fairly readable in this hand due to the height difference between
'c' and 'h'.


--- ff ---

Description: Double-f used as a capital F in some secretary-hand
conventions. In this hand, however, the scribe appears to use an ornate
single capital F in headings rather than the 'ff' convention.

Example words:
  - "ffor" would be the expected form, but this scribe writes "For" with
    a decorated capital F in the headings (lines 7, 11, 15)
  - "handfull": the single 'f' mid-word, followed by 'u', not a double-f

Notes: The absence of the ff convention for capital F is a DISTINCTIVE
FEATURE of this hand. The scribe uses elaborate capital F instead.


--- -es graph ---

Description: A looped downstroke at the end of words representing '-es'.
In this hand, it appears as a final flourish after the last letter.

Example words:
  - "leaues" (lines 3-4): the ending may use the -es graph
  - "pyles" (line 7): the ending

Notes: Hard to distinguish from a spelled-out '-es' in this hand. Examine
whether the ending shows two distinct letters (e then s) or a single
looped stroke.


--- -er graph ---

Description: A hook-shaped upstroke representing '-er' (or sometimes -ar,
-or, -re). In this hand, it appears as a small curved mark above or at
the end of a word.

Example words:
  - Possible in "Cauntberries" (line 1): the '-er' in '-berries'
  - "together" (line 4-5): the '-er' at the end

Notes: When present, this graph needs to be expanded to the appropriate
vowel + r combination based on the scribe's own spelling elsewhere.


DISTINCTIVE FEATURES OF THIS HAND
====================================

1. CLEAR RECIPE STRUCTURE MARKERS: The scribe uses a consistent visual
   hierarchy -- recipe titles are centered and slightly larger, "Take"
   begins each recipe in a distinctive larger hand, and decorative dots
   or points separate sections. This formatting is helpful for navigation
   but does not affect letterform reading.

2. DECORATED CAPITAL T IN "TAKE": Every recipe begins with an ornate
   capital T that is much larger than surrounding text, with elaborate
   serifs and a broad horizontal crossbar. This is the most distinctive
   visual feature of the page.

3. CAPITAL F RATHER THAN FF: As noted above, the scribe uses a decorated
   single capital F rather than the secretary convention of 'ff' for
   capital F. This is notable and distinguishes this hand from many
   contemporary manuscripts.

4. CONSISTENT SECRETARY D: The backward-looping 'd' is used consistently
   throughout with no instances of the modern 'd' form. This is one of
   the most reliably identifiable letters in this hand.

5. MODERATE SECRETARY E: The secretary 'e' is used but is not as
   extremely reversed as in some hands. It is relatively readable here.

6. CLEAR LONG-S vs. F DISTINCTION: The scribe maintains a moderately
   clear distinction between long-s and 'f', with the crossbar on 'f'
   being more visible than in some hands. This reduces (but does not
   eliminate) the confusion between these two letters.

7. DECORATIVE SECTION DIVIDERS: The scribe uses rows of dots (periods)
   to frame recipe titles, creating visual separation between recipes.
   These are decorative, not textual.

8. "MY LORD OF CANTERBURY'S" HEADING: The page appears to be headed with
   an attribution -- recipes belonging to or associated with "My lord of
   Canterbury." The heading uses larger, more formal letterforms than the
   body text.


HIGH-RISK CONFUSIONS IN THIS HAND
====================================

Listed in order of risk, from most to least likely to cause misreadings:

1. **e / o** -- HIGHEST RISK. The secretary 'e' (reversed loop) and 'o'
   (round closed loop) are very similar in this hand. The 'e' tends to
   be slightly smaller and more angular, but in fast-written body text
   the distinction can be minimal. This will be the most frequent source
   of error.

2. **Minim letters (i, m, n, u)** -- HIGH RISK. Any sequence of minims
   is potentially ambiguous. The word "minim" itself would be nearly
   impossible to read in secretary hand. In this hand, the arches on 'm'
   and 'n' and the tittles on 'i' help somewhat, but fast-written
   passages will still cause problems.

3. **Long-s / f** -- MODERATE-HIGH RISK. Despite the scribe maintaining
   a moderately clear distinction, these two letters are inherently
   similar and require careful attention to the crossbar.

4. **c / r / t** -- MODERATE RISK. These three letters can resemble each
   other in secretary hand. In this hand, the distinctions are clearer
   than in some, but still require attention -- especially 'c' vs. 'r'.

5. **y / thorn** -- MODERATE RISK. Both look like 'y' but represent
   different things. Context is the main disambiguator.

6. **v / r** (secretary form) -- MODERATE RISK. Both are angular marks
   at minim height. Position in word and context help: 'v' is typically
   word-initial; secretary 'r' appears in all positions.

7. **a / o** -- LOW-MODERATE RISK. When the top of 'a' is left open, it
   can resemble 'o'. In this hand, 'a' usually has a more angular top
   closure.

8. **b / h** -- LOW RISK. Both have ascenders, but in this hand 'b' has
   a clear closed bowl while 'h' has an open arch. The distinction is
   generally reliable here.

NOTE ON METHODOLOGY
=====================

This alphabet was constructed by examining individual letterforms bottom-up
-- identifying clear pen strokes and comparing them across multiple
instances of the same letter in different words. Where a letter appears
in only one or two words on the page, the description is based on limited
evidence and should be confirmed against additional pages of this
manuscript. The most reliable descriptions are for letters that appear
frequently (e, t, h, a, o, n, s, r, l) because they could be confirmed
across many instances.
